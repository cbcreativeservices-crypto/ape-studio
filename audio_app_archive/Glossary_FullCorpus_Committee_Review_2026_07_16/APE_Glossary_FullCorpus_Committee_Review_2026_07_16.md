## ⚠️ High-priority items (factual errors / self-contradictions)

### Aux-fed subwoofer — AUDI201 / Loudspeaker System Deployment (intermediate) · ⚠ safety topic
- **[Audio Technical · `plain_english`]** The plain_english directly contradicts the (correct) definition. web-verified via ProSoundWeb ('A Detailed Explanation Of The Aux-Fed Subwoofer Technique') and Fulcrum Acoustic ('The Aux-Fed Subwoofer Technique'): an aux-fed subwoofer is a LIVE-SOUND technique where the subs are driven from a console aux send fed only by selected low-frequency channels (kick, bass, etc.), exactly as the definition states. But the plain_english instead describes a home-theater AV receiver's LFE/sub output doing crossover filtering ('the receiver does the filtering... can line the sub up with the main speakers'). That is home-theater bass management, a different concept, and is wrong for this term.
  → *Fix:* Rewrite to match the definition: e.g. 'Instead of letting the subs get the whole mix, the engineer uses a dedicated aux send to route only the low-frequency channels (kick, bass, floor toms, synth bass) to the subs. Because only chosen sources reach the subs, the low end stays tight and uncluttered, and unwanted low-frequency energy from vocals, guitars, and cymbals is kept out of the subs.' Remove all AV-receiver/home-theater framing.
- **[Audio Technical · `purpose_function`]** web-verified (ProSoundWeb, Fulcrum Acoustic): purpose_function describes home-theater bass management ('Enables integrated bass management with receiver, allows receiver-controlled crossover and phase adjustment'), which is not what an aux-fed subwoofer is. The purpose of aux-fed subs is source-selective LF control at the mixing console in live sound.
  → *Fix:* Replace with the live-sound purpose: 'Gives the engineer source-by-source control over what reaches the subwoofers, keeping low-frequency muddiness out of the mains, reducing the number of sources contributing sub energy, and allowing sub level to be managed independently of the main L/R mix.'
- **[Audio Technical · `practical_application`]** web-verified (ProSoundWeb, Fulcrum Acoustic): practical_application is entirely home-theater ('Home theater systems typically use aux-fed subwoofers for integrated bass management. Receivers with dedicated sub outputs...'). Aux-fed subwoofing is a live-sound/FOH console technique, not a home-theater/AVR practice; this field is factually wrong.
  → *Fix:* Replace with live-sound practice: 'At the console the engineer creates a dedicated aux (often post-fader) as the sub feed, dials only low-frequency channels into that aux, and drives the subs from it; care is needed because the sub aux master stays up when the L/R master is pulled down, so the subs must be muted/managed separately.'
- **[Learning/Cognition · `plain_english`]** The definition correctly frames aux-fed subwoofer as a LIVE-SOUND console technique (subs driven from a dedicated post-fader aux send fed only by selected LF sources like kick, bass, toms). But plain_english explains a completely different concept: home-theater AVR bass management ('the receiver's dedicated subwoofer output', 'the receiver does the filtering'). A learner reads one thing in the definition and a contradictory thing in the plain explanation and will conflate two distinct topics.
  → *Fix:* Rewrite plain_english to match the definition's live-sound sense, e.g.: 'Instead of letting the subs grab lows from the full main mix, you send the subs their own aux mix that includes only the channels you choose - typically kick, bass, floor toms, and low synths. Vocals and cymbals never reach the subs, so the low end stays tight and you control sub content channel by channel.'
- **[Language/Communications · `plain_english`]** plain_english (and purpose_function/practical_application) describe a home-theater AV receiver's dedicated sub output and receiver-based bass management, but the definition defines the term as a live-sound technique of driving subs from a console aux send fed by selected LF channels. The fields contradict the definition, so a reader cannot tell what 'aux-fed subwoofer' actually means.
  → *Fix:* Rewrite plain_english (and align purpose_function/practical_application) to the definition's live-sound sense, e.g.: 'Instead of letting the subs take the full main mix, you feed them from a dedicated aux send and choose exactly which low-frequency channels go to it - typically kick, bass, toms, and synths. This gives the engineer channel-by-channel control over what reaches the subs and keeps other sources out of them.'

### Bottom-up troubleshooting — AUDI201 / Troubleshooting (intermediate)
- **[Audio Technical · `definition`]** Internal self-contradiction that would mislead a student. The definition and plain_english state bottom-up 'starts at the source (mic/input) and works forward toward the output,' but purpose_function and practical_application state it starts at 'the lowest level / physical/electrical layer / output' and works UP toward the source (e.g. 'confirm the amplifier and speakers work first, then move back through processing, console, and source'). These are opposite directions. Web-verified via howtonetwork.com and Cisco Press (ciscopress.com) troubleshooting-methodology references: bottom-up begins at the bottom/physical layer (cables, connectors, output/foundation end) and works UP toward the application/source — matching this entry's purpose_function and practical_application. So the definition and plain_english are the incorrect/contradictory fields.
  → *Fix:* Rewrite definition and plain_english to match the standard (and the entry's own purpose_function/practical_application): bottom-up troubleshooting starts at the output/lowest (physical/electrical) end of the chain — e.g. amp and speakers — and works back toward the source, verifying each foundational stage before higher-level causes. Remove the 'starting at the source and working toward the output' wording, which describes the opposite (top-down/source-first) direction.
- **[Learning/Cognition · `plain_english`]** The entry contradicts itself on which end is 'bottom', a direction that is the whole point of the method. plain_english defines bottom-up as starting 'at the source, such as the microphone or input, and works forward through the chain toward the output.' But scenario 1 ('first proving amps and speakers, then working back to the console') and scenario 2 ('from the physical layer (cabling) upward to application settings') both start at the OUTPUT/physical end and work back toward the source. A student who learns the stated direction and then reads the scenarios will be confused about how to actually run the method.
  → *Fix:* Make the definition and scenarios agree. The scenarios follow the common convention (bottom = the output/physical end), so revise plain_english to: 'Bottom-up troubleshooting starts at the output/physical end of the system (e.g., the speakers and amps, or the cabling) and works back toward the source and higher-level settings' — or, if the source-first direction is intended, replace scenario 1 with one that begins at the mic/input and moves toward the speakers. Pick one direction and use it consistently across definition, plain_english, and scenarios.
- **[Language/Communications · `purpose_function`]** Internal self-contradiction about the direction of the method. The definition and plain_english say bottom-up starts at the SOURCE/input and works forward toward the output; but purpose_function (and practical_application's PA example) say it starts at the OUTPUT/lowest physical layer and works UPWARD toward the source. A student cannot tell which way the method actually runs.
  → *Fix:* Reconcile the four fields to a single, consistent direction. As written, purpose_function ('starts at the lowest level... the output... and works upward toward the source') and practical_application (amp/speakers first, then back to source) describe output-to-source, while the definition and plain_english describe source-to-output. Rewrite the definition and plain_english to match the output-to-source direction used in purpose_function and practical_application (e.g., definition: 'Starting diagnosis at the output/lowest level of a signal path and working back toward the source, useful when the output or physical layer is suspect.'), or flip the other two fields instead — but all four must agree.

### Top-down troubleshooting — AUDI201 / Troubleshooting (intermediate)
- **[Audio Technical · `definition`]** Internal self-contradiction on the direction of the method that would mislead a student. The definition and plain_english state top-down 'start[s] at the system or output level and work[s] back toward the source' (output -> source), but purpose_function, practical_application, and scenario_contexts state the opposite: it 'starts at the highest level - the source... and works downward toward the output' / 'first verifies the source and high-level configuration, then proceeds stage by stage toward the output' (source -> output). The two halves of the entry describe opposite directions. (Web-checked: the term is used inconsistently across the field - e.g. Shure's troubleshooting guidance frames tracing from the mic/source end toward the amp as one valid approach - so the entry cannot rely on an external convention to resolve the conflict; it must be self-consistent.)
  → *Fix:* Pick one direction and make all five fields agree. Recommend aligning to the OSI-style framing the entry itself invokes in purpose_function ('highest/application/system level down to physical/output'): revise definition and plain_english to say top-down starts at the highest/system (source/configuration) level and works down toward the output, reserving the output->source direction for the paired 'bottom-up' entry. Ensure definition, plain_english, purpose_function, practical_application, and scenario_contexts all describe the same start point and direction.
- **[Learning/Cognition · `purpose_function`]** Internal self-contradiction on the direction of the method. definition and plain_english say top-down STARTS at the output/overall system and works BACK toward the source ('starts at the end you can observe, usually the output... works backward toward the source'). But purpose_function and practical_application say the opposite: it 'starts at the highest level — the source... and works downward toward the output' / 'first verifies the source... then proceeds toward the output'. A learner cannot tell which end 'top' is, which defeats the entire point of naming the strategy and will cause them to run it backwards.
  → *Fix:* Make the direction consistent across all four fields. Pick one convention and state it everywhere. Recommend aligning purpose_function/practical_application to the definition (start at the observed output/system level and work back toward the source), or, if the OSI-style 'application layer = top' sense is intended, rewrite definition and plain_english to match — but not both directions in the same entry.
- **[Language/Communications · `purpose_function`]** The fields contradict each other on which direction 'top-down' runs. The definition and plain_english say it STARTS at the output/system end and works BACK toward the source. But purpose_function says it 'starts at the highest level — the source ... and works downward toward the output', and scenario_contexts SC1 likewise starts 'at the playback source and working toward the speakers'. A student cannot tell whether top-down begins at the output or at the source.
  → *Fix:* Make the described direction consistent across definition, plain_english, purpose_function, and the scenarios (audio-technical reviewer to confirm the intended direction). To match the authoritative definition, reword purpose_function to: 'Top-down troubleshooting starts at the system/output end — where the symptom is observed — and works back toward the source, checking each stage in turn. It exists as a systematic method that begins at the observable result before drilling toward the origin.' Then align SC1 to start at the output rather than the playback source.

### Fader — MUSI190 / Mixers & Recorders (beginner)
- **[Audio Technical · `plain_english`]** The plain_english claims a fader "is called 'linear' because the slider's position maps directly to the level." That is factually wrong and self-contradicts this entry's own definition ("electrical taper follows an audio/logarithmic 'fader law' calibrated in dB") and its common_mistakes ("assuming the fader's taper is linear in dB, so small moves near the bottom do far more than expected"). Web-verified (SoundOnSound 'Are all faders created equal?'; sound-education/lenardaudio): a mixing-console fader is called 'linear' for its linear (straight-line) physical TRAVEL, as opposed to a rotary pot; its taper is logarithmic/audio (fader law), so position does NOT map directly/proportionally to level. A linear-TAPER fader (position 1:1 to signal) is precisely what a console channel fader is not.
  → *Fix:* Replace the sentence with: "It's called 'linear' because the control slides in a straight line (unlike a rotary knob) - not because level tracks position evenly. In fact a fader's travel follows a logarithmic 'fader law' (calibrated in dB), so a given amount of slider movement changes the level by a fairly consistent number of decibels rather than a fixed fraction of the signal."
