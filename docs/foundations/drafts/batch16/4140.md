Topic Foundations: Deliverables, Stems and Mastering for Picture

Imagine watching a new film in a cinema. Later, you watch it again on a plane, and then at home. A friend in another country hears the actors speak her own language. The pictures are the same each time, but the sound is not. So how does one finished mix become every version the film needs?

When the final mix, covered in its own topic, is complete, it is recorded as one finished soundtrack. The version for cinemas is called the theatrical printmaster. A written list from the company that receives the film sets the loudness limits, formats and files it must get. The files on that list are the film's deliverables.

But in one finished soundtrack, everything is blended together. Nobody can change the music without changing the voices. So the mix is also saved in separate groups, each in its own file. The main groups are dialogue, music and effects. The file with only the speech is called a dialogue stem, and the music has a music stem. Played together, these stems rebuild the full mix.

Separate stems make other languages possible. Without the dialogue, what remains is the music-and-effects track, and another country records new voices over it. But footsteps and small noises recorded on set were often on the voice microphones, so they disappear too. Editors add new sounds into those gaps until nothing is missing. Singing, the talking of crowds and actors' breaths belong to one language, so they often go on an optional track. The team that records the new language decides whether to keep each one.

The mix must also suit each place where people watch. A cinema is a large, quiet room, so its mix can go from very soft to very loud. Homes are noisier and have smaller speakers. For home viewing, the voices are made clearer and the loud and quiet parts are brought closer together. Television stations have their own loudness rules. A mix for planes removes offensive language and makes speech easier to hear over the noise of the aircraft.

Every version must also stay in time with the picture. A short beep is placed at an agreed point before the picture starts, on the picture and on every sound file. This beep is called a sync pop. If the beeps line up, the files match. A second beep after the last frame shows that the sound stayed in time to the end. Putting the finished mix back onto the final video file is called layback. Then someone checks the loudness, the timing, and that each channel plays from the correct speaker.

The flashcards explain each part, from the many kinds of stems to the way sound was once printed onto film. By the end, you will know how one finished mix becomes a complete set of files, and how that set is checked before delivery.
