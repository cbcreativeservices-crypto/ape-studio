Topic Foundations: ADR and Looping

Imagine a film scene on a busy street. On the day of filming, the microphone recorded the traffic more clearly than the actors. Weeks later, an actor goes into a quiet studio, watches the scene on a screen, and speaks the lines again. This is called automatic dialogue replacement. Its older name is looping, because the scene played again and again while the actor made new attempts. But the new line comes from a different room and a different day. How is it made to fit the scene?

The work starts before the actor arrives. Editors list every line in the edited film that must be recorded again, with its time and the reason. Each line gets a number on one shared list, and a supervisor plans the session from it.

In the studio, the actor wears headphones. A short part of the scene plays first, with the original recording as a guide. Usually the actor hears three short beeps and starts speaking where a fourth beep would come. That signal is called the three-beep cue. Other signals are lines that move across the screen. If one word goes wrong, recording starts again just before the mistake.

Starting on time is only the first part of sync, short for synchronization, the match between sound and picture. The rest depends on what the audience can see. When the mouth is clearly visible, every word must match the lips. That is called a hard-sync line. When the mouth is hidden, the timing can be looser. Often the actor records only one part of a line, and the editor joins it to the original.

A line in perfect sync can still sound wrong. If the character was running, the actor must breathe hard in the new recording too. Its pitch must rise and fall on the same words, with the same rhythm. Over a long session, a voice often becomes tired, so later takes stop matching. The room matters too. A studio has almost no echo, and a close microphone adds extra low frequencies. Editors join the best parts of several takes and add room sound until the new recording matches the original.

Not every voice in a scene belongs to a main actor. On set, people in the background usually only pretend to talk, so the crowd is added later. A team of voice actors hired for this is called a loop group. They record the quiet sound of many people talking, with no single word clear. That sound is called walla.

The flashcards explain each part in detail: planning a session, the signals in the studio, the levels of sync, matching a performance and its sound, and building crowd voices. By the end, you will know how a line recorded weeks later is made to sound as if it was spoken in the scene.
