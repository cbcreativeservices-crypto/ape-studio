Topic Foundations: ADR and Looping

Imagine singing along with a song, matching every word. Actors sometimes do something similar. In one scene beside a loud river, the microphone recorded the water more clearly than the actors. Weeks later, an actor watches the scene in a quiet studio and speaks the lines again. This is called automatic dialogue replacement, or ADR. Its older name is looping, because the scene played again and again for each new attempt. But the new line comes from a different room and day. How is it made to fit the scene?

The work starts before the actor arrives. Editors list every line that must be recorded again, with its time, its words and the reason. Each line on this list is called an ADR cue. Each has a number. A supervisor plans the studio session from this list.

In the studio, the actor wears headphones and records several attempts, called takes. A short part of the scene plays first, with the original recording as a guide. Usually the actor hears three short beeps and starts speaking where a fourth beep would come. If one word goes wrong, recording can start again just before the mistake.

Starting on time is only the first part of sync, short for synchronization, the match between sound and picture. The rest depends on what the audience can see. When the mouth is clearly visible, every word must match the lips. That is called a hard-sync line. When the mouth is hidden or far away, the timing can be looser. That is called a soft-sync line.

A line in perfect sync can still sound wrong. If the character was running, the actor must breathe hard too. The voice must rise and fall in pitch on the same words, with the same rhythm. Over a long session, a voice often becomes tired, so later takes can match less well.

The room matters too. A studio has almost no echo, and a close microphone often adds extra low frequencies. Editors join the best parts of several takes. They also add a little echo and the quiet background sound of the filmed place, until the new line matches the original.

Some voices were never recorded at all. On set, people in the background usually only pretend to talk, so their voices are added later to fit the scene. A team of voice actors hired to do this is called a loop group. They record the background sound of many people talking, usually with no single word clear. That sound is called walla. It can also include a few clear words that match the action.

The flashcards explain each step, from planning the session to recording the crowd, plus lines recorded with no picture and voice-over. By the end, you will know how a line recorded weeks later is made to sound as if it was spoken in the scene.
