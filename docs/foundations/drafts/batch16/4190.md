Topic Foundations: Sound Effects Editorial and Sound Design

Imagine a film scene. A man runs down a wet street at night. A car drives past, a door closes loudly, and rain falls. On the set, the microphone recorded mainly the actors' voices. Most other sounds were added later. So where do they come from, and how do they match the picture?

The work starts with a plan. The editors list every moment in the edited film that needs a sound. A few sounds come from the set. Many come from large collections of recordings, labeled with describing words so a search finds them quickly. Others are recorded for the film, in real places or in a quiet room. Finding or making these sounds and placing them with the picture is called effects editorial.

Placement matters most for a sound that belongs to one visible action, such as a door closing or a glass breaking. That is called a hard effect. It must start on the exact frame of the action, or the sound and the picture do not match. Editors also extend its start and end slightly, so it fades in and out. Glass, metal, wood and rubber each sound different when they hit something.

Between those sounds, a silent scene seems empty and false. Every real place has a quiet, steady sound, such as distant traffic, wind in trees, or air vents in a room. That sound under a scene is called background ambience. Editors keep long recordings of cities, forests, oceans and more. These recordings help the viewer know where the scene happens. They also fill gaps between edits, so the sound continues evenly.

Some things on screen have no real sound, such as a monster or a spaceship. Other real sounds are too quiet for film. So designers build the sound from several recordings: a sharp start, a main part, and the sound that continues after it. This is called sound layering. The parts must be placed exactly together, or the ear hears separate sounds.

Designers also change recordings. A small animal's sound, played slower, becomes lower and longer, so it can become a large creature. A sound can be stretched, reversed, or slowly turned into another sound. It can even be played through a loudspeaker in a real room and recorded again, so it gets that room's echo. That is called worldizing.

Other sounds connect one moment to the next. A fast sound of moving air can play over a cut. A sound rising in pitch makes the viewer expect something. A short, deep, loud sound marks an important moment, often in trailers, the short advertisements for a film.

The flashcards cover planning and finding sounds, placing effects, background sounds, building and changing sounds, and sounds between scenes. By the end, you will know where film sounds come from, and how each one is chosen, placed, built and changed.
