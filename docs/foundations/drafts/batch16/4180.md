Topic Foundations: Foley Performance and Recording

Imagine a film scene in which a man walks across a wooden floor and puts a cup on a table. You hear each step, his clothes moving and the cup touching the wood. Most of these sounds were not recorded during filming. The microphones on the set recorded the voices. So a person watched the scene later and performed the sounds in time with it. This craft is called Foley. How can one person perform another person's movements so well that the audience believes them?

The work happens in a quiet studio with little echo, called the Foley stage. Its floor has areas of wood, tile and concrete. A box set down into the floor, filled with gravel or sand, is called a Foley pit. Nearby rooms store shoes, clothes and objects. The artist performs while watching the film, and a recordist records each attempt. The scene is played many times, and each time one kind of sound is recorded. Each of these recordings is called a Foley pass. An editor can then fix one kind of sound without changing the others.

Often the first pass is the footsteps. The artist copies the actor's speed and weight, so each step sounds when the foot touches the ground on screen. This is called gait matching. The shoes must match the character's shoes, and the surface must match the ground. A man in boots on concrete sounds very different from a child in socks on carpet. A turn, a limp or a quiet careful step each sounds different again.

The next pass is usually the moves, the sounds of clothes and the body. The artist moves fabric that matches the costume near the microphone. A leather jacket creaks, and keys jingle in a pocket. Hands touching a table and bodies falling to the floor belong here too.

Another pass covers the objects that characters handle, such as cups, doors, keys and books, each with its own weight. Often the artist uses the real object. Sometimes a different object sounds more believable, and using it is called prop substitution. For example, coconut shells can produce the sound of horse hooves.

In every pass, the artist is acting. A good performer starts each movement slightly early, so the sound happens at the right moment. A sound can be louder than real life for a comedy scene, or so soft that the audience does not notice it. The microphone is usually close to the sound. Later, the person mixing the film makes each sound seem near or far.

The flashcards cover the stage and the team, the passes, footsteps on every shoe and surface, cloth and body movement, object sounds, and styles of performance. By the end, you will know how Foley sounds are planned, performed and recorded to match a scene.
