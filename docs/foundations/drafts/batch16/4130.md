Topic Foundations: Post Workflow, Spotting & Sessions

Imagine watching a film. An actor whispers in a windy field, and every word matches the lips. The sound you hear rarely came from the camera. A separate machine usually recorded it, and many people then edited and mixed it. So this topic answers one question: how does the sound travel from the set to the finished film, organized and matching the picture?

It starts with the microphones on set. A voice microphone works best near the mouth, but the camera must not see it. Usually a crew member holds a long pole with the microphone above the actors, just outside the picture. That is called a boom microphone. When the pole cannot reach, a tiny microphone is hidden in clothing or hair, with a small radio pack under the costume. In film work, actors are often named talent, so this is called talent wiring.

The microphones go to a small mixer and recorder that run on batteries. A second recorder often makes a backup copy. The crew also records the quiet background of the room, for later use.

Recording picture and sound on two separate machines is called double-system sound, so they must be joined later. Each machine marks every moment of its recording with a clock time in hours, minutes, seconds and frames (the single pictures that make up the film). That clock time is called time code. At the start of each take, one recorded attempt of a shot, someone also closes a hinged board, called a slate, with a loud clap. The clap gives editors one exact point to match.

A clock time only helps if people can find the take. So the sound person writes down every take, with its scene, time code and file name. Then the picture editor improves the edit, version by version. When the timing is final, this version of the film has reached picture lock. It then goes to the sound team with its edit lists.

Even so, the picture often changes a little after lock. A list of changes tells the sound team how to move its sounds to match. Then the sound team, the director and the composer watch the film together. They decide where each piece of music and each important sound starts and stops. That meeting is called a spotting session. Next, editors place dialogue, effects, backgrounds and music on separate tracks, in time with the picture.

The flashcards explain each step. They cover the equipment on set, time code and slates, the written lists of takes, the stages of the picture edit, and the planning of the sound. They also cover the rooms where each job happens, including the final mix room, built as a small cinema. By the end, you will know how a soundtrack travels from the set to the final mix, with every piece easy to find and matched to the picture.
