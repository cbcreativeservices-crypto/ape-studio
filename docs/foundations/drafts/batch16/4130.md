Topic Foundations: Post Workflow, Spotting and Sessions

A video filmed on a phone holds the picture and the sound together. On a film set, the camera usually records the picture and only a lower-quality guide sound. A separate machine records the main, high-quality sound. So this topic answers one question: how is the sound brought from the set into the finished film, organized and matched to the picture?

On set, a voice microphone works best near the mouth, but the camera must not see it. Usually a crew member holds a long pole with the microphone just above the edge of the camera's view. That is called a boom microphone. Another choice is a tiny microphone hidden in an actor's clothing, with a small radio pack. The microphones go to a small battery-powered mixer and recorder.

Because the camera and the recorder are separate machines, their recordings must be matched later. This way of working is called double-system sound. Each machine often marks every moment with a clock time in hours, minutes, seconds and frames (the single pictures of the film). This clock time is called time code.

Each recorded attempt of a shot is a take. At the start of most takes, someone closes a hinged board, the slate, with a loud clap. The clap gives editors one exact point to match. The sound person also lists every take in a written report and records the quiet sound of each room, so editors can fill gaps later. Each day, the new takes are matched to the picture for the director to watch.

The picture editor then puts the shots in order. When the editor and director agree the timing is finished, the film has reached picture lock. Around this time, the sound team, the director and the composer watch the film together. They decide where each piece of music and important sound starts and stops. That meeting is called a spotting session.

The sound team receives the locked film with a text file listing where each sound and picture clip starts and ends. Even so, the picture often changes a little after lock. A list of changes tells the sound team how to move its sounds to match. That work is called conform, and it can happen more than once.

Once the sounds match the picture, each part is prepared in a room built for that step, often in one building. Editors prepare dialogue, effects and music in small editing rooms. The final mix usually happens in a room set up as a small cinema, so the sound matches what audiences will hear. That room is called a re-recording stage.

The flashcards explain the equipment, sounds recorded without picture, the daily syncing of takes, the files and records, and the rooms at each step. By the end, you will know how the sound from the set reaches the final mix, organized and matched to the picture.
