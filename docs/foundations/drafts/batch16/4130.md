Topic Foundations: Post Workflow, Spotting and Sessions

A video filmed on a phone holds the picture and the sound together. On a film set, the camera usually records the picture and only a lower-quality guide sound. A separate machine records the main, high-quality sound. So this topic answers one question. How does the sound from the set reach the finished film, kept in order and matched to the picture?

That main sound starts at the microphones. A voice microphone works best near the mouth, but the camera must not see it. A crew member often holds it on a long pole, just above the edge of the camera's view. That is called a boom microphone. Another choice is a tiny microphone hidden in an actor's clothing, with a small radio pack. Both feed a small battery-powered mixer and recorder.

Because the camera and the recorder are separate machines, their recordings must be matched later. This way of working is called double-system sound. Each machine often marks every moment with a clock time in hours, minutes, seconds and frames (the single pictures of the film). This clock time is called time code.

Each recorded attempt of a shot is a take. At the start of most takes, someone closes a hinged board, the slate, with a loud clap. The clap gives editors one exact point to match. The sound person also lists every take in a written report and records the quiet sound of each room, so editors can fill gaps later. Each day, new takes are matched to the picture for the director.

The picture editor then puts the shots in order. When the editor and director agree the timing is final, the film reaches picture lock. Around this time, the director watches the film with the sound team and, often in a separate meeting, with the composer. They decide where each piece of music and important sound starts and stops. Each such meeting is called a spotting session.

The sound team receives the locked film with project files, including a list of where each sound and picture clip starts and ends. Still, the picture often changes a little after lock. A list of changes tells the sound team how to move its sounds to match. That work is called conform, and it can happen more than once.

While this matching goes on, editors prepare the dialogue, effects and music in small editing rooms, often in one building. The final mix usually happens in a room set up as a small cinema, so the mix sounds the way it will in theaters. That room is called a re-recording stage.

The flashcards explain each step, from the equipment on set to the rooms where the sound is finished. By the end, you will know how the sound from the set reaches the final mix, kept in order and matched to the picture.
