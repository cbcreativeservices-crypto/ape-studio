Topic Foundations: Dialogue Editing

Imagine a film scene in which two people talk at a kitchen table. Their voices sound clear, and the quiet sound of the room stays steady. But the scene was filmed in many separate shots, over several hours, while a refrigerator started and stopped and the microphones moved. So how do separate recordings become one natural conversation in which nobody hears the edits?

The work starts with collecting the material. The editor gathers the recorded attempt, or take, used in the edited film for every line and places it under the picture, in time with the actors' lips. This first step is called dialogue assembly. Then the clips are arranged on tracks. Each camera position, called a setup, has its own sound. So clips from one setup share a track, and a new setup goes on another track. This is called setup checkerboarding. Each setup can then be adjusted separately.

Each line often has several microphones to choose from. A microphone on a long pole above the actors usually sounds the most natural. A tiny microphone clipped under clothing sounds closer, but it can lose some high frequencies. Both can be used together, but the voice reaches them at slightly different times. This can cancel some frequencies and make the voice sound unnatural. That sound is called phasey speech. To prevent it, editors move one recording so both are exactly in time.

Even then, a cut can still be heard because of the background. Every place has a quiet sound of its own, even when nobody talks. This is called room tone. Shots recorded at different times often have different room tone, so the background can change suddenly at a cut. Editors collect quiet moments from before and after each take, and use them to fill the gaps. A steady background hides the cuts.

Sometimes the word itself is the problem. An actor makes a mistake, or a noise covers one syllable. The editor can take the same word, sound or breath from another take. Copying a clean breath into place is called a breath graft. The join is often hidden in a pause. When no take works, the actor records the line again in a studio, which is a separate topic.

The last problem is unwanted noise. Clothing rubs the clip-on microphone, mouths make small clicks, and radio interference adds crackling sounds. Special tools reduce these sounds. Too much cleaning, however, can weaken the "s" sounds and leave faint, short tones that change pitch quickly, called a musical-noise artifact. Useful sounds from the set, such as footsteps, are kept on their own tracks instead of being removed.

The flashcards explain each part in detail: gathering recordings, arranging tracks, combining microphones, keeping the background steady, repairing words, and removing noise without damaging the voice. By the end, you will know how dialogue from many separate shots becomes one natural conversation.
