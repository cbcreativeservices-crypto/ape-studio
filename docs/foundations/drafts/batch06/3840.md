Topic Foundations: Recording Fundamentals and Signal Chain

When you record a voice message on a phone, the microphone, electronics and storage are all in one device. A studio splits this path into separate pieces of equipment. Each stage can add noise or distortion, and a later stage usually cannot remove it. So how does sound travel to the file without gaining noise, and how does everyone hear it on the way?

The microphone turns a singer's voice into a weak electrical signal, so the first stage makes it stronger. That stage is called the microphone preamplifier, or mic preamp. An electric bass or keyboard often skips the microphone, reaching the preamp through a small box that changes its signal to suit the preamp input. Too little level leaves the signal close to the background noise of the equipment. Too much makes it distort. Engineers usually stay below each stage's maximum, leaving room for sudden loud moments.

Next, converters change the signal into numbers. In digital audio, sound above the maximum is cut off and distorts. Preamps and converters often share one box, called the audio interface. The computer processes these numbers in small groups. The size of each group is called the buffer size. A large group is stable but adds delay. A singer who hears their voice late finds it hard to stay in time. For this reason, many interfaces send the microphone straight to the headphones, skipping the computer. This is called direct monitoring.

The headphones also play the music the singer follows. This blend, separate from what the engineer hears, is called the cue mix. The engineer talks to the singer through these headphones, using a small control-room microphone.

This headphone mix matters because many songs are recorded one part at a time, each on its own track. Adding a new part while listening to the earlier ones is called overdubbing. Finished tracks can be locked, so nobody records over them by mistake.

On its way to each track, the signal can also pass through separate hardware processors. One cable often sends it out to the processor, and another brings it back. Recording the processed sound into the file is called printing. Printed processing usually cannot be removed later, so engineers often use only a little.

The engineer hears the recording through control-room speakers. A box, the monitor controller, chooses which source plays and how loud, without changing what is recorded. Many engineers keep the speakers at one fixed, measured loudness, and save corrected speaker settings so they can recall them later. This speaker feed can also be sent over the internet to a producer in another place.

The flashcards explain each stage, and how a song is recorded part by part. By the end, you will know how sound passes through each stage to the file, and how the singer and engineer hear it along the way.
