Topic Foundations: Recording Fundamentals and Signal Chain

When you record a voice message on a phone, the microphone, the electronics and the storage are all inside one device. A studio splits this path into separate pieces of equipment, so each one can be chosen and controlled. Each stage can add noise or distortion, and a later stage usually cannot remove it. So how does sound reach the file without gaining noise or distortion?

The microphone turns a singer's voice into a weak electrical signal, so the first stage makes it stronger. That stage is called the microphone preamplifier, or mic preamp. An electric bass or keyboard often skips the microphone and reaches the preamp through a small box that changes its signal so the preamp can accept it. Too little level leaves the signal close to the hiss. Too much makes it distort. Every stage has a maximum, so engineers stay well below it, leaving room for sudden loud moments.

Before the signal becomes digital, it can be changed in separate hardware processors. One cable sends the signal out, and a second brings it back. Processing used while recording cannot be removed later, so engineers often use only a little. During mixing, groups of tracks called stems may pass through hardware again. They may also be added together in hardware instead of in the computer. Mixing partly in hardware like this is called hybrid mixing.

Next, converters change the signal into numbers. In digital audio, sound above the maximum is cut off and distorts. In smaller studios, the preamps and converters are usually built into one box, called the audio interface.

The computer processes these numbers in small groups. The size of each group is called the buffer size. A large group is stable but adds delay. A singer who hears their own voice late finds it hard to stay in time. So many interfaces send the microphone straight to the headphones, skipping the computer. This is called direct monitoring.

The headphones also play the music that the singer sings along to. This headphone blend, separate from what the engineer hears, is called the cue mix. It matters most when new parts are recorded over earlier ones, or when the singer records the same part again on a second track. The engineer also speaks to the singer through these headphones, using a small control-room microphone.

The engineer hears the recording through control-room speakers. A box, the monitor controller, chooses which source plays and how loud, without changing what is recorded. Many engineers keep the speakers at one fixed, measured loudness, so their choices stay consistent. These headphone and speaker feeds can also be sent over the internet to a producer in another place.

The flashcards explain each stage of this chain in detail. By the end, you will know how each stage passes the sound to the next, and why a good recording depends on every one.
