Topic Foundations: Recording Fundamentals and Signal Chain

Imagine a singer in a small recording room, wearing headphones, while an engineer works at a computer in the next room. The voice must become a saved file. It passes through several pieces of equipment in turn. Each stage can add noise or distortion, and a later stage usually cannot remove it. So how does sound travel from the microphone to the file, without gaining noise or distortion?

The microphone turns the voice into a weak electrical signal, so the first stage makes it stronger. That stage is called the microphone preamplifier, or mic preamp. Too little level leaves the voice only a little louder than the background hiss. Too much makes it distort. An electric bass or keyboard often uses no microphone. Its signal reaches this same stage through a small box that matches it to the preamp.

Before the signal becomes digital, some changes to the sound can be made in separate hardware processors. A cable takes the whole signal out of a channel, through the processor, and back in. Used during recording, a processor's effect becomes part of the file and cannot be removed, so engineers often apply only a small amount.

Every stage has a maximum level, and the signal stays well below it to leave extra level for sudden loud moments. Next, converters change the signal into numbers. In digital audio, sound above the maximum is cut off at that level and distorted. The preamps and converters are usually built into one box between the microphones and the computer, called the audio interface.

The computer processes these numbers in small groups. The size of each group is called the buffer size. A large group keeps the computer stable but adds delay. A singer who hears their own voice late cannot easily sing together with the music. So many interfaces send the microphone signal directly to the headphones, before it reaches the computer. This is called direct monitoring.

The headphones also carry the music the singer records with. The blend in the headphones is separate from what the engineer hears, and it is called the cue mix. It matters most when someone records a new part while listening to earlier parts. That new part is called an overdub. Between attempts, the engineer speaks to the singer through the headphones, using a small microphone in the control room.

The engineer listens through speakers in the control room. A control box chooses which source plays and how loud, without changing what is recorded. Many engineers use the same loudness each time and check that each speaker plays the correct side.

The flashcards explain each part of this chain, from inputs and levels to processors, headphones, speakers and the main ways of recording. By the end, you will know how each stage passes the sound to the next, and why a good recording depends on every one.
