Topic Foundations: Recording Fundamentals and Signal Chain

When you record a voice message on a phone, the microphone, electronics and storage are all in one device. A studio splits this path into separate pieces of equipment. Each stage can add noise or distortion, and a later stage usually cannot remove it. So how does sound travel to the file without gaining noise, and how does everyone hear it on the way?

The microphone turns a singer's voice into a weak electrical signal, so the first stage makes it stronger. That stage is called the microphone preamplifier, or mic preamp. An electric bass or keyboard often skips the microphone, reaching the preamp through a small box that adapts its signal. Too little level leaves the signal close to the hiss. Too much makes it distort. Engineers stay well below each stage's maximum, leaving room for sudden loud moments.

The signal can also be changed in separate hardware processors. One cable often sends it from the interface to the processor, and another cable brings it back. Processing recorded with the signal usually cannot be removed later, so engineers often use only a little. During mixing, tracks can pass through hardware again. Mixing partly in hardware and partly in the computer is called hybrid mixing.

Next, converters change the signal into numbers. In digital audio, sound above the maximum is cut off and distorts. In smaller studios, preamps and converters often share one box, called the audio interface.

The computer processes these numbers in small groups. The size of each group is called the buffer size. A large group is stable but adds delay. A singer who hears their voice late finds it hard to stay in time. For this reason, many interfaces send the microphone straight to the headphones, skipping the computer. This is called direct monitoring.

The headphones also play the music the singer follows. This blend, separate from what the engineer hears, is called the cue mix. Many songs are recorded one part at a time, each on its own track. The cue mix matters most when a new part is added, or a part is recorded again on a second track. Finished tracks can be locked, so nobody records over them by mistake. The engineer talks to the singer through these headphones, using a small control-room microphone.

The engineer hears the recording through control-room speakers. A box, the monitor controller, chooses which source plays and how loud, without changing what is recorded. Many engineers keep the speakers at one fixed, measured loudness, so their choices stay consistent. This speaker feed can also be sent over the internet to a producer in another place.

The flashcards explain each stage, and how a song is recorded part by part. By the end, you will know how sound passes through each stage to the file, and how the singer and engineer hear it along the way.
