Topic Foundations: Band Tracking Workflow and Arrangement

Imagine a band playing a song in a rehearsal room. The players react to each other as they play. Recording everyone at once keeps them playing together, but every microphone also picks up the other instruments. So this topic asks: how is a band recorded while playing together, with a clear plan and each instrument's sound kept separate?

The work starts with a plan for the song. A song is built from sections. Verses tell the story, the chorus repeats the main idea, and a bridge is a different section, usually played once. The order of the sections is called the song structure. The band writes it on a chart, or the players memorize it, so everyone plays the same version.

The sections are only the outline. The players also decide which instruments play in each section, and what each one plays. This full plan is called the arrangement. A verse may use a few instruments, and the chorus adds more. Adding parts usually makes the song more intense, and removing parts makes it calmer. Short drum fills often mark the change to the next section.

With the plan agreed, recording usually begins with the drums and bass, because the other parts depend on their timing. These first parts are called bed tracks, because the other parts are built on them. The singer may sing a simple, unfinished version at the same time, only as a guide. The players often hear a steady beat in their headphones, called a click track. It keeps the band at one speed, so later parts match in time.

Playing in one room creates a problem. The drum microphone also picks up the guitar, and the vocal microphone picks up the drums. Sound that reaches the wrong microphone is called spill. Engineers reduce it with distance, with microphones pointed at their own source, and with movable padded screens. A loud kit or a singer may go into a small separate room. Some spill usually remains.

The band plays the song several times, and the engineer marks the best attempt. Later, other parts are added over the bed tracks, one at a time. Recording a concert, often from a truck or a portable rack, also keeps each microphone on its own track, but nothing can be repeated. A splitter sends a copy of each stage microphone to the recording equipment. A second recorder captures the same signals, so the show is still saved if one device fails. This is called redundant recording.

The flashcards cover song sections and their order, adding and removing parts, the charts musicians read, guide parts and the click, session order, screens and booths, and recording away from the studio. By the end, you will know how a song is planned and recorded, how spill is controlled, and how a live show is recorded so nothing is lost.
