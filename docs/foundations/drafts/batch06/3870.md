Topic Foundations: Instrument Recording by Source

Imagine you are recording a friend's acoustic guitar. Some notes rattle. Moving the microphone does not help, because the problem is in the guitar itself. So this topic asks: what is each instrument doing as it makes its sound, and how does that change the recording?

Most acoustic instruments have a part that vibrates and a part that moves the air. On a guitar, the strings are too thin to move much air. They pass their vibration through the bridge into a large flat piece of wood. That piece is called the soundboard. If the strings are too close to the neck, they hit the thin metal bars on it, called frets, and rattle. An adjustment of the neck and the string height is called a guitar setup. Until it is done, the rattle stays in every recording.

Electric guitars and basses add switches and knobs before any amplifier. The parts under the strings that turn their vibration into a signal are pickups. The switch that chooses which pickups you hear is called the pickup selector. The tone knob removes some high frequencies. Some basses also have a small amplifier inside, powered by a battery.

Other instruments change with the way the player plays. On a violin, the place where the bow touches the string changes the tone. Bowing near the bridge gives more high frequencies, and bowing over the fingerboard gives fewer. That place is called the bowing position. Brass instruments send most of their sound out of their wide open end. Players can put a mute into it to make the sound quieter and change its tone. In harmonicas and accordions, the player's breath or hands push air across thin metal strips that vibrate.

Keyboard instruments make their sound inside a case. In a piano, hammers covered in felt, a soft thick cloth, strike the strings. Electric organs mix tones with sliders and often play through a speaker that has a rotating part. A pipe organ has many sets of pipes. One set, and the control that switches it on, is called a stop. Electronic keyboards also have settings for pitch and touch.

Many of these instruments can also send out an electrical signal. A small box changes that signal so it can travel through a long cable with little noise. Engineers often record this signal, with no amplifier or effect, together with a microphone. Later, this recording can be sent through a real amplifier and recorded again. That process is called reamping. It lets the engineer choose the amplifier sound after the performance is finished.

The flashcards explain each family, from guitars, banjos and harps to bowed strings, winds, keyboards and organs, and their direct outputs. By the end, you will know how each instrument makes its sound and sends it out, and why many tone problems are best solved in the instrument itself.
