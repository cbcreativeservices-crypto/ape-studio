Topic Foundations: Instrument Recording by Source

Imagine you are recording a friend's acoustic guitar. Some notes rattle. Moving the microphone does not help, because the problem is in the guitar itself. So this topic asks: what is each instrument doing as it makes its sound, and how does that change the recording?

Most acoustic instruments have a part that vibrates and a part that moves the air. On a guitar, the strings are too thin to move much air. They pass their vibration through the bridge into a large flat piece of wood, called the soundboard. A harp also has a soundboard, while a banjo passes the vibration to a thin stretched head. If guitar strings sit too close to the neck, they can hit the thin metal bars on it, the frets, and rattle. An adjustment of the neck and the string height is called a guitar setup. Until then, the rattle is usually recorded too.

The way the player plays also changes the vibration. On a violin, the place where the bow touches the string changes the tone. Bowing near the bridge gives more high frequencies, and bowing over the fingerboard gives fewer. That place is called the bowing position.

In wind instruments, air inside a tube vibrates. The player starts it with the lips, with a thin reed, or by blowing across a hole. Brass instruments send most of their sound out of their wide open end, the bell. A mute placed in it makes the sound quieter and changes its tone. In harmonicas and accordions, the player's breath or hands push air across thin metal reeds that vibrate.

Keyboard instruments keep the vibrating part inside a case. In a piano, hammers covered in felt, a soft thick cloth, strike the strings. Many electric organs mix tones with sliders and often play through a speaker that has a rotating part. A pipe organ has many sets of pipes. One set, and the control that switches it on, is called a stop.

Electric instruments turn the vibration into an electrical signal. On an electric guitar or bass, the parts under the strings that do this are the pickups. A switch called the pickup selector chooses which pickups you hear, and the tone knob removes some high frequencies. This signal can also go straight to the recording. A small box changes the signal so it can travel through a long cable with little noise. Engineers often record this signal and a microphone at the same time. Later, the recorded signal can be sent through a real amplifier and recorded again. That process is called reamping. So the amplifier sound can be chosen after the performance.
