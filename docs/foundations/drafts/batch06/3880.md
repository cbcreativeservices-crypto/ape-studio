Topic Foundations: Drum-Kit Recording

Hit a drum once and listen closely. You hear the hit, then a note that keeps sounding, and sometimes a small rattle. Each drum is a hollow shell with a thin plastic head stretched across one or both open ends. Microphones pick up all of these sounds, and they are hard to remove later. So this topic asks: how is a drum kit, one drummer's set of drums and cymbals, prepared so it sounds the way the song needs?

Preparation starts with the heads. The head you hit is called the batter head. The head on the other side, called the resonant head, also vibrates and controls how long the drum keeps sounding. Bolts around the rim set the tension of each head. Higher tension gives a higher pitch. When the head has the same pitch at every bolt, the drum gives one clear note.

A well-tuned drum may still keep sounding too long, and then other instruments are harder to hear. So drummers add soft material to the head, such as a small sticky gel pad, tape, or a cloth. This is called drum damping. Too much removes the drum's clear pitch, so many engineers start with a little.

The kick drum, the largest drum, needs its own preparation. A pedal swings a small hammer, the beater, against the head. A hard beater adds a short click at the start of each hit. A soft beater made of felt, a thick pressed cloth, adds less. A pillow inside the shell shortens the long low sound. A round hole in the front head, called a kick port, also shortens it, and lets a microphone go inside.

The snare drum has a different problem. Thin metal wires are stretched across its bottom head, and their buzz is the snare's own sound. But they also buzz when other drums play. Loose bolts can rattle too. So before recording, the drummer plays each drum and listens, then tightens or damps any part that buzzes.

Cymbals usually keep sounding longer than the drums, and their high frequencies reach every microphone on the kit. So drummers choose cymbals and sticks to suit each song. Sound from other drums and cymbals that reaches a microphone is called drum bleed. Less bleed makes each drum easier to control.

Bleed also causes a problem for a small sensor on a drum, called a drum trigger. It makes an electronic unit play a stored sound with each hit. The sensor reacts only above a set level. If that level is too low, bleed can make it play when nobody hit the drum.

The flashcards explain how each part of the kit is prepared, from the heads to the sensors. By the end, you will know how each part of a kit makes its sound, and how preparing it reduces unwanted sounds in the microphones.
