Topic Foundations: Drum-Kit Recording

Imagine a drummer in a recording room. Each drum is a hollow shell with a thin plastic head stretched across one or both open ends. The microphones pick up every hit, every note that keeps sounding, and every small rattle, and these are hard to remove later. So this topic asks: how is a kit prepared so it sounds the way the song needs before recording starts?

Preparation starts with the heads. The head you hit is called the batter head. The head on the other side is not struck, but it vibrates too and controls how long the drum rings. It is called the resonant head. Bolts around the rim set the tension of each head. Higher tension gives a higher pitch. When the head has the same pitch at every bolt, the drum gives one clear note.

A well-tuned drum may still ring too long and make other instruments harder to hear. So drummers add material to the head, such as a gel pad, tape, or a cloth. This is called drum damping. Too much removes the drum's own note, so many engineers start with a little.

The kick drum, the largest drum, needs its own preparation. A pedal swings a small hammer, the beater, against the head. A hard beater adds a short click at the start of each hit, and a soft felt one adds less. A pillow inside the shell shortens the long low sound. A round hole in the front head, called a kick port, also shortens it, and allows a microphone to be placed inside.

The snare drum has a different problem. Thin metal wires are stretched across its bottom head, and their buzz is the snare's own sound. But they also buzz when other drums play. Loose bolts can rattle too. So before recording, the drummer plays each drum and listens, then tightens or damps any part that buzzes.

Cymbals usually ring longer than the drums, and their high frequencies can make the drums harder to hear. So drummers choose cymbals and sticks to suit each song. Each microphone also picks up the other drums and cymbals. That unwanted sound is called drum bleed.

Bleed also causes a problem for a small sensor on a drum, called a drum trigger. It makes an electronic unit play a stored sound with each hit. The sensor reacts only above a set level. If that level is too low, bleed can make it play when nobody hit the drum.

The flashcards explain each part: heads and tuning, ways to reduce ringing, the kick drum, snare wires and rattles, the parts of the kit, cymbals and sticks, and sensors. By the end, you will know how each part of a kit makes its sound, and how it is prepared so the microphones pick up only the sounds you want.
