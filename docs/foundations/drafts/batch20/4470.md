Topic Foundations: Magnetic Tape Preservation

Imagine a box of old letters kept in a damp basement for many years. Some pages stick together, and the paper has turned brittle and curled. A magnetic tape ages in similar ways, but the damage is often hidden, and one careless playback can destroy the recording. So how do you tell what condition a tape is in, and how do you keep it safe?

A tape is a thin plastic strip with a coating of tiny magnetic particles, held on by a glue-like layer. This glue often absorbs water from the air and breaks down. This is called binder hydrolysis. The coating turns sticky, so the tape makes a high, loud noise and leaves coating on the machine. This problem is called sticky-shed syndrome, and the coating that comes off is part of the recording. So one rule comes first: when a tape makes a high noise or leaves residue, stop the machine immediately.

The plastic strip can fail too. Many older tapes use a plastic called acetate. As it breaks down, it releases an acid that smells of vinegar, which makes the breakdown faster. The tape turns brittle, shrinks and curls, so it snaps easily. This is called vinegar syndrome.

Even a tape in good condition can be damaged by the way it is wound. The wound roll is called the tape pack. If it is wound too tightly, the inner layers bend out of shape. If it is wound too loosely, layers slide, and a sudden stop folds them into permanent creases. That folding is called cinching.

Because much of this damage is hidden, a tape is inspected before it is played. Someone checks the coating, the joins, the plain leader strips at each end, the reel and the case, and looks for mold or dirt.

Problems are treated with the gentlest methods first, such as wiping off dust with a soft, dry pad. A sticky tape can be heated gently in a controlled oven. This is called tape baking, and it is never done to acetate tapes, which heat can damage. It helps for a short time only, so the tape is copied immediately.

After that, the tape is wound evenly and stored in cool, dry air at a steady temperature. Reels stand upright. Over the years, the magnetism of a loud sound can copy onto the layer it touches as a faint echo. That echo is called print-through. Cool storage helps limit it, and storing the tape wound to its end makes the echo less noticeable. A cold tape is warmed slowly to room temperature inside a sealed bag, so water does not form on it.
