Topic Foundations: Magnetic Tape Preservation

Imagine an old tape reel that has sat in a hot attic for many years, and you want to play it at once. But an old tape can be damaged in ways you cannot see, and one careless playback can destroy the recording. So this topic answers one question: how do you tell what condition a tape is in, and how do you keep it safe?

Start with how a tape is made. A thin plastic strip carries a coating of tiny magnetic particles, held on by a glue-like layer. Over the years, this glue often absorbs water from the air and slowly breaks down. This is called binder hydrolysis. The coating turns sticky, so the tape makes a high, loud noise and leaves its coating on the machine. This problem is called sticky-shed syndrome. Coating that comes off takes part of the recording with it. So the first rule never changes: when a tape makes a high noise or leaves residue, stop the machine immediately.

The plastic strip can fail too. Many older tapes are made of a plastic called acetate. As it breaks down, it releases an acid that smells of vinegar, and that acid makes the breakdown faster. The tape turns brittle, shrinks and curls, so it snaps easily. This is called vinegar syndrome.

Even a healthy tape can be damaged by the way it is wound. The wound roll is called the tape pack. If it is wound too tightly, the inner layers bend out of shape. If it is wound too loosely, layers slide, and a sudden stop folds them into permanent creases. That folding is called cinching. Over the years, a loud sound can also be copied onto the next layer as a faint echo. That echo is called print-through.

So a tape is inspected before it is played. Someone checks the coating, the joins where it was repaired, the case, and any mold, insects or dirt. Then the problems are treated, starting with the gentlest methods. A sticky tape can be heated gently in a controlled oven for hours. This is called tape baking, and it is never done to acetate tapes, which heat can damage. It helps for a short time only, so the tape is copied immediately.

After that, the tape is wound evenly and stored in cool, dry air that stays at the same temperature, which slows decay. Reels stand upright on their edge. A cold tape is brought to room temperature slowly inside a sealed bag, so water does not form on it.

The flashcards cover decay of the coating and the plastic, damaged packs, joins, cases and contamination, treatment, and storage rooms. By the end, you will know why a tape is checked and stabilized before it is ever played, and what keeps it safe for years.
