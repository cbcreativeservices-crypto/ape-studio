Topic Foundations: Tape Machine Mechanics and Alignment

Imagine playing an old family recording on an old tape machine. The voices have few high frequencies, and long notes slowly rise and fall in pitch. So a technician asks one question first. Does this machine move the tape at a steady speed and read it exactly as it was recorded?

Moving the tape is the job of the machine's mechanical part, called the transport. The reels only feed and collect the tape. A metal shaft turns at a steady rate, and a rubber wheel presses the tape against it. That shaft is called the capstan. Spring-loaded arms measure how tightly the tape is pulled, so the reel motors can keep that pull steady. Guides hold the tape at the right height, and brakes stop the reels safely.

When one of these parts is worn, dirty, or not perfectly round, the speed changes, and the pitch changes with it. A slow rise and fall in pitch is called wow. Fast, small changes in speed are called flutter. A technician plays a steady test tone and measures how much its pitch moves.

Steady speed is only the first part of the job. A tape head is a small ring of magnetic metal with a very narrow opening where it touches the tape. That opening is called the gap. The tape must press closely against it. Even a tiny space, from dirt or a worn head surface, weakens the high frequencies first.

Close contact is not enough if the head is at the wrong angle. The gap must be at a right angle to the direction the tape moves, as it was on the machine that made the recording. If it tilts, high frequencies are lost, and the two stereo channels no longer play at exactly the same time. Setting this angle is called azimuth alignment. The head must also be at the correct height, and lean neither forward nor backward.

With the heads set, the machine is checked against a standard. A test tape with tones recorded at exact levels is called a reference tape. The technician plays it first and adjusts the playback until every tone reads correctly. Then the recording settings are adjusted. While recording, the machine adds a steady tone too high for people to hear, so the recording has less distortion. Its amount is set for each type of tape. Every measurement is written in a log.

The flashcards follow the same path. They cover the parts that move the tape, speed changes and their causes, and how heads are built and wear.
