Topic Foundations: Tape Machine Mechanics and Alignment

Imagine an old wind-up record player whose spring is running down. As the record slows, the music drops lower in pitch. A tape machine has the same weakness, because the pitch depends on how fast the tape moves. So how does a tape machine move the tape at a steady speed, and read it exactly as it was recorded?

Moving the tape is the job of the machine's mechanical part, called the tape transport. A metal shaft turns at a steady rate, and a rubber wheel presses the tape against it. That shaft is called the capstan. Spring-loaded arms sense the pull on the tape, so the reel motors can keep it steady. Brakes stop the reels safely.

When one of these parts is worn, dirty, or not perfectly round, the speed changes, and the pitch changes with it. A slow rise and fall in pitch is called wow. Fast, small changes in speed are called flutter. On many machines, a circuit checks the capstan speed and corrects it.

The tape then passes the heads. A tape head is a small ring of magnetic metal with a very narrow opening where it touches the tape. That opening is called the gap. The tape must press closely against it. Even a tiny space, from dirt or a worn head surface, weakens the high frequencies first. Moving tape also wears the head down.

Close contact is not enough if the head sits at the wrong angle. The gap must be at a right angle to the direction the tape moves. If it tilts, high frequencies are lost, and the stereo channels no longer play at exactly the same time. Setting this angle is called azimuth alignment. The head must also sit at the correct height. Otherwise, a faint copy of the next track can leak in, which is called crosstalk.

These settings are usually checked with a test tape, called a reference tape, that holds tones at exact levels. Its tones are used to set the head angle, the playback level and the frequency balance. When new parts are added to a song, the record head also plays the old tracks, so the parts stay in time. Setting up this playback is called sync-head alignment.

After that, the recording side is set. Before recording, a separate head erases the old sound. While recording, the machine adds a steady tone too high for people to hear. Without it, the tape stores sound unevenly, and that causes distortion. Setting the amount of this tone for each type of tape is called bias calibration. The results are usually written in a log.
