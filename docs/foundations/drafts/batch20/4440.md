Topic Foundations: Tape Machine Mechanics and Alignment

Imagine an old wind-up record player whose spring is losing its power. As the record slows, the music drops lower in pitch. A tape machine has the same weakness, because the pitch depends on how fast the tape moves. So how does a tape machine move the tape at a steady speed, and read it exactly as it was recorded?

Moving the tape is the job of the machine's mechanical part, called the tape transport. A metal shaft, called the capstan, turns at a steady rate, and a rubber wheel presses the tape against it. On many machines, spring-loaded arms sense the pull on the tape, so the reel motors can keep it steady. Brakes stop the reels gently, so the tape does not stretch.

When one of these parts is worn or dirty, the speed changes, and the pitch changes with it. A slow rise and fall in pitch is called wow. Fast, small changes in speed are called flutter.

The tape then passes the heads. A tape head is a small ring of magnetic material with a very narrow opening where it touches the tape. That opening is called the gap. Even a tiny space between tape and head weakens the high frequencies first. Over time, a head can also become slightly magnetized and add noise, so it is demagnetized.

Close contact is not enough if the head sits at the wrong angle. The gap must be at a right angle to the direction the tape moves. If it tilts, high frequencies are lost, and the stereo channels no longer play exactly in time. Setting this angle is called azimuth alignment. The head must also lean neither forward nor back, so it touches the tape evenly. If it sits at the wrong height, a faint copy of the next track can be heard. This is called crosstalk.

These settings are usually checked with a reference tape, a test tape that holds tones at exact levels. Its tones set the head angle, level and frequency balance. The record head is also set this way. When new parts are added to a song, the record head plays the old tracks, so the new and old parts stay in time.

Then the recording side is set. Just before the record head, a separate head erases the old sound. While recording, the machine adds a steady tone too high for people to hear. Without it, the tape records the weak parts of each sound wave poorly, and that causes distortion. Setting the amount of this tone for each type of tape is called bias calibration.

The flashcards explain how the transport moves the tape, what causes speed errors, and how the heads are cared for, aligned and calibrated. By the end, you will know what keeps a tape machine steady, and how it is set up to play and record correctly.
