Topic Foundations: Tape Machine Mechanics and Alignment

Imagine an old wind-up record player whose spring is losing its power. As the record slows, the music drops lower in pitch. A tape machine has the same weakness, because the pitch depends on how fast the tape moves. So how does a tape machine move the tape at a steady speed, and read it exactly as it was recorded?

Moving the tape is the job of the machine's mechanical part, called the tape transport. A metal shaft, called the capstan, turns at a steady rate, and a rubber wheel presses the tape against it. Spring-loaded arms sense the pull on the tape, so the reel motors can keep it steady.

When one of these parts is worn, dirty, or not perfectly round, the speed changes, and the pitch changes with it. A slow rise and fall in pitch is called wow. Fast, small changes in speed are called flutter.

The tape then passes the heads. A tape head is a small ring of magnetic material with a very narrow opening where it touches the tape. That opening is called the gap. Even a tiny space between the tape and the head, from dirt or wear, weakens the high frequencies first. Over time, a head can also become slightly magnetized and add noise, so it is demagnetized.

Close contact is not enough if the head sits at the wrong angle. The gap must be at a right angle to the direction the tape moves. If it tilts, high frequencies are lost, and the stereo channels no longer play at exactly the same time. Setting this angle is called azimuth alignment. The head must also sit at the correct height and lean neither forward nor back. Otherwise, a faint copy of the next track can leak in, which is called crosstalk.

These settings are usually checked with a reference tape, a test tape that holds tones at exact levels. Its tones set the head angle, the playback level and the frequency balance. The record head needs the same care, because when new parts are added to a song, it also plays the old tracks, so the parts stay in time. Setting up this playback is called sync-head alignment.

Then the recording side is set. Just before the record head, a separate head erases the old sound. While recording, the machine adds a steady tone too high for people to hear. Without it, the tape records the weak parts of each sound wave poorly, and that causes distortion. Setting the amount of this tone for each type of tape is called bias calibration. The results are written in a log.
