Topic Foundations: Tape Recording, Formats & Formulations

Imagine holding a cassette up to the light: a thin brown tape wound between two small reels. Unlike a record, there is no groove to see, because the sound is held as magnetism. So what is tape made of, how does it hold a sound, and why does each kind need its own settings?

Every tape starts as a thin plastic strip, called the base. On top is a coating of tiny magnetic particles, held together by a glue called the binder. In most tapes the particles are shaped like short needles, lined up along the tape. The full mix of materials is called the tape formulation. A thinner base fits more tape on a reel, but it stretches and tears more easily.

The materials matter because of how those particles behave. After the record head magnetizes them, they keep some of that magnetism. That kept magnetism is the recording, and it is called remanence. How hard it is to change or erase the magnetism is called coercivity. High coercivity means a stronger field is needed to record and to erase.

But the particles do not respond evenly to weak signals, so a recording made this way has a lot of distortion. The solution is to add a steady, very high tone that nobody can hear. As the tape moves away from the head, the tone fades, and the magnetism that remains follows the music closely. This is called biasing, and each formulation needs its own amount. High frequencies are still recorded at a lower level, because they are stored as very short patterns. So machines raise some frequencies while recording and make the opposite change on playback. A tape played with the wrong setting has the wrong balance of low and high frequencies.

Even with correct settings, tape has limits at both ends. At the quiet end, the uneven particles make a soft, steady hiss. At the loud end, the tape can hold only so much magnetism. Recorded louder than that, it gently lowers the loudest peaks and adds extra tones. That is called tape saturation. To make every machine agree on a normal level, a test tape holds a tone at one standard strength of magnetism. That strength is called reference fluxivity.

Tapes also differ in how their width is divided into tracks. One channel can use the whole width, or two, four or more narrower tracks can share it. These choices define each format, from studio reels to cassettes and video tape.
