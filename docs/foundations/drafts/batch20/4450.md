Topic Foundations: Tape Recording, Formats and Formulations

Imagine holding a cassette up to the light and seeing a thin brown tape between two small reels. Unlike a record, it has no groove, because the sound is stored as magnetism. So how does a tape store sound, and why does each kind of tape need its own settings?

Every tape starts as a thin plastic strip, called the base. On top is a coating of tiny magnetic particles, held together by a glue called the binder. The full mix of materials is called the tape formulation.

The materials matter because of how those particles behave. After the record head magnetizes them, they keep some of that magnetism. That kept magnetism is the recording, and it is called remanence. How hard it is to change or erase the magnetism is called coercivity. High coercivity means a stronger field is needed to record and to erase.

But the particles respond unevenly to weak signals, which causes distortion. The machine therefore adds a steady, very high tone that nobody can hear. It switches the magnetism of the particles back and forth as they pass the head. As the tape moves away, the tone fades, and the magnetism left behind matches the sound more closely. This is called biasing, and each formulation needs its own amount.

Biasing reduces distortion, but high frequencies still play back weaker. They are stored as very short magnetic patterns, which are harder to record and to read. So machines raise some frequencies while recording, which is called record equalization. Playback makes the opposite change. The wrong playback setting gives the wrong balance of low and high frequencies.

Even with correct settings, tape has limits at both ends. At the quiet end, the uneven particles make a steady noise called tape hiss. At the loud end, the tape can hold only a limited amount of magnetism. When the sound is too loud, the tape lowers the loudest peaks and adds extra tones. That is called tape saturation. Machines also need one shared normal level. A test tape holds a tone at one standard strength of magnetism, called reference fluxivity, and each machine is set to match it.

Each format balances these limits in its own way. The width of the tape can hold a single track or several tracks side by side, and that layout is called the track configuration. Wider tracks give more signal compared with the hiss, and faster tape records high frequencies more easily. Cassettes come in several types, each with a different coating, so the machine must use the matching bias and playback setting.
