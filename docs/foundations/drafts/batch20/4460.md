Topic Foundations: Audio Restoration and Archival

Before a phone with a cracked screen is repaired, many people copy its photos first. Old recordings are treated the same way, because some exist nowhere else and each play can damage them further. So how is sound copied correctly from a damaged original, and how is noise then removed without changing the real performance?

The work follows one rule: make a copy first, and remove noise later. The first copy is made at high quality. It is called a flat transfer, and it is kept unchanged. All repairs are made on a second copy. Every step is written down, from the machine and its settings to where the item came from.

Because the flat transfer must be correct, the original is prepared first. Old joins in a tape are repaired, and a worn felt pad inside a cassette is replaced. Then the tape must play the way it was recorded. If the speed is wrong, the pitch is too high or too low. Hum from the power supply often has a known pitch, so it can show the true speed.

Most recordings also changed the balance of low and high frequencies on purpose. Playback must make the exact opposite change. It uses a curve, which is a fixed plan of how much to raise or lower each frequency. This is called playback equalization. Old recordings used many curves, and often nobody wrote down which one. Many tapes also used a hiss-reduction system that must be reversed during playback. When the system is unknown, the tape is copied without reversing it. This is called an undecoded transfer, and the decision is made later.

Records have different problems. Older discs often have wider grooves, so they need a larger needle. A broken or very fragile disc may be damaged by any needle. Instead, detailed pictures of the groove are taken, and software turns its shape into sound. That is called noncontact playback.

Repair then begins on the second copy. A short click is removed, and the gap is filled from the sound on both sides. That is called declicking. For steady hiss, the tool first measures the noise in a quiet moment. Then it lowers only that noise at each frequency. This is called spectral denoising. Hum is removed at its own frequency and at the related higher frequencies. Too much removal adds unnatural sounds and removes real sound. So each repair is compared with the flat transfer, and every tool and setting is written down.

The flashcards explain each step in detail: planning and documenting a transfer, preparing tapes and discs, correct playback, and the repair tools. By the end, you will know how sound is safely copied from a damaged original, how noise is removed from it, and why every choice is written down.
