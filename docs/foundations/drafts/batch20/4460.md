Topic Foundations: Audio Restoration and Archival

Imagine a radio station finds boxes of old tapes and records in its basement. Some hold voices and music that exist nowhere else. The tape is fragile, and some discs are cracked. Each play can damage them further. So how do you copy sound from a damaged original correctly, and then remove noise without changing the real performance?

The work follows one rule: make a copy first, and remove noise later. The first copy is made at high quality, with nothing removed. It is called a flat transfer, and it is kept unchanged. Noise is removed from a second copy. Every step is written down, from the machine and its settings to where the item came from.

Because that first copy must be correct, the original is prepared first. Old joins between pieces of tape can dry and fail, and worn parts inside a cassette can make the high frequencies quieter, so these are repaired. Then the machine must play the tape the same way it was recorded. If the speed is wrong, the pitch is too high or too low. Hum from the power supply has a known pitch, so it can show the true speed. Most recordings also changed the balance of low and high frequencies on purpose, and playback must reverse that change with a matching curve. This is called playback equalization. Old recordings used many curves, and often nobody wrote down which one.

Many tapes also used a system that reduced hiss. It changed the sound during recording, and the same system must reverse it during playback. When the system is unknown, the tape is copied without reversing it. This copy is called an undecoded transfer, and the decision is made later.

Records have different problems. Older discs often have wider grooves, so they need a larger needle. A broken or very fragile disc may be damaged by any needle. Instead, detailed pictures of the groove are taken, and software turns its shape into sound. That is called noncontact playback.

When the flat copy is safe, repair begins. A short click is removed, and the gap is filled from the sound on both sides. That is called declicking. For steady hiss, the tool first measures the noise in a quiet moment. That sample is called a noise print, and only that noise is lowered. Hum is removed at its own frequency and at the related higher frequencies. Too much removal adds new, unnatural sounds and removes real sound, so each repair is compared with the original. A damaged part can also be replaced from a better copy.

The flashcards explain each step in detail, from planning the transfer to repairing damage and combining copies. By the end, you will know how sound is safely copied from a damaged original, how noise is removed from it, and why every choice is written down.
