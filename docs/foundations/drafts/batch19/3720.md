Topic Foundations: Theatrical Sound

On a phone call, you usually hear a friend clearly, because the microphone is close to their mouth. In a musical, singers are far from most of the audience. They dance and turn away while a band plays. Yet the audience expects to hear every word, every night, even when another actor plays the role. So how does the sound team do this?

It starts with a microphone on every performer. A tiny microphone usually sits near the hairline, and a thin cable runs to a radio transmitter hidden in the costume. Lead actors often wear a spare microphone. On the mixing desk, each role has its own set of controls, called a character channel. If another actor plays the role, that actor's microphone is sent to the same channel.

Each voice is then adjusted on its own channel. A filter reduces low sounds, such as footsteps and clothing noise. A compressor keeps soft and loud singing at a steadier level. A short delay makes the voice seem to come from the actor, not the loudspeaker.

An open microphone that nobody is using picks up stage noise. So the operator turns each microphone up just before the actor speaks, and down after the last word. This is called line-by-line mixing. In a busy scene, one person cannot remember every level, so the mixing desk stores them. Each saved moment of the show is a cue. A show run from this list of saved steps is called a cue-based mix. Some channels can be locked, so that no cue changes them.

The voices must stay louder than the band. The musicians usually play in a lowered area by the stage, each instrument with its own microphone. All their channels are combined into one shared path with one volume control. That path is called the orchestra subgroup. With it, the operator can raise or lower the whole band at once.

Some band parts are recorded, and so are sounds such as a doorbell, thunder or rain. A playback computer holds each one as a separate cue, with its own level and start point. Each cue goes to chosen loudspeakers, even one hidden inside a radio that is part of the scenery. Quiet background music under speech is called underscore.

Because the show depends on that computer, a second computer follows the same cues at the same point. If the first fails, the second continues. This is called redundant playback. Each part of this work belongs to someone in the sound team. A designer plans the sound, and a production sound engineer builds and looks after the system. A sound operator mixes every performance.
