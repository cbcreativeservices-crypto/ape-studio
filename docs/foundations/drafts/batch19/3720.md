Topic Foundations: Theatrical Sound

On a phone call, you usually hear a friend clearly, because the microphone is close to their mouth. In a musical, singers are far from most of the audience. They dance and turn away while a band plays. Yet the audience expects to hear every word, every night, even when another actor plays the role. So how does the sound team do this?

It starts with a microphone on every performer. A tiny microphone usually sits near the hairline, and a thin cable runs to a radio transmitter hidden in the costume. Lead actors often wear a spare microphone. On the mixing desk, each role has its own set of controls, called a character channel. If another actor plays the role, that actor's microphone is sent to the same channel.

Each voice is adjusted on its own channel. A filter removes footstep noise, and a compressor keeps soft and loud singing at a steadier level. A short delay makes the voice seem to come from the actor, not the loudspeaker. An unused open microphone picks up stage noise, so the operator raises each one just before the actor speaks and lowers it after the last word. This is called line-by-line mixing.

In a busy scene, one person cannot remember every level, so the mixing desk stores them. Each saved moment of the show is a cue. The cues are built during rehearsals, and in the show the operator moves to each next cue in the order of the script. A show run from this list of saved steps is called a cue-based mix.

The voices must stay louder than the band. The musicians usually play in a lowered area by the stage, each instrument with its own microphone. All their channels are combined into one shared path with one sliding volume control, called the orchestra subgroup, so the operator can raise or lower the whole band at once.

Some band parts are recorded, and so are sounds such as a doorbell, thunder or rain. A playback computer holds each one as a separate cue, with its own level, slow volume change and start point. Each cue goes to chosen loudspeakers, even one hidden inside a radio that is part of the scenery. Quiet background music under speech is called underscore.

Because the show depends on that computer, a second computer follows the same cues at the same point. If the first fails, the second continues. This is called redundant playback. The show's sound is also sent to the performers on stage, to the dressing rooms, and to receivers for listeners with hearing loss.

The flashcards explain each of these parts, and the people who plan and operate the sound. By the end, you will know how theater sound is planned, mixed and protected, so it stays the same whichever actor plays each role.
