Topic Foundations: Stage Platforms, Scenery and Soft Goods

Imagine you sit in a theater before a play. A heavy curtain hides the stage. When it opens, you see the walls of a room, a painted sky, and actors on different levels. Later, those walls move off the stage and a forest appears. So what is a stage built from, and how do its parts change what the audience sees and hears?

Much of it is cloth. All the stage fabrics and curtains together are called soft goods. The main curtain opens to start the show. Some curtains slide sideways along a track, and some lift straight up. Many theaters also have a fireproof barrier at the front of the stage, called the fire curtain. In a fire, it drops to block flames and smoke from the audience. Stage fabrics are usually treated and tested so they do not burn easily.

Other curtains never open, because they are there to hide things. Tall, narrow curtains at the sides hide the areas where crew members wait. Each one is called a leg. Wide, short curtains across the top hide the lights above. Each one is called a border. They are made of black cloth that is not shiny, so they do not reflect light.

Behind the actors, a large cloth often shows the scene. It may be painted, or lit evenly with colored light. One kind of cloth has a very open weave. Lit from the front, it looks solid. When only the area behind it is lit, the audience can see through it. That cloth is called a scrim. So one cloth can show two different scenes, one after the other.

Under the actors are platforms. Low platforms lift a choir or a drummer so the audience can see them. They are locked together so they cannot move. High edges get guardrails, and ramps let people in wheelchairs reach each level. Platforms can also move. A whole room of lightweight painted walls can sit on a platform with wheels and roll off the stage. Part of the floor can turn in a circle, or rise from below.

All of this also changes the sound. A stage is usually tall, and much of the players' sound rises into the empty space above. For music without loudspeakers, crews place hard walls around the players and hard panels above them. That set is called an acoustic shell. It sends the sound back to the musicians, so they hear each other, and out to the audience. Heavy curtains do the opposite and absorb sound.

The flashcards explain each of these parts in detail, from curtains and fabrics to platforms, moving floors and sound panels. By the end, you will know what a stage is built from, how its parts move between scenes, and how they change what the audience sees and hears.
