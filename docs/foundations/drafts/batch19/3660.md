Topic Foundations: Venue Architecture and Spaces

Imagine you buy a ticket and walk into a theater. You see only the seats and the stage. Behind the walls are rooms for performers, workshops, and large doors where trucks unload. So this topic asks: how is a venue arranged, and where does each part of a show happen?

First, venues differ in how the stage and the audience are placed. In many theaters, every seat faces one framed opening at the front of the stage. That is called a proscenium theater. Other venues are open to the sky over most of the audience, and the seats or grass rise up a slope. That kind of venue is called an amphitheater. A black-box theater is one plain dark room. Its stage and seats can be set up differently for each show. Venues also range from small clubs to large arenas, but most share the same main parts.

Churches are another kind of venue, and their spaces have older names. People enter through a hall just inside the main doors, called the narthex. The main worship room is often called the sanctuary, and the area where people sit is called the nave. At the front, the leaders of the service stand near the altar, in an area called the chancel. Singers often stand in a raised balcony called a choir loft.

In every venue, the area where the audience sits is called the house. The first raised level above its main floor is often called the mezzanine. Higher levels are called balconies, and small private booths on the side walls are called box seats. Aisles run between the rows, and spaces for wheelchairs are called accessible seating. Seats at the back, at the sides, and under a balcony are often far from the stage or partly blocked. The house manager is in charge of the audience and its safety, and tickets are sold or collected at the box office.

Good sound for every seat also depends on where the engineer works. The main mixing desk is usually in the seating area, near the center and some distance from the stage. There the engineer hears what most people hear. That place is called the front-of-house mix position. Many churches place a sound desk among the seats in the same way. Lighting and spotlight operators often work from booths at the back of the house or on a balcony.

Every show also needs rooms the audience never sees. Performers prepare in dressing rooms and wait in a shared room near the stage, called the green room. Scenery and costumes are made in workshops. In larger venues, equipment often enters across a raised platform at the height of a truck's floor. That platform is called the loading dock.

Every space must also stay safe for the people in it. Fire doors and the road for fire trucks must never be blocked. If the power fails, emergency lighting turns on by itself and shows the way to the exits. A first aid station holds supplies for treating injuries.

The flashcards describe each of these spaces, from the type of building to the rooms behind the stage. By the end, you will know how a venue is arranged, and where each part of a show happens.
