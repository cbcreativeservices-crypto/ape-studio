Topic Foundations: Venue Architecture and Spaces

Imagine you arrive at a theater for your first show as crew. The audience will see only the seats and the stage. Behind the walls there are rooms for performers, workshops, and large doors where trucks arrive. So this topic asks: how is a venue arranged, and where does each part of a show happen?

First, venues differ in how the stage and the audience are placed. In many theaters, every seat faces one framed opening at the front of the stage. That is called a proscenium theater. Others are open to the sky, with seats or grass rising up a slope. That kind of venue is called an amphitheater. In a plain black room, the stage and seats can be moved for each show. Venues also range from small clubs to arenas and stadiums. In halls for music without microphones, close side walls reflect sound quickly, so listeners hear sound arriving from the sides as well.

Wherever the stage is, the area where the audience sits is called the house. The first raised level above its main floor is often called the mezzanine, and balconies are higher. In the house, left and right are named from the audience's seats. Seats at the back, at the sides, and under a balcony are often the hardest to reach with clear sound and a full view. So designers check these seats with special care.

Good sound for every seat also depends on where the engineer works. The main mixing desk is usually in the seating area, near the center and some distance from the stage. There the engineer hears what most people hear. That place is called the front-of-house mix position. In a closed room at the back, the engineer hears less of the sound in the hall.

Churches need a mix position too, but many of their main spaces have older names. The long main room where people sit is called the nave. The leaders of the service stand near the altar at the front. Singers and a pipe organ often sit in a raised area. Families with small children may sit in a separate room with a window.

Every venue also needs rooms the audience never sees. Performers prepare in dressing rooms. Scenery and costumes are made in workshops, and instruments and cases have storage rooms. Equipment usually enters across a raised platform at the height of a truck's floor. That platform is called the loading dock. From there, wide corridors and an elevator for heavy loads take cases to the stage. Fire doors and the road for fire trucks must never be blocked.

The flashcards cover the kinds of venues and halls, the seating levels, the mix and control rooms, church spaces, backstage rooms, and the route for equipment. By the end, you will know how a venue is arranged, and where each part of a show happens.
