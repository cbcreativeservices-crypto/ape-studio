Topic Foundations: Venue Architecture and Spaces

Imagine you buy a ticket and walk into a theater. You see only the seats and the stage. Behind the walls are rooms for performers, workshops, and large doors where trucks unload. So this topic asks: how is a venue arranged, and where does each part of a show happen?

Venues differ in how the stage and the audience are placed. In many theaters, every seat faces one framed opening at the front of the stage. That is called a proscenium theater. Other venues are open to the sky over most of the audience, and the seats or grass rise up a slope. That is called an amphitheater. A black-box theater is one plain dark room. Its stage and seats can be set up differently for each show. Concert halls have their own shapes, such as a tall, narrow box with the stage at one end, or seats set in stepped blocks around the stage. Venues also range from small music clubs to large arenas and stadiums.

Many churches are also used as venues, and their rooms have older names that crews still use. People enter through a hall near the doors. The main worship room, where people sit, is often called the sanctuary. Singers often stand in a raised balcony called a choir loft.

In every venue, the area where the audience sits is called the house. The first raised level above its main floor is often called the mezzanine. Each higher level is called a balcony. Some seats are in small private booths on the side walls. Rows are divided by aisles, and some places are kept for people who use wheelchairs. One person, the house manager, is in charge of the audience.

Good sound for every seat also depends on where the engineer works. The main mixing desk is usually in the seating area, near the center and away from the stage. There the engineer hears what most people hear. That place is called the front-of-house mix position. Lighting operators often work from booths at the back of the house.

Every show also needs rooms the audience never sees. Performers prepare in dressing rooms and wait in a shared room near the stage, called the green room. Scenery and costumes are made in workshops, and equipment is kept in storage rooms. Equipment often enters across a raised platform at the height of a truck's floor. That platform is called the loading dock. These working areas also hold the venue's safety features. Fire doors and the road for fire trucks must never be blocked. If the power fails, emergency lighting turns on by itself and shows the exits.

The flashcards describe each of these spaces, from the type of building to the rooms behind the stage. By the end, you will know how a venue is arranged, and where each part of a show happens.
