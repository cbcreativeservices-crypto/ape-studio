Topic Foundations: Stage Geography & Backstage

Imagine you are watching a school play. You see a lit floor, the actors, and some scenery. An actor walks to the side and disappears, then enters again from the other side. Around what you see are hidden spaces and paths. The crew must place every actor, chair and microphone stand in the same spot at every show. So how does everyone name each place on and around a stage?

The answer starts with shared directions. They are named from the performer's point of view, facing the audience. The performer's left side is called stage left. From a seat, that side is on your right. The back of the stage is called upstage, and the front is called downstage. The two pairs combine, so downstage right is the front corner on the performer's right.

These directions work only when the audience sits in front. In many theaters, a wall separates the stage from the seats, and the audience watches through a large opening in it. That wall is called the proscenium. Other stages have no such wall. Some extend forward so the audience sits on three sides, and some have seats all around. On those stages, crews usually agree in advance which side counts as the front.

Directions describe areas, but a crew also needs exact spots. One reference line runs down the middle of the stage, and another runs across it. Every position can be measured from these two lines. Then a small piece of colored tape marks each exact spot on the floor. That piece is called a spike mark. In the dark, a stage edge can cause a dangerous fall, so some tape glows, and open edges have rails or covers.

Much marked scenery and equipment is kept out of view until it is needed. It is kept in the hidden spaces at each side of the stage. Each of these side spaces is called a wing. Actors wait there to enter, and scenery is stored in order. A hidden path behind, above or under the stage lets people cross to the other side without being seen.

The space under the stage holds lifts and machines. A piece of the floor can open, so an actor or scenery can rise from below. Beyond the stage are the rooms and places that performers and crew use during the show. They use their own door and read a notice board for the day's times. Near the stage there is space for fast costume changes. At the side, the stage manager gives the commands for every change, and an engineer controls what performers hear.

The flashcards follow the same order, from the directions to every hidden space beside and below the stage. By the end, you will know how each place on and around a stage is named and measured, and how performers and crew move unseen.
