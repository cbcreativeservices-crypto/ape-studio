Topic Foundations: Stage Geography and Backstage

When you give a friend directions, "turn left" helps only if you both know which way your friend is facing. A stage crew needs the same agreement. Actors leave through hidden spaces and enter again elsewhere. The crew must place every actor, chair and microphone stand in the same spot at every show. So how does everyone name each place on and around a stage?

The answer starts with shared directions. They are named from the performer's point of view, facing the audience. The performer's left side is called stage left. From a seat, that side is on your right. The back of the stage is called upstage, and the front is called downstage. The two pairs combine, so downstage right is the front corner on the performer's right. On plans, crews often shorten these names to letters, such as DS for downstage and USL for upstage left.

These directions work only when the audience sits in front. In many theaters, a wall separates the stage from the seats, and the audience watches through a large opening in it. That wall is called the proscenium. Some other stages have no such wall. They extend forward so the audience sits on three sides, or they have seats all around. On those stages, crews usually agree in advance which side counts as the front.

Directions describe areas, but a crew also needs exact spots. One reference line runs down the middle of the stage, and another runs across it. Small pieces of colored tape mark exact spots on the floor. Each piece is called a spike mark. In the dark, a stage edge can cause a dangerous fall, so some tape glows, and open edges have rails or covers.

Scenery and equipment are kept out of view until they are needed. They wait in the hidden spaces at each side of the stage. Each of these side spaces is called a wing. Actors wait there to enter. A hidden path behind or above the stage lets people cross to the other side unseen. The space under the stage can hold lifts and machines. A piece of the floor can open, so an actor or scenery can rise from below.

All these hidden spaces lead to the rooms that performers and crew use during the show. They enter through a separate stage door and read a notice board for the day's times. Near the stage there is space for fast costume changes. At the side of the stage, the stage manager often directs every change. Nearby, an engineer mixes what the performers hear on stage. That place is called the monitor-world position.

The flashcards follow the same order, from the directions to the hidden spaces and rooms. By the end, you will know how each place on and around a stage is named and measured, and how performers and crew move unseen.
