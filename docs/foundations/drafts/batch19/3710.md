Topic Foundations: Show Control, Cueing & Communications

Imagine you are backstage during a play. An actor slams a door, and at that exact moment the stage lights turn off and thunder sounds. A planned change of light, sound or scenery is called a production cue. So how does a team make many changes happen at exactly the right moment, every night?

One person, the stage manager, usually decides the timing. Before rehearsals, the moment for every change is written into a special copy of the script. During the show, the stage manager reads from it and speaks to each operator, the person at each control desk. First comes a warning. That warning is called stand by. Then, at the exact moment, one short word tells the operator to act. That word is called go. Other fixed phrases ask for a repeat, or stop the show when something is unsafe.

These words must reach the operators, but the audience must not hear them. So the crew talks on a private system of headsets. That system is called the intercom. Each headset plugs into a small box on the belt, with a button to talk. On a simple system with one channel, everyone hears everyone. Because people share one channel, they speak briefly. Larger systems give each crew its own channel.

Not everyone can wear a headset, though. Performers in dressing rooms need to know when to go to the stage. Backstage loudspeakers carry the live sound from the stage, and timed announcements that say how many minutes remain before the start. Where speech is not possible, a small lamp gives the signal. Usually the lamp turns on to mean get ready, and turns off, or turns green, to mean act now. That lamp is called a cue light.

On go, the sound operator usually presses one button on a computer. The computer holds every sound in a numbered list. Each press plays the next item. One item can also start the next automatically, or slowly lower a sound. A stop button can end every sound at once.

Some changes must happen together more exactly than people can press buttons. So machines signal each other. One machine can send a continuous time signal, and the others act at set times on it. Others send short messages, or simply close a switch. Linking the systems so that one press starts sound, light and video together is called show control. In a haunted house, an attraction with scary rooms for visitors, a hidden sensor can start a scream when a visitor steps on a certain spot.

The flashcards explain each part: the words for calling a show and the calls before it, intercoms, backstage loudspeakers and cue lights, lists of sounds, machine signals, and haunted attractions. By the end, you will know how the crew talks privately, and how people and machines start each change on time.
