Topic Foundations: Show Control, Cueing and Communications

At the start of a race, runners hear a warning and then the start signal, so they all begin together. A live show needs the same timing. A planned change of light, sound or scenery is called a production cue. So how does a team make each change happen at the right moment, every night?

The stage manager usually calls the timing. The moment for every change is planned before and during the technical rehearsals. It is written into the stage manager's special copy of the script, called the prompt book. In the show, the stage manager reads from it and speaks to each operator at a control desk. First comes a warning, called stand by. Then, at the exact moment, one word tells the operator to act. That word is called go. Other fixed phrases ask for a repeat, or stop the show when something is unsafe.

These words must reach the operators, but the audience must not hear them. So the crew talks on a private system of headsets. That system is called the intercom. Each headset plugs into a small belt box with a talk button. On a simple system with one shared channel, everyone hears everyone, so people speak briefly. Larger systems give each crew its own channel.

Not everyone wears a headset. Performers in dressing rooms hear the stage through backstage loudspeakers. Before the show, the stage manager also makes timed calls on them. The half-hour call says the show starts in about half an hour. The places call sends everyone to their starting positions. Where speech is not possible, a small lamp gives the signal. Usually the lamp turns on to mean get ready, and turns off, or turns green, to mean act now. That lamp is called a cue light.

On go, the sound operator usually presses one button on a computer. The computer holds every sound in a numbered list. Each press plays the next item. One item can also start the next automatically, or slowly lower a sound.

Some changes must happen together more exactly than people can press buttons. So machines signal each other. One machine can send a running clock signal, and others start their cues at set times. A simpler signal is a closing switch, which starts a cue. Linking systems so one press starts sound, light and video together is called show control.

An actor can also press such a switch, or a visitor can step on it. In a haunted attraction, a place with scary rooms, a hidden floor switch can play a scream as a visitor walks past. A planned scary moment like this is called a scare cue.

The flashcards explain each part, from calling a show to the machines that start changes. By the end, you will know how the crew talks privately, and how people and machines start each change on time.
