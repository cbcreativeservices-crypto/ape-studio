Topic Foundations: Orchestra Pit and Ensemble Layout

When friends sing together across a large room, they often start and stop at slightly different times. A musical has the same problem. The singers are on stage, and the musicians often sit in a lowered space in front of the first row of seats. That space is called the orchestra pit. How is it arranged, and how do the players stay in time with singers they often cannot see?

In many theaters, the pit floor sits on a large powered platform. It can drop low to make the pit, or rise to add stage or seats. That platform is called the pit lift. An open pit has a dangerous edge, so a low wall or railing guards the front. The pit doors are also exit routes, so nothing may block them.

With the space safe, the players need seats. A common plan puts the quieter string instruments, such as violins, at the front, the woodwinds in the middle, and the loud brass and drums at the back. Each group has a leader, who sits in the top seat of that group. That seat is called the principal chair. In a small pit, a very loud instrument may play in a closed room beside it.

Players at the back need to see the conductor, who leads the music with hand movements. So they often sit on low platforms that rise toward the back. The conductor usually stands on a low platform at the audience side of the pit, facing the stage. The players sit between the conductor and the stage, facing the conductor. So the conductor can see the players and, above them, the singers. A clear view between the conductor and a performer is called a conductor sightline.

Not every musician sits in the pit. Sometimes a band plays on the stage, where the audience can see it. A choir may stand on risers at the back of the stage. Others play behind the scenery or in another room, with no clear view. Then a camera points at the conductor, and screens show the beat.

The players must also hear the singers over their own instruments. So the sound team sends sound back to them through small speakers or earphones. This sound is called pit foldback. The pit also stays in touch with the crew through headsets, cue lights and talk microphones. Together these links are called pit communication.

The audience must hear the musicians in the pit too. A microphone aimed at one instrument or group is called a pit spot microphone. Others hang above the players to pick up the whole group.

The flashcards follow the same order, from the pit itself to its seating, sight and sound. By the end, you will know how a pit is built and seated, how its players see and hear the show, and how their music reaches the audience.
