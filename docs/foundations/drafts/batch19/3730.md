Topic Foundations: Worship Sound

Imagine a large stone church during a service. A leader reads a prayer, a choir sings, and an organ plays. When the organ stops, its sound continues for several seconds. That is good for the music. But spoken words are hard to follow, because each word is still sounding when the next begins. So how can speech stay clear in a room built for music?

Sound continues because it reflects from the walls, floor and ceiling. When the end of one word hides the start of the next, that is called reverberant masking. Music usually needs more reflection, and speech needs less. So most worship rooms are a compromise.

The team cannot change the building, but it can plan for each place where people speak. A preacher speaks from a raised stand called a pulpit. Readers use a reading stand, and the leader also speaks at the altar. Many churches also have a raised stage for singers and a band.

Each place needs a microphone close to the mouth, because it picks up more of the voice and less of the room's reflections. A leader who moves often wears a tiny microphone on a thin arm that hooks over one ear. This is called an earset microphone. A person standing in a baptism pool, a pool of water used in some religious ceremonies, should use only a wireless microphone, because a cable can bring deadly electricity into the water.

A choir cannot stand close to microphones. Microphones that hang from the ceiling and point down at the singers are called hanging choir microphones. Because they are far away, they also pick up the room's reflections. So the usual aim is to add only enough level for the back rows to hear the words.

The band at the front creates a different problem. Loud drums and amplifiers are kept behind screens or in closed boxes, away from other microphones. The players also need to hear themselves. A speaker on the stage that points back at them is called a platform monitor. Its sound also reaches the choir microphones, so both are planned together.

Every open microphone also picks up sound from the loudspeakers. If that sound returns too loudly, the system makes a loud ringing tone. So each extra open microphone lowers the level possible before ringing starts. When a device turns down unused microphones by itself, this is called automatic microphone mixing. Tall, narrow column loudspeakers send more sound toward the seats and less toward the stone walls.

The flashcards cover worship room acoustics, the places and people of a service, and microphones for talkers, choirs and the congregation. They also cover the band, organ, monitors, and systems that control many microphones and aim the sound. By the end, you will know why worship rooms are difficult for speech, and how the sound is planned so words stay clear.
