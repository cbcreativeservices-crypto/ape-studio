Topic Foundations: Worship Sound

If you speak in an empty stairwell with hard walls, each word keeps sounding after you stop. Many large places of worship have the same effect, but longer. That often helps music, but spoken words are hard to follow, because each word is still sounding when the next begins. So how can speech stay clear in a room built for music?

Sound continues because it reflects from the walls, floor and ceiling. When the end of one word hides the start of the next, that is called reverberant masking. Music usually needs more reflection, and speech needs less. So most worship rooms are a compromise.

The team cannot change the building, but it can plan for each talker. A preacher speaks from a raised stand called a pulpit, and the leader also speaks at the altar. Each place needs a microphone close to the mouth, to pick up more of the voice and fewer reflections. A leader who moves often wears a tiny microphone on a thin arm that hooks over one ear. This is called an earset microphone. A person standing in a baptism pool, used in some religious ceremonies, should use only a wireless microphone. A cable can bring deadly electricity into the water.

A choir rarely stands close to a microphone. Microphones that hang from the ceiling and point down at the singers are called hanging choir microphones. Because they are far away, they also pick up the room's reflections. So the usual aim is to add just enough level for the back rows to hear the words.

The people in the seats sing too, often with an organ. The room's reflections help them hear each other, so the system usually adds little to their singing. A microphone above the seats may pick up the singing for a recording or a stream. A pipe organ is usually loud enough to fill the room by itself.

A band at the front adds a different problem. Loud drums and amplifiers are often kept behind screens or in boxes. A loudspeaker on the stage that points back at the players is called a platform monitor. Its sound also reaches the choir microphones, so both are planned together.

Every open microphone also picks up sound from the loudspeakers. If that sound returns too loudly, the system makes a loud ringing tone. So each extra open microphone lowers the level possible before ringing starts. When a device turns down unused microphones by itself, this is called automatic microphone mixing. Tall, narrow column loudspeakers can also send more sound toward the seats and less toward the high ceiling, so the room adds fewer reflections.

The flashcards explain each part, from the room and its people to the microphones and loudspeakers. By the end, you will know why worship rooms are difficult for speech, and how the sound is planned so words stay clear.
