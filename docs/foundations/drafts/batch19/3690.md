Topic Foundations: Rigging Hardware, Loads and Motorized Flying

Before hanging a heavy mirror, you check that the hook can hold it. Crews hang much heavier lights, speakers and scenery over people. So how is heavy equipment held and moved overhead, so that nothing falls on anyone?

A falling load can kill, and a worn or overloaded part can break without warning. So every part has a maximum weight for normal use, set far below the weight that breaks it. That limit is called the working load limit. A sudden jolt can go past it. A load that drops a short way and is caught pulls much harder than its weight. That jolt is called a shock load. Because any load could fall, nobody stands under it while it moves, or until it is secured. That area is called the fall zone.

Loads also need strong places to hang from. Many theaters have a tall space above the stage, with high walkways where crews work. At the top is a steel frame that holds pulleys and ropes. That frame is called the gridiron.

In many theaters, long metal pipes hang from steel ropes that run over pulleys on the gridiron and down to a frame of stacked steel weights. When the weights match the load, one person can move the pipe by hand. This is called a counterweight system. If they do not match, the pipe can move fast on its own.

The height where each pipe stops is called the trim height. In theater language, "in" means down and "out" means up. Before a move, the crew says the direction loudly and waits for an answer. Anyone may say stop, and everything stops at once.

Concerts often hang equipment from the steel beams of a roof. An electric machine that pulls a metal chain lifts a frame of metal tubes. That machine is called a chain motor. Its control box usually has an emergency stop button. Where there is no roof, or the roof cannot hold the weight, towers that stand on the floor hold it. This is called a ground support system.

Whatever holds the load, the maker's table, called a load chart, shows how much it may hold. A frame holds less at one spot than when weight is spread along it. Small metal parts join the steel above to the load below. Metal loops closed by a pin join the parts. Fabric straps wrap around beams. Steel ropes often end in loops held by pressed metal sleeves. Each part must be rated for the load. A second steel rope catches the load if a part fails. A trained rigger checks every part for damage before a load is lifted.

The flashcards explain each part of this chain, from safety to the hardware that holds each load. By the end, you will know how equipment is hung and moved overhead, and how each part is chosen and checked.
