Topic Foundations: Rigging Hardware, Loads and Motorized Flying

Before hanging a heavy mirror, you check that the hook can hold it. Crews hang much heavier lights, speakers and scenery over people. So how is heavy equipment held and moved overhead, so that nothing falls on anyone?

A falling load can kill, and a worn or overloaded part can break without warning. So every part has a maximum weight for normal use, set far below the weight that breaks it. That limit is called the working load limit. A load that drops a short way and is caught pulls much harder than its weight. That jolt is called a shock load. Because any load could fall, nobody stands under it while it moves, or until it is secured. That area is called the fall zone.

Loads also need strong places to hang from. Many theaters have a tall space above the stage, with ladders, high walkways and platforms where crews work. At the top, a steel frame holds the pulleys. That frame is called the gridiron.

Long metal pipes hang from steel ropes that run over pulleys on the gridiron and down to a frame of stacked steel weights. When the weights match the load, one person can move the pipe by hand. This is called a counterweight system. If they do not match, the pipe can move fast on its own.

The height where each pipe stops is called the trim height. In theater language, "in" means down and "out" means up. Before a move, the crew says the direction loudly and waits for an answer. Anyone may say stop, and everything stops at once.

Concerts often hang equipment from the steel beams of a roof. An electric machine that pulls a metal chain lifts a frame of metal tubes. That machine is called a chain motor. Its control box usually has an emergency stop button. Where no roof is strong enough, towers that stand on the floor hold the weight. This is called a ground support system.

For a frame of metal tubes, the maker's table, called a load chart, shows how much it may hold. It holds less at one spot than when the weight is spread along it. A series of small parts joins the beam to the load. A fabric strap often wraps around the beam. Metal loops closed by a pin join it to the next part, often a steel rope that ends in a loop held by a pressed metal sleeve. Each part must be rated for the load. A second steel rope can catch the load if a part fails. A trained rigger checks every part for damage before a load is lifted.

The flashcards explain each part of this chain, from safety to the hardware that holds each load. By the end, you will know how equipment is hung and moved overhead, and how each part is chosen and checked.
