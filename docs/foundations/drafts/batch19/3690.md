Topic Foundations: Rigging Hardware, Loads and Motorized Flying

Before hanging a heavy mirror, you check that the hook can hold it. If the hook fails, the mirror falls. Crews hang much heavier lights, speakers and scenery above people's heads. This topic asks: how is heavy equipment held and moved overhead, so that nothing falls on anyone?

The answer starts with the danger. A falling load can kill, and parts often break without warning. So every part has a maximum weight it may hold in normal use, set far below the weight that breaks it. That limit is called the working load limit. A load that drops a short distance and is caught pulls with much more force than its weight alone. The ground under a load is called the fall zone. Nobody stands there while the load moves, or until it is secured.

Loads also need strong places to hang from. Many theaters have a tall space above the stage, with high walkways. Rails and harnesses protect crews there from falls. At the top is a steel frame that holds pulleys and ropes. That frame is called the gridiron. Arenas hang equipment from the steel beams of the roof instead.

In a theater, each long metal pipe hangs from steel ropes. The ropes run over pulleys on the gridiron and down to a frame of stacked steel weights. When the weights match the load, one person can move the pipe by hand. This is called a counterweight system. If they do not match, the pipe can move very fast and hit something.

Once a pipe can move, the crew must know where it stops. The height each pipe is set to hang at is called the trim height. In theater language, "in" means down and "out" means up. Before a move, the crew says the direction loudly and waits for an answer. Anyone may say stop, and everything stops at once.

Concerts usually have no such tall space, so they use electric machines instead. An electric machine that pulls a metal chain lifts a frame of metal tubes. That machine is called a chain motor. A control box operates it and has an emergency stop button.

Small metal parts join the steel above to the load below. Metal loops closed by a pin link one part to the next. Fabric straps wrap around beams, and steel ropes end in pressed loops. A connection is only as safe as its weakest part. A second steel rope, called a safety cable, catches the load if a part fails. A trained rigger also checks every part for wear and damage before a load goes up.

The flashcards follow the same order, from the safety rules to the spaces overhead and the parts that hold each load. By the end, you will know how equipment is hung and moved overhead, how much weight each part may hold, and who checks it.
