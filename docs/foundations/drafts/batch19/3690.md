Topic Foundations: Rigging Hardware, Loads and Motorized Flying

Before hanging a heavy mirror, you check that the hook can hold it. Crews hang much heavier lights, speakers and scenery over people. So how is heavy equipment held and moved overhead, so that nothing falls on anyone?

A falling load can kill, and a worn or overloaded part can break without warning. So every part has a maximum weight for normal use, set far below the weight that breaks it. That limit is called the working load limit. If a load drops a short way and is then caught, the sudden pull on the rope can be many times its weight. That jolt is called a shock load. Because any load could fall, nobody stands under it while it moves, or until it is secured. That area is called the fall zone.

Loads also need strong places to hang from. Many theaters have a tall space above the stage. Crews reach it by ladders and work from high walkways and side platforms along the walls. At the top, a steel frame holds the pulleys. That frame is called the gridiron.

Long metal pipes hang from steel ropes that run over pulleys on the gridiron and down to a frame of stacked steel weights. When the weights match the load, one person can move the pipe by hand. This is called a counterweight system. If they do not match, the pipe can move fast on its own.

The height where each pipe stops is called the trim height. In theater language, "in" means down and "out" means up. Before a move, the crew calls the direction and waits for a reply. Anyone may say stop, and everything stops at once.

Concerts often hang equipment from the steel beams of a roof. An electric machine that pulls a metal chain lifts a frame of metal tubes, called a truss. That machine is called a chain motor. Where no roof is strong enough, towers that stand on the floor hold the weight. This is called a ground support system.

For each truss, the maker's table, called a load chart, shows how much it may hold. It holds less at one spot than when the weight is spread along it. The parts that join the truss to the roof beam also have rated limits. A fabric strap often wraps around the beam. A metal loop closed by a pin joins the strap to a steel rope. The rope ends in a loop held by a small pressed metal tube. A second steel rope can catch the load if a part fails. A trained rigger checks every part for damage before a load is lifted.

The flashcards explain each part of this chain, from safety to the hardware that holds each load. By the end, you will know how equipment is hung and moved overhead, and how each part is chosen and checked.
