Topic Foundations: Beatmatching, Mixing and Effects

Imagine a dance party where the music never stops. One song ends, and the next is already playing with the same steady beat. The person who chooses and joins the songs is called a disc jockey, or DJ. So how does a DJ join songs without a break, and how do they choose what to play?

The DJ usually has two music players and a mixer. Between the level slider for each player, the mixer has a sideways slider that moves the sound from one song to the other. It is called a crossfader. Many DJs use one box instead, called a DJ controller, which controls laptop software. Others use record players with special control records, which tell the laptop software how the DJ moves each record. That setup is called a digital vinyl system.

While one song plays, the DJ starts the next in headphones only. This is called cueing. The two songs often play at different speeds. A speed control changes the new song until both speeds match. A small push on the record or wheel then lines up the drum hits. Matching the speed and lining up the beats is called beatmatching. Songs are built from sections called phrases, so DJs usually begin the new song at the start of a phrase.

Even when the beats match, two bass lines together make the low frequencies unclear. A common method is to turn the low frequencies down on the old song and up on the new one together. That is called a bass swap. Effects help too. A filter can slowly remove the low or high frequencies. A loop repeats a short part while the next song is made ready.

Much of this work starts before the event. DJs often collect song versions with a simple beat at the start and end, which are easier to mix. Software measures each song's speed and key, so the DJ can choose songs in matching or related keys, which are the sets of notes the songs use. The DJ also marks start points, called cue points.

During the night, reading the crowd means watching who dances and who leaves, then choosing the next song. On a club night, the songs usually grow more energetic as the room fills.

Different kinds of events need different music from the DJ. A wedding set mixes well-known songs from many years, so guests of every age hear something they know. At hip-hop shows and DJ contests, the record itself becomes an instrument. DJs move it back and forth by hand while the crossfader switches the sound on and off. This is called scratching.
