Topic Foundations: Beatmatching, Mixing and Effects

Imagine a dance party where the music never stops. One song ends, and the next is already playing with the same steady beat. The person who chooses and connects the songs is called a disc jockey, or DJ. So how does a DJ connect songs without a break, and how do they choose what to play?

The DJ usually has two music players and a mixer. A sideways slider on the mixer, called a crossfader, moves the sound from one song to the other. Many DJs use one controller box instead of players and a mixer. It runs music software on a laptop. Others use record players with special records that control the software.

While one song plays, the DJ starts the next in headphones only. This is called cueing. The two songs often play at different speeds. A speed control changes the new song until both match. A small push on the record or wheel lines up the drum hits. Matching the speed and lining up the beats is called beatmatching. Songs are built from sections called phrases, so DJs usually start the new song on a new phrase.

Even when the beats match, two bass lines together usually make the low frequencies unclear. A common method turns the low frequencies down on the old song and up on the new one. That is called a bass swap. Effects help too. A filter can slowly remove low or high frequencies, and an echo can repeat the old song's last sound as it ends. A loop repeats a short part while the DJ prepares the next song.

Much of this work starts before the event. DJs often collect song versions with a simple beat at the start and end, which are easier to mix. Software usually measures each song's speed and estimates its key. A key is a song's set of notes. The DJ can then choose songs whose notes sound good together. The DJ also marks start points, called cue points.

During the night, reading the crowd means watching who dances and who leaves, then choosing the next song. At a club, an early DJ often plays calmer songs while people arrive, and later DJs play more energetic songs. The type of event also changes the music. A wedding usually needs well-known songs.

At hip-hop shows and DJ contests, the crowd often expects more than one song following another. There, DJs use the record itself to make new rhythmic sounds. DJs move it back and forth by hand while the crossfader switches the sound on and off. This is called scratching.
