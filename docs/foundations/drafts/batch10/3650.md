Topic Foundations: Beatmatching, Mixing and Effects

Imagine a dance party where the music never stops. One song ends, and the next is already playing with the same steady beat. The person who chooses and joins the songs is called a disc jockey, or DJ. This topic answers one question: how does a DJ join songs without a break, and how do they choose what to play?

The DJ usually has two music players and a mixer. While one song plays, the DJ starts the next in headphones only. Listening to the next song in private is called cueing. Sliders on the mixer then raise or lower each song's volume.

In the headphones, the songs often play at different speeds. A speed control makes the new song faster or slower until both match. Then the DJ gives the record, or a wheel on the player, a small push, so the drum hits happen at the same moment. This is called beatmatching. Software can also do it automatically. Songs are built from sections, each called a phrase, and the next song usually starts at the beginning of one.

Even when the beats match, two bass lines together make the low frequencies unclear. The mixer has knobs for low, middle and high frequencies. A common method turns the low frequencies down on the old song and up on the new one at the same time. That is called a bass swap. Filters, echoes and short repeated parts can make the move from one song to the next less sudden or more exciting. Many DJs also choose songs in musical keys that match or are closely related.

A DJ can also use the record player as an instrument. They move the record back and forth by hand, often while a slider switches the sound on and off. This is called scratching. Performers combine many named movements in practiced routines and contests.

Every mix and performance depends on preparation. DJs collect music. They choose versions with a long, simple beat at the start and end, which are easier to mix. Software measures each song's speed and key, and important points are marked. A spare copy of the music is usually ready.

The DJ watches who is dancing and who is leaving. To read the crowd means to choose the next song from what people do. Across a night, the person who plays first slowly makes the music more exciting, the busiest hours have the most exciting songs, and the end is often calmer. Most DJs keep reliable songs ready in case the dance floor empties.
