Topic Foundations: Beatmatching, Mixing and Effects

Imagine a dance party where the music never stops. One song ends, and the next is already playing with the same steady beat. The person who chooses and joins the songs is called a disc jockey, or DJ. So how does a DJ join songs without a break, and how do they choose what to play?

The DJ usually has two music players and a mixer. Each player has its own level slider on the mixer. Below them, a sideways slider called a crossfader moves the sound from one song to the other. Many DJs use one box instead, which controls laptop music software. Others use record players with special records that tell the software how each record moves.

While one song plays, the DJ starts the next in headphones only. This is called cueing. The two songs often play at different speeds. A speed control changes the new song until both speeds match. A small push on the record or wheel then lines up the drum hits. Matching the speed and lining up the beats is called beatmatching. Songs are built from sections called phrases, so DJs usually begin the new song at the start of a phrase.

Even when the beats match, two bass lines together make the low frequencies unclear. A common method turns the low frequencies down on the old song and up on the new one together. That is called a bass swap. Effects help too. A filter can slowly remove low or high frequencies, and an echo can repeat the old song's last sound as it ends. A loop repeats a short part while the next song is made ready.

Much of this work starts before the event. DJs often collect song versions with a simple beat at the start and end, which are easier to mix. Software measures each song's speed and key. A key is a song's set of notes. The DJ can then choose songs in matching or related keys. The DJ also marks start points, called cue points.

During the night, reading the crowd means watching who dances and who leaves, then choosing the next song. At a club, an early DJ often plays calmer songs while people arrive, and later DJs play more energetic songs. The type of event also changes the music. A wedding set mixes well-known songs from many years.

Some events ask for another way of playing. At hip-hop shows and DJ contests, DJs often use the record itself to make new rhythmic sounds. DJs move it back and forth by hand while the crossfader switches the sound on and off. This is called scratching.
