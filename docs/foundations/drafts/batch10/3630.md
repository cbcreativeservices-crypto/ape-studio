Topic Foundations: Concert-Audio Slang

Imagine standing backstage while a concert crew prepares a show. The stage is loud, everyone is busy, and people speak in very short phrases. A short phrase is quick to say and easy to hear over noise or on a radio. This topic answers one question: what do the crew's words mean, so that you can understand the conversation around you?

Most of these words are names for equipment. Headphones are called cans. The speaker cabinets are called boxes. Small clip-on microphones, the battery packs worn on the body, and the mixing console all have short names too. Some common microphones are known only by their model number. All the equipment set up for a show is called the rig.

Before the show, each part of the rig is tested. "Check one two" is what a person says into a microphone to test it and to prove that it reaches the right channel. When a position is finished, with cables tied and covers back on, the crew says to "button it up". Short spoken reports then tell everyone that each team is ready.

The places where this equipment stands have names as well. Crews often add the word "world" to an area's name. The area where the amplifier racks stand is amp world. The name also tells everyone which person is responsible for a fault there. The mixing position during the show is called the hot seat, because everything done there happens live and cannot be undone.

At that position, much of the talk is about level, and many requests give no number. "Bring it up" means turn something up, and "back it off" means turn it down a little. You change the level until the person who asked is satisfied. Other phrases warn that a level is too high. When the meters stay in their red top section for most of the show, crews say the system is "living in the red". There is no extra level left for a loud moment.

During the show, other phrases describe trouble. The most serious report is "we are dead in the water". It means the system has failed completely, and nothing is ready to replace it. "The show must go on" means the crew finds a temporary solution and repairs the fault later. It never means continuing when someone could get hurt. Trouble can also come from the crew's own ears. After hours of loud sound, ears become less sensitive. A rest in a quiet place, called an ear break, helps.

The flashcards explain every phrase in this language. They cover the names of equipment, the checks before a show, the names of areas, requests about level, the words for trouble, tired hearing, and the people on a crew. By the end, you will know what the crew means when it speaks, and what each phrase asks for.
