Topic Foundations: Concert-Audio Slang

Imagine your first day working in a busy restaurant kitchen. The cooks call out short words that you do not know yet. A concert crew also has its own short words, which are quick to say and easy to hear over loud sound or on a radio. So what do the crew's words mean, so you can understand the conversation around you?

Many of these words name equipment. Headphones are called cans. Speaker cabinets are called boxes. Clip-on microphones, body-worn battery packs and the mixing console have short names too. Some common microphones are often called by their model number. All the equipment set up for a show is called the rig.

Before the show, each part of the rig is tested. "Check one two" is what a person says into a microphone to test it and prove it reaches the right channel. When a position is finished and tidy, the crew says to "button it up".

The places where equipment stands have names too. Crews often add "world" to an area's name. The area where the amplifier racks stand is amp world. The mixing position during the show is called the hot seat, because everything done there is live and cannot be undone.

That position is not always good for listening. Under a balcony or inside a small room, the sound can differ from what the audience hears. Crews call this mixing in a hole. A seat where the sound is poor, however good the mix is, is called a bad seat. Tired ears can mislead too, because hours of loud sound make them less sensitive. A rest in a quiet place, called an ear break, helps.

Much of the talk there is about level, often with no number. "Bring it up" means turn something up, and "back it off" means turn it down a little. When the meters stay in their red section most of the show, the system is "living in the red", with no level left for a loud moment. Other phrases judge the result. "That plays" means a result is good enough to keep, and "that does not play" means it has to change. "White glove" means the highest level of care and finish.

Trouble during the show has its own phrases. The most serious report is "we are dead in the water". It means the system has failed, with nothing ready to replace it. "The show must go on" means the crew finds a temporary solution and repairs the fault later. It never means continuing when someone could get hurt. Someone called in at the last minute to fix a failing show is called a fireman.

The flashcards explain each phrase, and also the words for people on a crew and in the audience. By the end, you will know what the crew means when it speaks, and what each phrase asks for.
