Topic Foundations: Monitor Engineering

Imagine a band on a large stage. The big speakers point at the audience, away from the band. The drums are loud, but the singer can hardly hear her own voice, so she cannot tell if she is in tune. Each performer needs to hear something different. So how does each person on stage hear what they need?

The oldest answer is a slanted speaker on the stage floor, tilted up toward one performer's ears. That speaker is called a wedge. A drummer often has a larger speaker beside the drums, to feel the kick drum as well as hear it. Speakers at the sides of the stage help players who move.

But every wedge points at a microphone. The microphone picks up the wedge, and the wedge plays that sound louder. This repeats again and again and becomes a loud, high tone. That is called acoustic feedback. Before the show, an engineer raises each wedge until that tone starts, then lowers that frequency with an equalizer. That process is called ringing out. Loud wedges also make the stage itself louder, and the microphones pick up that sound too.

Many performers avoid this with an earpiece that plays their mix directly into the ear. It is called an in-ear monitor. It seals the ear, so the performer can usually listen more quietly. But a sealed ear can still receive very loud sound. A device called a limiter stops sudden loud peaks, but the volume must stay low. Microphones pointed at the audience are often added, so the performer still hears the audience and the hall.

With wedges or with earpieces, every performer needs their own mix. On larger shows, a device called a splitter often copies each microphone signal to a separate console for the stage. Each performer gets their own output, and a level control on each channel, called a send, sets how loud it is in that mix. The sends are usually independent of the main faders, so changes for the audience do not change the stage sound. A singer usually wants more of their own voice. A drummer may want the click, a steady beat that keeps everyone at the same speed.

During the show, those needs change. A musician who wants more of their own sound asks for "more-me", often with an agreed hand signal. The engineer therefore needs a clear view of the stage. The engineer answers through a microphone only the performers hear. On some stages, performers make the change themselves, on a phone or on a small box of their own. That small box is called a personal mixer.

The flashcards explain each part in detail: the speakers, earpieces, mixes and stage communication. By the end, you will know how each performer gets a mix that helps them play well, while the stage stays free of feedback.
