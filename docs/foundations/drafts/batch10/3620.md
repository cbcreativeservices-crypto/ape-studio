Topic Foundations: Monitor Engineering

Imagine singing in a car while the music is very loud. It is hard to hear your own voice, so it is hard to tell if you are in tune. Performers on a large stage have the same problem. The big speakers point at the audience, away from the band, and the drums are loud. Each performer also needs to hear something different. So how does each person on stage hear what they need?

One common answer is a slanted speaker on the stage floor, tilted up toward one performer's ears. That speaker is called a wedge. A drummer often has a larger speaker, to feel the kick drum as well as hear it. Speakers at the sides of the stage help players who move.

But a wedge usually points toward a microphone too. The microphone picks up the sound from the wedge, and the wedge plays that sound louder. The loop grows into a loud, continuous tone. That is called acoustic feedback, or howlround. Before the show, an engineer raises each wedge until that tone starts, then turns down just that pitch with an equalizer. That process is called ringing out.

Loud wedges also make the whole stage louder, so many performers use an earpiece that plays their mix into the ear. It is called an in-ear monitor. It seals the ear, so the performer can usually listen more quietly. But a sealed ear can still receive very loud sound. A device called a monitor limiter reduces sudden loud peaks before they reach the ear, but the volume must still stay low.

With wedges or earpieces, each performer usually needs their own mix. A singer often wants more of their own voice. A drummer may want the click, a steady beat that keeps everyone at the same speed. On larger shows, each microphone signal is often split. One copy goes to the mixing desk for the audience, and another goes to a second mixing desk near the stage that makes one mix for each performer. On each channel, a level control called a monitor send sets how loud that channel is in one mix. Changes for the audience usually do not change these stage mixes.

During the show, those needs change. A musician who wants more of their own sound asks for "more-me", often with an agreed hand signal. So the engineer needs a clear view of the stage, and answers through a microphone that only the performers hear. Some performers make the change themselves, on a phone or a small box of their own. That small box is called a personal mixer.

The flashcards explain each part in detail, from speakers and earpieces to separate mixes and stage communication. By the end, you will know how each performer gets a mix that helps them play well, and how the engineer works to prevent feedback.
