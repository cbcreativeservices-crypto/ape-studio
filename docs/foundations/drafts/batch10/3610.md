Topic Foundations: Front of House Mixing Workflow

Imagine you are in the audience at a theater show. A few rows behind you, a sound engineer stands at a large mixing desk, also called a console. There, they balance every voice and instrument for the audience. That position, and the main mix made there, is called front of house, or FOH. How does this engineer prepare, so the mix works from the first song to the last?

The work usually starts with a list of every microphone and instrument on stage. Each one plugs into a numbered stage input, and the desk connects each input to a channel. That plan is called the stage patch, and each channel gets a clear name. The same signals are often copied to other desks too.

Next, the crew makes sure that each input works. That test is called the line check. A crew member speaks into each microphone in turn, and each must reach the right channel. The crew also checks microphone power and cables with reversed wires.

A working input does not yet give a good sound. That comes in the soundcheck. Many engineers follow a common order for each input. First they set its input level. Then they remove unneeded low frequencies. Next they adjust its low, middle and high frequencies. Effects usually come last.

Then the band plays real songs at show volume, often the loudest and the quietest, because each shows different problems. Some engineers record every channel and later play it back through the desk without the band. That is called a virtual soundcheck. A digital desk saves its whole setup in one file, called a show file. Engineers keep the last version they know works. On large shows, a second desk can take over if the first one fails.

When the show begins, many engineers raise the channels from silence, starting with a main part such as the lead voice. A room full of people absorbs some of the sound, so the first song often needs quick corrections. The engineer then moves the volume sliders, called faders, by hand. They turn each solo up when it starts and down when it ends, and mute unused microphones. The meters help, but the final decisions come from listening.

At a festival, these steps happen much faster, because many bands share one stage and often one desk. So the stage patch usually stays the same for every band, and nobody re-plugs channels without a reason. That habit is called festival patch discipline. Each band's file is loaded quickly, and channels are muted before any cable is moved. A visiting engineer can also use the venue's desk without losing its settings.

The flashcards explain each of these steps in detail. By the end, you will know how an engineer prepares, tests and saves every input, and how the mix stays clear through a whole show.
