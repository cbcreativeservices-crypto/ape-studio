Topic Foundations: Front of House Mixing Workflow

Imagine a concert. In the middle of the audience, a sound engineer stands at a large mixing desk, also called a console. From there, they control the balance of every voice and instrument. That place, and the mix made there, is called the front of house. How does this engineer prepare, so the mix works from the first song to the last?

The work starts with a list of every microphone and instrument on stage. Each one plugs into a numbered socket on the stage. The desk connects each socket to a channel. That plan is called the stage patch, and each channel gets a clear name. Often the same signals are also copied to other desks, for the musicians' own speakers or for a recording.

Next, each input must be proven to work. That test is called the line check. A crew member taps each microphone, one at a time. The crew also checks power for the microphones that need it, and cables with reversed wires. Any problem is fixed immediately.

A working input does not yet give a good sound. That comes in the soundcheck. The engineer sets each input level, removes low frequencies that a source does not need, adjusts the low, middle and high frequencies, and adds effects last. Then the band plays real songs at the true show volume, often the loudest song and the quietest song, because each shows different problems. Some engineers record every channel, to adjust the mix later without the band.

A digital desk saves its whole setup in one file, called a show file. It also holds settings for each song, which load instantly. Engineers save after the soundcheck and keep the last version they know works. For large shows, a second desk copies the first and can replace it if it fails.

When the show begins, the engineer often raises the channels from silence, starting with one main part, such as the lead voice. A room full of people absorbs some of the sound and changes it, so the first song needs fast corrections. Then the engineer moves the volume sliders, called faders, by hand, raises each solo in time, and mutes unused microphones. The level meters on the desk help, but the final decisions come from listening.

At a festival, many bands share one stage and often one desk, so the stage patch stays the same for every band. That rule of never changing the patch is called festival patch discipline. Each band's file is loaded quickly, and channels are muted before any cable is moved. A visiting engineer can use the hall's own desk while the hall's own settings stay safe.

The flashcards explain each of these steps in detail. By the end, you will know how an engineer prepares, tests and saves every input, and how the mix stays clear through a whole show.
