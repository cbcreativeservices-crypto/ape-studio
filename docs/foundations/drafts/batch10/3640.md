Topic Foundations: Road Crew

Imagine a concert in the evening. Instruments fill the stage, and speakers and lights hang above it. That morning, the same building was an empty room. Everything arrived in trucks, and it will leave again in the night. So how does a team bring a whole show into a building, operate it, and take it away again, safely and on time?

The answer starts with the people the audience does not see. They work in separate departments, such as sound, lighting, video and staging. Some travel with the show from city to city. Others work for the building or are hired for one day. Each small team has a leader who gives out the jobs, keeps the team on schedule, and checks that the work is safe. That leader is called the crew chief.

Inside each department, people often have very specific jobs. Some hang heavy equipment safely above the stage. Some set up the main speaker system. Some look after the wireless microphones. A person who looks after the band's own instruments and amplifiers is called a backline tech, short for technician.

All these people follow one timed plan for the day. Getting all the equipment off the trucks and into the building is called the load-in. Then the stage is built and tested before the audience enters. When several bands play one show, the crew switches from one band's equipment to the next. That switch is called a changeover, and it has a strict time limit. After the show, everything is taken apart and goes back onto the trucks.

Fast packing at night depends on order during the day. The equipment travels in hard cases with wheels and foam inside. Crews pack the trucks in a set order, so the cases needed first come off first. Empty cases, lids and spare equipment go to one storage area until the end of the night. That area is called the boneyard. It keeps walkways and fire exits clear.

Cables need the same care as cases. The crew lays them along planned routes and tapes them down, so nobody trips. Backstage is often dark and noisy, so the crew uses short spoken calls. One call warns everyone that something is falling from above. On the radio, people say "copy that" to mean "I heard you and I understand". It does not mean the task will be done. Outdoors, the weather is a danger too. Heat, cold and storms can stop the work, so the crew watches the forecast.

The flashcards explain each part in detail: the jobs and departments, the plan of a show day, trucks and cases, cable routes, the spoken calls, and work in difficult weather. By the end, you will know who does what on a show day, and how a show is brought into a building, performed, and taken out again.
