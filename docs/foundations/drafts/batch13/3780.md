Topic Foundations: Passive Components

Imagine opening an old guitar amplifier. Inside is a board covered with parts: small cylinders with colored stripes, round metal cases, coils of copper wire, and tiny glass parts. They cannot make a signal larger. They only reduce, store, or direct energy. A part like this is called a passive component. What does each one do, and how can you tell a good part from an old, failing one?

The striped cylinders reduce the flow of electric current and turn some of its energy into heat. Each one is called a resistor. Its material decides how it behaves. A thin metal coating gives an accurate value and adds little noise. Wire wound on a ceramic rod handles more heat. Each resistor also has a marked value, an allowed error from that value, and limits for voltage, power and heat.

The round metal cases do a different job. Each one stores electric charge on two metal layers with an insulating layer between them. This part is called a capacitor. Many capacitors have a plus end and a minus end, and must be connected the correct way, because one connected backwards can burst. A capacitor passes changing signals, such as music, but blocks a steady voltage. The insulating layer is called the dielectric, and it decides how the part behaves. Plastic film keeps its value steady over time. A very thin oxide layer stores the most charge for its size.

Each capacitor has a small built-in resistance that turns some current into heat. In the oxide type, this resistance often rises as its liquid slowly dries out. An old capacitor can leak fluid, or its flat top can swell into a dome shape.

Unlike a capacitor, a coil of wire stores energy in a magnetic field. This part is called an inductor. It resists sudden changes in current, so it reduces high frequencies more than low ones. A core of iron or ferrite, a magnetic ceramic, gives more effect from a smaller coil. With too much current, the core holds all the magnetism it can, and the coil suddenly loses much of its effect.

Some glass parts let current flow in one direction only. Each one is called a diode. Diodes help change power from a wall socket into one-direction power for a circuit. Some give off light. Others carry sudden short jumps in voltage away from chips. Other parts change their resistance with heat or light. Materials also matter. Some metals block magnetic hum, some crystals turn pressure into voltage, and a quartz crystal vibrates at one exact rate, so it keeps accurate time.

The flashcards cover resistors and their jobs, values and ratings, capacitors and how they fail, coils and magnetism, diodes and protection parts, and the materials behind them. By the end, you will know what each part does and why a designer chooses one type over another.
