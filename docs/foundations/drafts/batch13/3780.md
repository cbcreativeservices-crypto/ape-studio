Topic Foundations: Passive Components

Imagine opening a guitar amplifier. Inside is a board covered with parts: small cylinders with colored stripes, round metal cases, coils of copper wire, and tiny glass parts. They cannot add power to a signal. They only reduce, store, or direct energy. A part like this is called a passive component. What does each one do, and what makes one type better than another for a job?

The striped cylinders reduce the flow of electric current and turn some of its energy into heat. Each one is called a resistor. Its real value can differ from its marked value by a small allowed amount. It also has a safe heat limit. Resistors are made from carbon, metal film, or wound wire, and each kind adds a different amount of noise.

The round metal cases do a different job. Each one stores electric charge on two metal layers with an insulating layer between them. This part is called a capacitor. It passes changing signals, such as music, but blocks a steady voltage. Its size is measured in farads. Most audio capacitors use much smaller units, such as the microfarad and the picofarad.

The insulating layer is called the dielectric. Its material sets much of how the part behaves. Plastic film keeps its value steady over time. An electrolytic capacitor uses a very thin oxide layer and a liquid inside, so it stores a large charge for its size. Most have a plus end and a minus end, and one connected backwards can burst. With age, the liquid can dry out, and an old one can leak or swell. Many capacitors and resistors are also named for their job. One capacitor may pass a signal between two parts of a circuit. Another may keep a supply voltage steady.

A coil of wire stores energy in a magnetic field instead. This part is called an inductor. It resists sudden changes in current, so it reduces high frequencies more than low ones. Its size is measured in henrys. With too much current, an iron core inside the coil cannot hold more magnetism, and the coil loses much of its effect.

The tiny glass parts let current flow mainly in one direction. Each one is called a diode. Diodes help turn wall power into one-way current. Some diodes give off light. Others let current flow backwards above one exact voltage, which can hold a voltage steady. Some protect chips from short, sudden rises in voltage, and a related metal-oxide part does this too.

Some parts change their resistance on purpose, with heat or light. A quartz crystal vibrates at a very steady rate, so circuits use it to keep accurate time.

The flashcards explain each of these parts, with their units and ratings. By the end, you will know what each passive part does, how it can fail, and why a designer chooses one type.
