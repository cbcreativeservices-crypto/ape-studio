Topic Foundations: Electromechanical Parts and Hardware

Imagine sitting at a mixing console. You turn a knob, and a voice gets louder. Inside, small parts move, touch, or use magnetism to change the electrical signal. What are these parts, and what does each one do?

Behind the knob is a strip of material that resists electricity. A small metal contact slides along the strip as you turn, and its position sets how much signal goes through. This part is called a potentiometer. Our ears do not hear changes in loudness evenly, so a volume control changes the level only a little at first. The shape of that change is called the taper.

Many jobs only need on or off, or a choice between two paths. A switch does this with metal contacts that touch or separate. Contacts can bounce for a moment when they close, and they can spark when they open. Some knobs on digital equipment turn without stopping and send counted steps to a chip. Such a knob is called an encoder.

A switch needs a hand to move it. Sometimes the circuit must switch itself, for example to keep the speakers silent while an amplifier turns on. A small current flows through a coil, the coil becomes a magnet, and the magnet pulls the contacts together. This part is called a relay.

Magnetism can also carry the signal itself. Two coils of wire are wound on a shared iron core, with no wire joining them. The changing signal in one coil creates the signal in the other. This part is called an audio transformer. Because the coils do not touch, it keeps the wiring of two connected devices separate, which can reduce hum. The number of wire loops on each coil sets how much the voltage rises or falls. The comparison between the two numbers of loops is called the turns ratio.

Transformers also have a second job. A larger one lowers the voltage from the wall socket to what the equipment needs. If too much current flows, a fuse breaks the circuit. Portable equipment often gets its power from a battery instead of the wall socket.

The signal level often needs to be lowered or shown to the user. A few resistors can lower the level by one fixed amount. This group is called an attenuator pad. A needle meter or a row of small lights shows how high the level is. All of these parts are held in place by hardware. In larger equipment, circuit boards often slide into a frame and plug into a shared board at the back. Metal plates called heat sinks carry heat away from hot parts.

The flashcards explain each of these parts in detail. By the end, you will know what each part inside audio equipment does, and how these parts move, switch, or use magnetism to control the signal and the power.
