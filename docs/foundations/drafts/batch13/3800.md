Topic Foundations: Electromechanical Parts & Hardware

Imagine sitting at a mixing console. You turn a knob, and a voice gets louder. You press a button, and a small light turns on. Inside, small parts move, touch, or use magnetism to change the electrical signal. What are these parts, and what does each one do?

Start with the knob. Behind it is a strip of material that resists electricity. A small metal contact slides along the strip as you turn, and its position sets how much signal goes through. This part is called a potentiometer. Our ears do not hear changes in loudness evenly, so a volume control changes the level only a little at first. The shape of that change is called the taper. A slider on a mixer works the same way, in a straight line.

Many jobs only need on or off, or a choice between two paths. A switch does this with metal contacts that touch or separate. Some switches stay where you leave them, and others spring back. Contacts can bounce for a moment or spark when they open, so designers add parts to control this. Some knobs on digital equipment turn all the way around without stopping and only send counted steps to a computer chip. Such a knob is called an encoder.

A switch needs a hand to move it. Sometimes the circuit must switch itself, for example to keep the speakers silent while an amplifier turns on. A small current flows through a coil, the coil becomes a magnet, and the magnet pulls the contacts together. This part is called a relay.

Magnetism can also carry the signal itself. Two coils of wire are wound on a shared iron core, with no wire joining them. The changing signal in one coil creates the signal in the other. This part is called an audio transformer. Because the coils do not touch, it can block hum and keep the dangerous voltage from the wall socket away from the user. How much the voltage rises or falls depends on how the number of wire loops on one coil compares with the other. That comparison is called the turns ratio.

These parts all need safe power. Transformers also lower the voltage from the wall socket to what equipment needs. A fuse breaks the circuit and stops the current if too much flows. Not every level change needs a moving part. A few resistors can lower the level by one fixed amount, and a needle meter or a row of small lights shows how high the signal level is.

The flashcards cover knobs and their curves, switches and relays, transformers, fixed level reducers, fuses and batteries, meters and displays, and how large equipment is built from plug-in boards. By the end, you will know what each part inside audio equipment does, and why one replacement part fits where another does not.
