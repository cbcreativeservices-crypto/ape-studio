Topic Foundations: Circuits & Topologies

Imagine following a voice through a small mixer, from its input to its output. It passes through hundreds of small parts. What matters is how they are connected. The pattern of those connections is called the topology. This topic asks how connected parts become a working audio circuit.

The answer starts with rules that are true in every circuit. For the same voltage, a larger resistance lets less current flow. That rule is called Ohm's law. All the current that flows into a connection point must also flow out. Capacitors and coils also oppose an audio signal, but the amount changes with frequency. That opposition is called reactance. These rules decide the voltage and current at every point in a circuit.

The same rules explain a stage, a small group of parts that does one job, usually around an amplifier chip. Most stages send part of the output back to the input, so the chip can correct its own errors. That path is called a feedback loop. Two resistors in the loop set how much bigger the signal becomes. Other stages let a weak source send its signal through a long cable without losing level. If the feedback arrives too late, a stage can produce its own unwanted signal, often too high to hear.

Stages also send signals to other devices, and a long cable collects hum, a low buzz from nearby power wiring. So professional equipment often uses two matched wires. The hum usually appears equally on both wires. That shared part is called common-mode noise. The receiving stage uses only the difference between the wires, so the shared noise cancels.

Inside a device, stages also change and combine the sound. Resistors and capacitors around an amplifier make filters, which pass some frequencies and reduce others. Feedback that adds to the input on purpose makes an oscillator, which produces a steady tone. In a mixer, every channel sends its signal to one shared path where the signals are added. That path is called a summing bus.

All of these stages need steady power. Power from a wall socket changes direction many times every second. Diodes make it flow in one direction, large capacitors keep the voltage from falling between the one-direction pulses, and a regulator holds it steady. Many audio circuits need a positive and a negative supply, so the signal can move above and below zero. Some supplies switch the power on and off very fast. They waste less energy as heat, but their noise must be kept out of the audio.

The flashcards follow the same order, from circuit laws to power supplies, and also explain how circuits are drawn. By the end, you will know how parts connect into stages and stages into a device, and why each stage behaves as it does.
