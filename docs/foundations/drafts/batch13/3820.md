Topic Foundations: Manufacturing, Assembly and Production

Imagine an engineer who has just finished a new audio interface. The engineer designs its board once, but a factory may build it thousands of times. So how does a design become a product that a factory can build, check and sell?

The process starts with a first version built by hand. An engineer often uses a plastic board full of holes, with metal clips inside. That board is called a breadboard. Parts are pushed into its holes, so a circuit can be tested without solder, the metal that is melted to join parts. A hand-built circuit is easy to change, but slow to build, and each copy is slightly different.

So a factory product usually uses a printed circuit board. It is a stiff sheet of glass fiber and plastic, with thin copper lines as its wiring. Many boards stack several copper layers. A tiny hole lined with metal carries a signal from one layer to another. That hole is called a via.

To make it, the board factory works from exact files. The engineer draws the circuit in design software and gives each part a set of flat copper areas that fits it. The software checks that no copper lines are too thin or too close. Then it writes the files the factories need: one for each copper layer, one that shows where each part goes, and a list of every part to buy. Designers often prefer parts that a second company also makes, in case one part stops being made.

Those parts come in standard cases. Some have wire legs that go through holes and are soldered on the other side. This older method is called through-hole. Most modern parts sit flat on the surface instead, so equipment can be smaller. Chips may have pins along their edges or contacts on their underside.

Assembly puts the parts on the board. Solder paste is spread through a thin metal sheet with holes, so it lands only on those copper areas. A machine places each part. Then the board passes through an oven, where the melted solder forms every joint at once. That process is called reflow soldering.

Finished boards are then checked, often every one, sometimes a sample from each batch. Cameras compare each board with a correct picture, and pins touch test spots to measure the connections. The percentage of boards that pass is called yield. Before sale, the product is tested for electrical safety and for the electrical noise it sends out. In many countries, it must pass these tests and a check for harmful materials before it can be sold.

The flashcards explain each step in detail, from the first test board to the final checks. By the end, you will know how audio equipment goes from a first test circuit to a product that a factory can build the same way every time.
