Topic Foundations: Preamp and Converter Engineering

Imagine a singer in a studio. Before her voice can be stored on a computer, it must be made larger and then turned into numbers. Any noise or error added at this first step stays in the recording. So how is the first part of the signal path designed to add as little as possible?

The part that makes the signal larger is called a preamplifier. Some designs bring the signal in through a transformer, a part made of coils of wire, which separates the devices electrically and rejects cable noise. Others use electronic circuits alone. Some designs, often with tubes, add a small amount of distortion on purpose, and engineers choose them for that.

The input also affects the microphone. If the input's impedance, its resistance to the signal, is too low, the level drops and the balance of low and high frequencies can change. This is called microphone loading. So inputs are usually much higher in impedance than the microphone. Switches can add a pad that lowers a loud signal, send power to microphones, or remove very low frequencies.

Even a good preamplifier adds a faint hiss. Heat in every resistor makes a small noise, which sets the lowest possible noise. To compare preamplifiers fairly, engineers measure the noise and remove the effect of the gain, how much the signal was made larger. The result is called equivalent input noise. A lower number means a quieter preamplifier.

Next, the clean signal is turned into numbers. First, a filter removes frequencies too high to capture correctly. Then, many times each second, a circuit holds the voltage still while it is measured. Most modern converters measure much more often than needed, then move most of their error above hearing, where a filter removes it. This design is called a delta-sigma converter. Playback reverses the process.

Every measurement happens on a timing pulse from a clock circuit. If the pulses are uneven, some measurements come slightly early or late. This unevenness is called clock jitter, and it adds small errors. When several devices work together, one usually sends the clock to the others. Specifications describe how small the errors are. One gives the range between the loudest clean signal and the noise. Another shows how many digital bits really carry clean audio.

A recording interface often holds preamplifiers and converters in one box. Many also have their own processing chips, which can run effects with very little delay, so performers hear themselves with effects while they record. Some can correct the sound of monitor speakers. Some can even change their real input circuit to copy the behavior of an older preamplifier.

The flashcards cover preamplifier designs and switches, converters, clocks, and modern interfaces. By the end, you will know what happens to a signal at the first part of its path, and what the numbers that describe it mean.
