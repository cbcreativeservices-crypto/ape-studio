Topic Foundations: Racks, Patchbays and Wiring Infrastructure

Imagine the control room of a recording studio. Cables inside the walls link it to sockets in the next room, where microphones plug in. This topic asks where equipment is installed, and how each signal reaches its place so the next technician can follow it.

Equipment is usually installed in a rack, a metal frame with upright strips full of holes. Most professional units share one width and screw to those strips. Height is counted in equal steps, and one step is called a rack unit. Racks can be open frames, closed cabinets, or cases on wheels. Hand-operated equipment, such as the mixer, usually sits on desks and stands.

Equipment in a rack creates heat. Cool air usually enters at the front and leaves at the back. A plain cover, called a blanking panel, fills each empty space so hot air cannot return to the front. The units usually connect to one shared ground point, for safety and lower noise. Audio cables are kept away from power cables to reduce hum.

Even installed units need their connections changed, and reaching behind a rack is slow. So the inputs and outputs of many units are wired to rows of sockets on one front panel, called a patch bay. Short cables on the front connect any output to any input. Usually each upper socket is connected inside to the one below. That built-in default connection is called normalling. Inserting a plug usually interrupts it. On some, a plug in the upper socket only takes a copy, and the default connection still works.

Signals also travel between rooms. A built-in cable linking two fixed points is called a tie line. One microphone may need to feed two rooms. A device that copies one signal to several outputs is called a splitter. Many use small transformers, parts that pass the signal with no direct wire link, so the systems stay electrically separate, with less hum. On stage, many inputs often share one box, called a stage box.

Instruments must also reach the wall sockets. A bass or keyboard gives a signal that weakens and picks up noise on long cables. A small box changes it into the kind of signal a microphone sends. It is called a direct box. A passive one needs no power, and an active one does.

In large buildings, many wires end on plastic blocks, each wire pressed into a metal slot. Cables lie in metal trays, hang on hooks, or pass through protective pipes. They end at plates in walls and boxes in floors, where equipment plugs in. Labels let the next technician follow each path.

The flashcards explain each part, from racks to the cables inside a building. By the end, you will know where audio equipment is installed, how signals reach each destination, and how a system is built so others can work in it.
