Topic Foundations: Clocking, Redundancy and Network Management

Imagine a live television broadcast where sound passes through several digital devices. Digital sound is a long row of measurements, taken many thousands of times every second. A small clock inside each device sets when each measurement happens. But no two clocks run at exactly the same speed. So how do many devices keep the same time?

When two clocks run at slightly different speeds, the timing of their measurements slowly becomes different. The receiving device then has to skip or repeat a measurement, and this usually makes a click. That is called a clock slip. To prevent this, one device keeps the time, and all the others follow it.

In a studio, the leading device often sends timing pulses through a separate cable. This timing signal has one pulse for every measurement, and it is called word clock. The last device in the chain needs a small part that absorbs the pulse, or part of each pulse travels back and disturbs the timing. A large studio may use one shared timer for every room. The audio clock often follows the video timing signal, so sound and picture stay together.

On an audio network, the timing travels in the same cables as the sound. Devices send timing messages that describe the quality of their clocks. A fixed set of rules then selects the best clock automatically. That device is called the grandmaster. Inside each follower, a circuit keeps correcting its own speed to match the leader. The leader can follow the world time standard, which comes from atomic clocks and often reaches devices through satellites.

But a cable, or the box that connects network cables, can still fail during a show. So many systems connect each device to two separate networks, and the same sound travels on both at once. If one fails, the sound still arrives on the other without a gap. This is called a redundant network. A backup clock can also become the leader if the first leader stops, though the sound may stop for a moment.

Backup paths only help if someone knows they are working. Many devices show a light while they follow the shared clock. A tool that shows this for every device, and records every time a device loses the clock, is called a sync monitor. A management computer can also read the status and settings of every network device. A common set of rules for this is called the simple network management protocol. Engineers usually check all of this before the work starts.

The flashcards explain each part, from the clock inside one device to network leaders, backup networks and the tools that report problems. By the end, you will know how digital devices keep the same time, how a system keeps working when one part fails, and how faults are found early.
