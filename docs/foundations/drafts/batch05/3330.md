Topic Foundations: Clocking, Redundancy and Network Management

Imagine a recording session where the sound passes through several digital devices. Each device measures the sound many thousands of times every second. A small clock inside it decides when each measurement happens. But no two clocks run at exactly the same speed. So how do many devices keep the same time?

When two clocks run at slightly different speeds, the timing of their measurements slowly becomes different. The receiving device then has to skip or repeat a measurement, and this usually makes a click. That is called a clock slip. The usual solution is simple. One device keeps the time, and all the others follow it.

In a studio, the leading device often sends timing pulses through a separate cable. This timing signal has one pulse for every measurement, and it is called word clock. The last device on a cable needs a matching end plug, so the signal does not reflect back. One shared timer for a whole building is called the house clock. The audio clock is often set to follow the video timing signal, so sound and picture stay together.

On an audio network, the timing travels in the same cables as the sound. Devices send timing messages to each other. They compare the quality of their clocks and choose the best one automatically. That device is called the grandmaster. Inside each follower, a circuit keeps correcting its own speed to match the leader. The leader itself can follow time from satellites or atomic clocks.

A shared clock solves one problem, but a cable, or the box that connects network cables, can still fail during a show. So many systems connect each device to two separate networks, and the same sound travels on both at once. If one fails, the sound is already arriving on the other, so nobody hears a gap. This is called a redundant network. A backup clock can also become the leader if the first leader stops, though the sound may stop for a moment.

Backup paths only help if someone knows they are working. A tool that shows whether each device is following the shared clock, and records every time a device loses it, is called a sync monitor. Engineers usually check all of this before the audience arrives.
