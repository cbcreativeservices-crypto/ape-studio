Topic Foundations: Cables and Wiring

Imagine a band setting up for a show. Everything connects to the mixer with cables. A cable's parts affect what reaches the other end. So this topic asks: how is the right cable chosen and placed, so the signal arrives without added noise and nobody gets hurt?

Inside a cable are metal wires, usually copper, that carry the current. Each is one solid wire or many thin strands that bend more easily. A wire's thickness is given as a number, which is called the gauge. A smaller number means a thicker wire that carries more current. The outer covering, called the jacket, protects the wires. Its material decides, for fire safety, where the cable may be installed. A cut or crushed jacket can expose the wires and cause a shock or a fire, so damaged cables are removed.

A layer of woven metal or foil, called the shield, blocks much electrical noise and radio interference. It does little against hum, a low buzzing sound often caused by magnetic fields from power wiring. So microphone cables add a second method. Two wires carry one signal and are twisted together, so outside noise reaches both almost equally. The input keeps only the difference between the two wires, so most noise cancels. This design is called a balanced cable.

Length also changes the signal. On a long cable, the metal resists the current, so some power becomes heat along the way. The wires also store a small electric charge, which can reduce high frequencies. Where pairs lie side by side, a signal can leak into the next pair, which is called crosstalk.

So each job uses its own cable. An instrument cable has one signal wire inside a shield. It cannot cancel noise the way a balanced cable does. Over distance, it also loses high frequencies, so it is usually kept short. A speaker cable has thick wire for its high-power signal. Outside noise is very small compared with that signal, so it usually has no shield. On a large stage, many shielded pairs share one thick cable, which is called a snake.

Microphone signals can also travel as data. A network cable carries them on twisted pairs of thin wires. Network cables come in numbered categories, and higher ones are tested for higher speeds. Some digital signals use one center wire inside a round shield. Over long distances, glass fiber carries data as pulses of light, which electrical noise cannot affect.

All of these cables need careful handling. Cables are labeled at both ends, coiled without twisting, and covered across walkways so nobody trips. At the plug, a collar takes the pulling force off the wires. It is called strain relief.
