Topic Foundations: Cables and Wiring

Imagine a band setting up for a show. Microphones, guitars, speakers and the mixing desk all connect with cables. The parts inside a cable affect what reaches the other end. So this topic asks: how is the right cable chosen, placed and cared for, so the signal arrives without added noise and nobody gets hurt?

Inside a cable are metal wires, usually copper, that carry the current. A wire's thickness is given as a number, which is called the gauge. A smaller number means a thicker wire that carries more current. The outer covering, called the jacket, protects the wires. Its material also decides, for fire safety, where the cable may be installed.

Wires also pick up noise from power cables and motors. A layer of woven metal or foil around the signal wires blocks much of it. That layer is called the shield. For low hum caused by magnetic fields, microphone cables use a second method. Two matched wires, twisted together, carry one signal, so noise reaches both almost equally. The receiving input keeps only the difference between the two wires, and most noise cancels. This design is called a balanced cable.

Length also changes the signal. The metal resists the current, so some power becomes heat. The wires also store a small charge, which can reduce high frequencies on a long guitar cable. Fast digital signals need a cable that matches the equipment electrically. Otherwise, part of each signal pulse is reflected back and changes its shape.

So each job has its own cable. An instrument cable cannot cancel noise, so it is kept short. A speaker cable carries high power, so it has thick wire and no shield. On a large stage, many microphone signals share one thick cable, which is called a snake.

Those microphone signals can also be sent as data. Network cable carries them on twisted pairs of thin wires, in numbered categories, each tested for a higher data speed. In tight bundles, signals pass between pairs and nearby cables, which limits speed and length. Over long distances, glass fiber carries data as pulses of light, which electrical noise cannot affect.

Every cable must also last, and never hurt anyone. A cut or crushed power cable can cause a shock or a fire, so it is removed from use. Cables across walkways are covered so nobody trips. Where a cable enters a plug, a collar holds the pulling force so it does not reach the thin wires. That collar is called strain relief.

The flashcards explain each part: metals, wire sizes and jackets, shields and noise, long cables, the cable for each job, snakes, network cable and fiber, and safe handling. By the end, you will know how a cable's parts change the signal, and how cables are chosen and cared for.
