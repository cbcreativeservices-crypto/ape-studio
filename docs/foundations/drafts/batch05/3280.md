Topic Foundations: Cables and Wiring

Imagine a band setting up for a show. Everything connects to the mixing desk with cables. A cable's parts affect what reaches the other end. So this topic asks: how is the right cable chosen and placed, so the signal arrives without added noise and nobody gets hurt?

Inside a cable are metal wires that carry the current. A wire's thickness is given as a number, which is called the gauge. A smaller number means a thicker wire that carries more current. The outer covering, called the jacket, protects the wires. Its material decides, for fire safety, where the cable may be installed. A cut or crushed jacket can expose the wires and cause a shock or a fire, so damaged cables are removed.

A layer of woven metal or foil, called the shield, blocks much electrical noise and radio interference. It does little against hum. Hum is a low buzzing sound caused by magnetic fields. So microphone cables also use a second method. Two twisted wires carry one signal, so the noise reaches both almost equally. The input keeps only the difference between the two wires, so most noise cancels. This design is called a balanced cable.

Length also changes the signal. On a long cable, the metal resists the current, so some power becomes heat before it reaches the other end. The wires also store a small electric charge, which can reduce high frequencies. Where pairs lie side by side, a signal can leak into the next pair, which is called crosstalk.

For these reasons, each job uses its own cable. An instrument cable has one signal wire inside a shield. It cannot cancel noise the way a balanced cable does. On a long run, it also loses high frequencies, so it is usually kept short. A speaker cable has thick wire for its high-power signal. Outside noise is tiny next to that signal, so it usually has no shield. On a large stage, many shielded pairs share one thick cable, which is called a snake. At one end, it often splits into single cables.

Microphone signals can also travel as data. A network cable carries them on twisted pairs of thin wires. Network cables come in numbered categories, and higher ones are tested for higher speeds. Some digital signals use one center wire inside a round shield. Over long distances, glass fiber carries data as pulses of light, which electrical noise cannot affect.

Cables are labeled at both ends, coiled without twisting, and covered across walkways so nobody trips. Where a cable enters a plug, a collar takes the pulling force off the wires. That collar is called strain relief.
