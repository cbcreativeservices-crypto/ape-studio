Topic Foundations: Vehicle Audio

Imagine driving with music playing. The tires make a constant noise, and the engine makes a low, steady sound. The cabin is small, and every seat is closer to some speakers than to others. This topic answers one question: how does a sound system work well in a small, noisy space that moves?

The system starts in the dashboard. The main unit there, called the head unit, plays the music. In many new cars, the factory unit is connected to the car's computer network. When it is replaced, adapters often keep the factory wiring and steering wheel buttons working.

A separate amplifier needs much more current than the head unit. A thick wire brings it from the battery, with a fuse near the battery, so a damaged wire is unlikely to start a fire. The car's metal body carries the current back, so the ground wire must be short and bolted to bare metal. A bad ground connection often adds a high tone that rises and falls with the engine speed. That tone is called alternator whine.

The amplifier then powers the speakers. Its input control is set to match the head unit's output level, so the signal is not distorted. That adjustment is called gain matching. Coaxial speakers have a small high-frequency speaker mounted in front of a larger one, so both fit in one hole. Component speakers keep the small speaker separate, so it can be mounted high, near the windshield. The lowest notes come from a subwoofer in its own box. The box can be fully closed, or have an open tube that usually gives more bass in a narrower range.

The cabin also changes the sound. Its small, closed space increases the level of the lowest notes. Because no seat is in the center, the nearest speakers are heard first. A processor delays them, so all the sound arrives together. That is called time alignment. Heavy mats on the doors and floor reduce panel vibration.

The vehicle also makes its own noise, from tires, motors, gears and wind. Some reaches the cabin through the air, and some as vibration through the body. Engineers measure how much noise comes along each path. This is called transfer path analysis. They then reduce the loudest paths first. Some cars use their speakers to cancel road noise.

Boats, aircraft and emergency vehicles have the same noise and small spaces, plus needs of their own. Boat equipment is usually sealed against water and salt. In aircraft, pilots talk through an intercom, and a recorder saves cockpit sound. Emergency vehicles add sirens with different patterns of rising and falling pitch.
