Topic Foundations: Vehicle Audio

Imagine driving on a highway with music playing. The tires make a constant noise, and the motor hums. The inside of the car is a very small space, and every seat is closer to some speakers than to others. All the power comes from the battery and the alternator, a generator turned by the motor. This topic answers one question: how does a sound system work well in a small, noisy space that moves?

The system starts in the dashboard. The main unit there plays the music. That unit is called the head unit. In many new cars, the factory unit is connected to the car's computer network, so replacing it is not simple. Adapters keep the factory wiring and steering wheel buttons working, or change the factory speaker signal into one an amplifier can use.

A separate amplifier needs much more current than the head unit. A thick wire brings it from the battery, with a fuse near the battery, so a damaged wire cannot start a fire. The car's metal body carries the current back, so the ground wire must be short and bolted to bare metal. A bad ground connection often adds a high tone that changes pitch with the speed of the motor. That tone is called alternator whine.

The amplifier then powers the speakers. Its input control is set to match the head unit's output level, so the signal is not distorted. That adjustment is called gain matching. Small speakers for high frequencies are often mounted near the windshield. The lowest notes come from a subwoofer in its own box.

The cabin also changes the sound. Because it is small and closed, it increases the deep bass. Because no seat is in the center, the nearest speakers are heard first. A processor delays them slightly, so all the sound arrives together. That is called time alignment. Heavy mats on the doors and floor stop the metal panels from vibrating.

Car makers study the vehicle's own noise too. Tires, motors, gears and wind all make sound. Some travels through the air, and some as vibration through the body. Measuring each path's share of the noise is called transfer path analysis. Engineers reduce the noise on the loudest paths first. Some cars use their speakers to cancel road noise or to add motor sound.

Boats, aircraft and emergency vehicles face the same question with different noise and dangers. Boat equipment is sealed against water and salt. Pilots talk through an intercom, and a recorder saves the sound in the pilots' cabin. Sirens use different patterns of rising and falling pitch.
