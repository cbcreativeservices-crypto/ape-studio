Topic Foundations: Commercial Audio and Assisted Listening

Imagine walking through a large airport. An announcement plays, and you hear it clearly at every gate. Yet you see no large speakers. A traveler with hearing aids hears the same words in her ears. This is installed audio, a sound system built into a building. It answers one question: how does the sound reach every person in every room clearly?

The first answer is many small speakers instead of a few loud ones. Everyone is close to a speaker, so the level is more even. This is called a distributed system. One amplifier feeds the speakers over long, thin wires at a high voltage, so little current flows. A small transformer at each speaker lowers the voltage again, and a setting on it chooses how much power the speaker takes. That setting is called the speaker tap. The total power of all the taps must stay below what the amplifier can supply.

In a meeting room, the job is different. One person speaks to a group, and the speakers make the voice clear rather than loud. For events, the talks, microphones and slides follow a planned order that is practiced first. Slides appear on a large screen.

Those slides go from a laptop to a screen or projector through one cable, which often carries the sound too. Screens make the picture in different ways, such as with liquid crystals or tiny mirrors. When they connect, the screen sends the laptop a message listing the picture sizes and sound types it can use. The laptop is often where a call with people in other places begins.

Those people hear the room through its microphones. But the microphones also pick up their voices from the room speakers, so they hear their own voice as an echo. A processor measures how sound goes from the speakers to the microphones and removes that part. It is called an echo canceller. Every microphone that is turned on also adds room noise. A mixer that turns up the microphone of the person speaking, and turns the others down, is called an automixer.

Even clear speech can be hard to understand for people with hearing loss. So the sound is also sent directly to the listener. A wire around the room carries the sound as a magnetic signal. That wire is called a hearing loop. Many hearing aids pick up the signal with a tiny coil. That coil is called a telecoil. Other systems use infrared light, radio, or the listener's own phone. The law often requires this help, and a technician uses a meter to check the signal at every seat.

The flashcards cover speaker lines, presentations and events, video and screens, clear calls, and sound for people with hearing loss. By the end, you will know how a building's sound reaches every room and every listener.
