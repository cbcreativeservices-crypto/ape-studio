Topic Foundations: HiFi Consumer Audio

Imagine an evening at home. One person plays a vinyl record. Another watches a film with sound all around. A third listens to music through earbuds. This topic is about high-fidelity (hi-fi) home audio. It asks one question: how does each kind of home equipment play a recording close to the original?

On a record, the music is stored as a tiny wavy groove. A small needle follows the groove. That needle is called the stylus. A small device turns its movement into a weak electrical signal. An arm holds the device over the record and presses the needle down with a set weight. That weight is called the tracking force.

The signal from the record is weak. When the record was made, its low frequencies were reduced and its high frequencies raised. So a small amplifier raises the level and returns all frequencies to their original balance. That amplifier is called the phono preamp.

Then the signal reaches the speakers. A passive speaker has no amplifier inside, so a separate amplifier powers it. A powered speaker has its amplifier built in. Small speakers often sit on a shelf or stand, and tall ones stand on the floor and usually give deeper bass. Their position matters too. A common starting point is the two speakers and the listener in a triangle. The speakers are turned slightly toward the seat. That turn is called toe-in.

For films, the system adds speakers at the sides and the back, so sounds seem to come from all directions. Small speakers usually cannot reproduce very low frequencies. So a central unit sends those frequencies to one or more bass speakers. That setting is called bass management. The central unit often measures the room with a microphone and adjusts each speaker for the main seat.

Most music now arrives as digital data. A wireless link through the home network can keep the full quality and play music in several rooms. A short-range link to a phone usually reduces the data. So some detail is lost, and the sound arrives slightly late. Many products also add their own processing. Some attempt to restore high frequencies lost from the data, and others make voices clearer.

Most headphones and earbuds use that short-range link. Sealed ear cups block much outside sound, and open ear cups let sound pass both ways. Earbuds need a good seal in the ear, or the low frequencies are lost. Small microphones measure the noise around the listener. The headphones then play an opposite sound wave that cancels much of the steady, low noise. This is called active noise cancellation.
