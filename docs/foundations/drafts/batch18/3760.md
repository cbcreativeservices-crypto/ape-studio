Topic Foundations: HiFi Consumer Audio

Imagine an evening at home. One person plays a vinyl record. Another watches a film with sound all around the sofa. A third listens to music through earbuds. This topic is about high-fidelity (hi-fi) home audio. It asks one question: how does each kind of home equipment carry a recording to the listener, and what does it need to sound correct?

The record is the oldest source. The music is stored as a tiny wavy groove in a plastic disc. A small needle follows the groove. That needle is called the stylus. A small device turns its movement into a weak electrical signal. An arm holds the device over the record and presses the needle down with a set weight, so it stays in the groove. That weight is called the tracking force.

The signal from the record is very weak. Also, its low frequencies were reduced and its high frequencies raised when the record was made. So a small amplifier raises its level and returns all frequencies to their original balance. That amplifier is called the phono preamp. Then a main amplifier powers the speakers, and their position matters. A common starting point is the two speakers and the listener in a triangle, with the speakers turned slightly toward the seat. That turn is called toe-in.

For films, the system adds speakers at the sides and the back, so sounds seem to come from all directions. Small speakers cannot reproduce very low frequencies, so a central unit sends them to one large bass speaker. That setting is called bass management. The central unit often measures the room with a microphone and adjusts each speaker for the main seat.

Most music now arrives as digital data. A wireless link through the home network can keep the full quality and play music in several rooms. A short-range link to a phone usually reduces the data, so some detail is lost and the sound arrives slightly late. Many products add their own processing, for example to restore lost high frequencies, increase the low frequencies, or make voices clearer.

Most headphones and earbuds use that short-range link. Sealed ear cups block much outside sound, and open ear cups let sound pass both ways. Earbuds need a good seal in the ear, or the low frequencies are lost. Small microphones measure the noise around the listener, and the headphones play an opposite sound wave that cancels much of the steady, low noise. This is called active noise cancellation. The same microphones can also let outside sound in, so the listener hears people and traffic.

The flashcards cover turntables and speakers, home theater, digital connections, headphones, product processing and speaker wiring hidden in walls and ceilings. By the end, you will know how each kind of home equipment carries a recording to the listener, and what helps it sound close to the original.
