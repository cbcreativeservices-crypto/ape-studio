Topic Foundations: Audio System Design & Specification

Imagine a new shopping center that is still being built. The walls are not finished, and cables hang from the ceiling. Soon, music will play in every shop, and announcements will reach every corridor. Someone must plan that sound now. So how does a plan for a building become a sound system that people can use for many years?

The plan starts with questions, not equipment. The designer asks the owner what the system must do and what limits there are, such as budget and space. This step is called the needs analysis. Then the designer visits the building to see the ceilings, the power, and where cables can be placed. That visit is called the site survey.

The answers first describe the jobs the system must do. A shop may need music and announcements, and an important announcement usually lowers the music automatically. An office may add a steady background sound that makes nearby conversations harder to understand. Each area with its own sound and its own volume is called a zone. In some halls, hidden microphones and speakers add reverberation, the sound that remains after a sound stops, so the room sounds larger.

Next, the designer decides where the sound must reach. The goal is a similar level everywhere people listen, without loud spots or quiet gaps. This evenness is called coverage, and it is judged at the height of the listeners' ears.

Much of the equipment is not in the listening rooms. Amplifiers produce heat and fan noise, so they usually go in a cool, locked room full of equipment racks. In a theater, the desk where the engineer controls the sound is usually among the seats, so the engineer hears what the audience hears.

These decisions are recorded in drawings, a list of every item, and a written description of the work. The installer sends a document for each product to be approved before buying anything. During building, cables are placed before the walls are finished, and heavy speakers attach to the building structure, not to ceiling tiles. Every hole in a fire wall must be sealed again. Emergency voice systems are built the same careful way. They often use cable that keeps working during a fire, and they test their own wiring continuously.

When the work is finished, the installer tests every part against the plan. This step is called commissioning. Then the drawings are corrected to show what was really built, staff learn the controls, and regular checks keep the system working.

The flashcards explain each step: planning and project organization, the jobs a system does, coverage, equipment rooms and operator positions, mounting and wiring, emergency systems, and testing and service. By the end, you will know how a permanent sound system is planned, specified, built, tested and kept working.
