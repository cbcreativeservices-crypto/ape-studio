Topic Foundations: Audio System Design and Specification

Imagine a new shopping center that is under construction. Soon, music will play in every shop, and announcements will reach every corridor. How does a plan for a building become a sound system that people can use for many years?

The plan starts with questions, not equipment. The designer asks the owner what the system must do and what limits apply, such as budget and space. This step is called the needs analysis. Then the designer visits the building to see ceilings, power and cable paths. That visit is called the site survey. The owner also chooses whether one company designs and builds the system, or installers offer prices on finished plans.

The answers tell the designer what jobs the system must do. An important announcement usually lowers or cuts the music. It is also heard where a local volume control is turned down. This is called priority paging. An office may add a steady background sound that makes nearby conversations harder to understand. In a theater, microphones and speakers can make the room sound larger. The most important job is safety. An emergency voice system gives spoken instructions to guide people out during a fire.

Each area with its own sound and its own volume is called a zone. A central digital processor decides what plays in each area and sends each source to the right zones.

Next, the designer decides where the sound must reach. The goal is a similar level everywhere people listen. This evenness is called coverage. The system also needs places for its equipment and for the people who run it. Amplifiers produce heat and fan noise, so they are often kept in a cool, locked equipment room with racks. Network cables meet in a main wiring room. In a theater, the mixing position, where the engineer controls the sound, is usually among the seats, so the engineer hears what the audience hears. Other positions may serve the performers or a television crew.

These decisions are recorded in drawings, a list of every item, and a written description of the work. Before buying, the installer sends the details of each product for approval.

During building, cables are placed before the walls are finished. Heavy speakers attach to the building structure, not to ceiling tiles. Every hole in a fire wall must be sealed again. Cables for the emergency system often keep working during a fire, and the system usually checks its own wiring for faults.

When the work is finished, the installer tests every part against the plan. This step is called commissioning. Then the drawings are corrected to show what was really built, and staff learn the controls. The flashcards explain each stage, from the first questions to regular service visits. By the end, you will know how a permanent sound system is planned, specified, built, tested and kept working.
