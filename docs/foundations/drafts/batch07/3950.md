Topic Foundations: Loudness, Quality Control, Delivery and Metadata

In the topic on mastering fundamentals, you learned how the final sound of a song is prepared. But the song will play on streaming services, radio, television and discs, and each one has its own rules. An error in the master also appears in every copy. So this topic asks: how do you prove that a master is correct before it leaves the studio?

The first check is loudness. Many streaming services measure the average loudness of a whole song, then turn each song up or down so all songs play at about the same level. This is called normalization. So a master made much louder than this common level is not played louder. It is simply turned down. To make it that loud, its loud and quiet parts were pushed closer together. Turning it down does not bring that difference back. A meter confirms that the master meets each destination's target.

Average loudness does not show short peaks. In a digital file, the highest point of a sound wave can be between the measured points. When a service converts the master into a smaller file, these peaks can rise and cause distortion. So engineers keep the highest peak a little below the maximum level. That space is called the true-peak margin.

Next, the engineer checks the sound itself. Meters and listening find clipping (peaks cut off), clicks and hiss. Some phones and radios play only one channel. To test this, the left and right channels are added together and played. Sounds that move in opposite directions in the two channels can cancel. This test is called a mono compatibility check.

Each destination requires a particular file type, quality and loudness. A file made exactly for one destination is called a delivery master. One best copy is usually kept unchanged for the future. The files go into a package with cover art and notes, and a checklist confirms that nothing is missing.

Each file also needs information that computers can read. Every recording gets its own permanent code, called a recording identifier, so the right people are paid. The whole release gets a barcode number, and tags store the title and credits. A short code is also calculated from each file's data, and again after copying. If the two codes match, the file did not change.

Only then is the work approved. A final listen to the whole release also checks the order of the songs and the gaps between them. An engineer confirms the technical rules are met, and the client confirms it sounds the way they wanted. The client's final, formal acceptance is called delivery sign-off.
