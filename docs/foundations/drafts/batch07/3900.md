Topic Foundations: Guitars, Bass and Keys

Imagine listening to a band on your phone. The bass plays low notes, two guitars play chords, and a piano plays more chords above them. Many of their frequencies overlap. If every part is simply made louder, they make each other hard to hear. So this topic asks: how does each instrument get its own frequencies and position, so every part can be heard?

The bass comes first, because it carries the lowest notes. It is often recorded two ways at once. One track is the signal taken directly from the instrument. The other comes from a microphone at the amplifier. The microphone signal arrives slightly later, so some frequencies partly cancel and the bass can lose low sound. Moving one track in time so the two match is called bass phase alignment.

Once the bass keeps its low frequencies, it must stay audible everywhere. Small speakers, such as those in phones, often cannot play the deepest notes. So the mix adds higher tones that belong to each bass note, and the brain still hears the missing low note. This is called bass harmonic enhancement. The kick drum uses the same low frequencies. So a compressor often measures the kick and turns the bass down briefly on each hit. This is called kick-triggered bass ducking.

With the low frequencies shared, the guitars need their own position and frequencies. Often the same guitar part is recorded twice. One attempt is placed far left and the other far right. Their small differences spread the sound between the speakers. These are called hard-panned doubles. The center is left free for the voice, the bass and the drums.

Left and right placement is not enough, because the guitars still overlap with the bass in frequency. So their lowest frequencies are usually reduced. Too much energy in the low middle frequencies makes notes hard to tell apart, and reducing it is called guitar mud reduction. Distorted guitars may also have a buzzing hiss in the highest frequencies, which is often reduced too.

The keyboards are often the hardest to place. A piano covers almost the whole range of frequencies, so it overlaps with every other part. It is usually recorded with two or more microphones, which must also match in time. Organs and synthesizers often play long, held notes in the background. Playing two or more of these held sounds together is called pad layering. Because there are so many tracks, the tracks of each instrument are often sent to one volume control.

The flashcards explain how the bass is recorded and kept audible, and how guitars are placed and shaped. They also cover how keyboards are mixed and how tracks are grouped. By the end, you will know how guitars, bass and keyboards each get their own frequencies and position in a mix.
