Topic Foundations: Orchestral, Choir and Acoustic-Ensemble Mixing

Imagine sitting in a concert hall while an orchestra plays. The violins are on one side, the cellos on the other, and the choir behind them. You also hear sound reflected by the hall. How can many microphones be combined so the recording still sounds like one group in one real room?

These mixes usually start from a few main microphones above the conductor. They pick up the whole orchestra together. Spot microphones sit close to single instruments and add detail to quiet parts. They are usually kept low in the mix. Each spot microphone creates a timing problem. Being close, it picks up each note before the main microphones do. The instrument can then seem too close. The two copies of each note also arrive at different times, and some frequencies partly cancel. To fix both problems, the spot signal is delayed slightly. This is called spot-delay compensation.

How near or far each instrument seems depends mostly on how much room sound is heard. Close microphones give direct sound, and far ones pick up mostly reflected sound. The balance between the two is called the direct-to-ambient ratio. When the music stops, the reflected sound continues and then fades. Keeping that fade is called hall-tail preservation.

Next, the level of each section must be set. A section is a group of the same instruments, such as all the violins. How well its players join into one sound is called section blend. Each group needs a level where it does not cover the sound of another.

Smaller acoustic groups, such as a guitar and piano, are mixed in a similar way, usually with more close microphones. Each instrument gets its own place from left to right, its own share of room sound, and small tone changes, so it sounds clear but natural. Close microphones also pick up small noises, such as a bow, flute keys, guitar strings or a page turning, so these are kept low with careful editing.

A choir is balanced in the same way, but its sections are groups of voices, from high to low. Each group must be heard, and the words must stay easy to understand. Loud "s" sounds are often reduced, because many singers make them at once. The choir's width, depth and room sound are set so the singers seem to stand together.

That balance changes as the music gets louder and quieter. A soloist can become hard to hear over a loud orchestra, so the soloist's level is often raised there. Levels may also be raised slowly as the music grows louder. When these slow changes are saved in the mix, this is called crescendo automation.

The flashcards cover each step for orchestras, choirs and smaller groups. By the end, you will know how separate microphones are combined so an orchestra, choir or small group sounds like one performance in one real room.
