Topic Foundations: Orchestral, Choir & Acoustic-Ensemble Mixing

Imagine sitting in a concert hall while an orchestra plays. The violins are on one side, the cellos on the other, and the brass farther back. Around them, you also hear sound reflected by the hall. A recording often uses many microphones. So how do you combine them so the result still sounds like one group in one real room?

These mixes usually start from a few main microphones above the conductor. They pick up the whole orchestra together, with the balance the players already created. Spot microphones sit close to single instruments and add detail to quiet parts. They are usually added only a little, because the main microphones provide most of the sound.

Each spot microphone creates a timing problem. Being close, it picks up each note before the main microphones do. The instrument can then seem too close, and some frequencies can cancel. So the engineer delays the spot signal slightly. This is called spot-delay compensation. It keeps each section where it sat, from left to right and from front to back. That sense of where each player sits on the stage is called stage imaging.

Front and back depend mostly on the room. Close microphones give direct sound. Microphones far from the stage pick up mostly sound reflected from the walls. The balance between the two is called the direct-to-ambient ratio. More direct sound places the listener near the stage, and more room sound farther back. When the music stops, the hall keeps sounding for a moment and then fades. Keeping that fade is called hall-tail preservation.

Next come the groups of players. A section is a group of the same instruments, such as all the violins. How well its players join into one sound is called section blend. Each group needs a level where it does not hide another. A choir is balanced in the same way. Its high and low voice groups must all be heard, its singers must join into one sound, and its words must stay easy to understand.

That balance changes as the music gets louder and quieter. A soloist can become hard to hear over a loud orchestra, so the engineer raises the soloist's level there. As the whole orchestra grows louder, some levels may also be raised slowly to keep the balance even. Saved level changes of this kind are called crescendo automation. Raising levels also raises small noises, such as a bow, flute keys, a piano pedal or a page turning, so careful placement and editing keep them low.

The flashcards cover the main and spot microphones, the hall, the stereo picture, sections and choir parts, level changes, small noises and the tone of single instruments. By the end, you will know how separate microphones are combined so that an orchestra or choir still sounds like one group in one real hall.
