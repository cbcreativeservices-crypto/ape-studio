Topic Foundations: Orchestral, Choir and Acoustic-Ensemble Mixing

Imagine sitting in a concert hall while an orchestra plays. The violins are on one side, the cellos on the other, and the choir behind them. You also hear sound reflected by the hall. How can many microphones be combined so the recording still sounds like one group in one real room?

These mixes usually start from a few main microphones above the conductor. They pick up the whole orchestra together, with the balance the players create. Spot microphones sit close to single instruments and add detail to quiet parts. They are usually kept at a low level in the mix.

Each spot microphone creates a timing problem. Being close, it picks up each note before the main microphones do. The instrument can then seem too close. The two copies of each note also arrive at different times, so some frequencies partly cancel. So the engineer delays the spot signal slightly. This is called spot-delay compensation. It keeps each section in its place on the stage.

Front and back depend mostly on the room. Close microphones give direct sound, and far ones pick up mostly reflected sound. The balance between the two is called the direct-to-ambient ratio. More direct sound places the listener near the stage, and more room sound farther back. When the music stops, the reflected sound continues and then fades. Keeping that fade is called hall-tail preservation.

Next, the level of each section must be set. A section is a group of the same instruments, such as all the violins. How well its players join into one sound is called section blend. Each group needs a level where it does not hide another. Smaller acoustic groups, such as a guitar and piano, are mixed the same way. Each instrument may get small tone changes and a place from left to right, so it sounds clear but still natural.

A choir is balanced in the same way, but its sections are groups of voices, from high to low. Each group must be heard, and the words must stay easy to understand. Loud "s" sounds are often reduced, because many singers make them at once. Room sound is kept too.

That balance changes as the music gets louder and quieter. A soloist can become hard to hear over a loud orchestra, so the soloist's level is raised there. As the orchestra grows louder, some levels may be raised slowly with it. Saved level changes of this kind are called crescendo automation. Raising a level also raises small noises, such as a bow, flute keys, guitar strings or a page turning, so these are kept low with careful editing.
