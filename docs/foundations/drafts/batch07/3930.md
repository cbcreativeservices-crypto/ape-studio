Topic Foundations: Mix Automation, Bus and Loudness

Imagine you have set the level of every track in a song, and they sound balanced. Then you listen to the whole song. In the verse, one quiet word becomes too quiet to hear. In the chorus, the voice is hard to hear. A balance that never changes is called a static mix. It is a good start, but a song changes from section to section. So how can the mix change with it, the same way every time?

The answer is that the mixer records the movements of its controls and repeats them on playback. On many large mixing consoles, small motors move the volume sliders. A setting on each track decides what happens when you touch a control. It can play back only, replace every move, or record only while your hand is on the control. Another setting starts recording when you touch a control and continues until playback stops. That setting is called latch.

Often you record one kind of control at a time. You play the song from start to end and work only on volume. Each complete playback like this is called a mix pass. Other passes record where parts switch off and on, move sounds left and right, or change the controls of an effect.

The recorded moves appear as a line over the track, with small points where the value changes. You can drag these points, draw a slow rise, or copy the moves of the first chorus to the second. You can also compare a new version with the old one before you keep it.

While these moves play, all the tracks are added together on one path, the mix bus. When many tracks are loud together, their peaks add up. If the total goes above the highest level the system can carry, the tops of the waves are cut off and the sound distorts. The space you leave below that level is called mix-bus headroom. A limiter can set a maximum level. Too much limiting removes the difference between loud hits and the average level, and the drum hits are no longer louder than the rest.

That difference often matters more than loudness, because many streaming services play songs at a similar level. The final test is how the mix sounds away from the studio, on phones, earbuds, laptops, cars and dance club sound systems. Small speakers play very few low frequencies. When a mix still sounds balanced on all of them, it has good translation. Engineers also compare their work with a finished song they trust, called a reference mix.

The flashcards explain each step in detail. They also describe the extra versions and group files made at the end. By the end, you will know how a mix changes over time, stays below its maximum level, and is checked so it sounds right on any system.
