Topic Foundations: Mix Automation, Bus and Loudness

Imagine you have set the level of every track, and the start of the song sounds balanced. In the verse, one soft word is hard to hear. In the chorus, the guitars are louder than the voice. A balance that never changes is called a static mix. It is a good start, but a song changes from section to section. So how can the mix change with it, the same way every time?

The answer is that the mixer records the movements of its controls and repeats them on playback. On many large consoles, motors move the volume sliders. A setting on each track decides what happens when you touch a control. It can play back only, replace every move, or record only while your hand is on the control. Another setting starts recording when you touch a control. After you let go, it keeps the last value until playback stops. That setting is called latch automation mode.

You often record one kind of control at a time. You play the song from start to end and work only on volume. Each complete playback like this is called a mix pass. Other passes record where parts switch off and on, move sounds left and right, or change the controls of an effect. The recorded moves appear as a line over the track, with small points where the value changes. You can drag these points, draw a slow rise, or copy moves to another chorus. You can also compare a new version with the old one.

While these moves play, all the tracks are added together on one path, the mix bus. When many tracks are loud together, their peaks add up. If the total goes above the highest level the output or file can carry, the tops of the waves are cut off and the sound distorts. The space you leave below that level is called mix-bus headroom.

A limiter on the mix bus sets a maximum level, so the whole mix can be made louder. Too much limiting shrinks the difference between loud hits and the average level, so drum hits are barely louder than the rest. Many streaming services turn louder songs down, so most songs play at a similar level. So keeping that difference often matters more than extra loudness.

The final test is how the mix sounds away from the studio, on phones, earbuds, cars and club sound systems. Small speakers play few low frequencies. When a mix still sounds balanced on all of them, it has good translation. Engineers also compare their work with a finished song they trust, called a reference mix.

The flashcards explain each step in detail, and the extra versions made at the end. By the end, you will know how a mix changes over time, stays below its maximum level, and is checked so it sounds right on any system.
