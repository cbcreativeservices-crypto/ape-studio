Topic Foundations: Mastering Fundamentals and Chain

Imagine you play a friend's finished song right after a song from a well-known album. The friend's song sounds quieter and seems narrower between the speakers. The mix is good, but it is not ready for release. One more step gives it a balanced tone, a suitable loudness and a correct final file. That step is called mastering. So what does this last step change, and how is each change decided?

Every decision depends on hearing the sound correctly. A mastering room has matching left and right sides, and speakers that play the full range of frequencies we hear. The speakers are set so that a known test signal always produces the same sound level. That is called monitor calibration. Then a change you hear comes from the music, not from the room. Each change is also compared with the original at the same loudness, because a louder version almost always seems better.

Next, the mix goes through a series of tools, one after another. That series is called the mastering chain. Tone is often shaped first. An equalizer makes small changes across wide frequency bands, so the lows, middle and highs are in balance.

Then the chain controls the loud and quiet parts. A song has large changes, such as a quiet verse and a loud chorus. It also has small, fast changes, such as the sharp start of each drum hit. A gentle compressor reduces the difference a little. Last, one tool sets a maximum level that the sound cannot go above. That tool is called a brickwall limiter. It lets the engineer raise the level of the whole song. If the level is raised too much, both kinds of change become much smaller.

The engineer also checks where sounds are placed between the speakers. A stereo mix can be divided into the sound both speakers share, in the center, and the differences, at the sides. Treating each part separately is called mid-side processing. The sides can be made wider or narrower, while the voice and bass stay steady in the center.

Finally, the sound becomes a release file. That file often stores each measured point of the sound with less detail. Rounding every point creates a small distortion in quiet parts. A very quiet, steady noise added during the rounding replaces it. That is called dithering. For an album, the engineer also sets the order of the songs and the silences between them, so the songs work together as one album.

The flashcards explain each part. They cover the room, each tool and the software that provides it, stereo work and file formats. They also cover mastering a single, an album or separate groups of instruments, and working with the artist. By the end, you will know what mastering changes, in what order, and how each decision is judged by listening and by measuring.
