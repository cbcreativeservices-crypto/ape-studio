Topic Foundations: Mastering Fundamentals and Chain

Imagine you play a friend's finished song right after a song from a well-known album. The friend's song sounds quieter and seems narrower between the speakers. One more step gives it a balanced tone, a suitable loudness and a correct final file. That step is called mastering. So what does this last step change, and how is each change decided?

Every decision depends on hearing the sound correctly. A mastering room has matching left and right sides, so the room does not pull sounds toward one side. Its speakers play almost the full range of frequencies we hear. They are also set so that a known test signal always produces the same sound level. That is called monitor calibration, and it means every song is judged at the same level. Each change is also compared with the original at equal loudness, because a louder version usually seems better.

The mix usually arrives as one stereo file, and sometimes as a few group files, such as drums and vocals. The sound then goes through a series of tools, one after another. That series is called the mastering chain. Tone is often shaped first. An equalizer makes small changes across wide frequency bands, so the lows, middle and highs are in balance.

Then the chain controls the loud and quiet parts. A song has large changes, such as a quiet verse and a loud chorus. It also has small, fast changes, such as the first moment of each drum hit. A gentle compressor reduces the difference a little. Last, one tool sets a maximum level that the sound cannot go above. That tool is called a brickwall limiter. It lets the engineer raise the level of the whole song. If the level is raised too much, both kinds of change become much smaller.

The engineer also checks where sounds are placed between the speakers. A stereo mix can be divided into the sound both speakers share, in the center, and the differences, at the sides. Treating each part separately is called mid-side processing. The sides can be made wider or narrower, while the voice and bass stay steady in the center.

At the end of the chain, the sound becomes a release file. That file often stores each measured point of the sound as a shorter number, so every point is rounded. The rounding creates a small distortion in quiet parts. So a very quiet, steady noise is added during the rounding, in place of that distortion. That is called dithering.

For an album, the engineer also sets the order of the songs and the silences between them. The artist then listens and may ask for changes before approval.
