Topic Foundations: Vocal Mixing

Imagine a song playing from a loudspeaker in a busy shop. You can hardly hear the drums, but you can still follow every word. The main singing part is called the lead vocal, and most of the mix is arranged around it. So how does a mix keep a voice clear, steady in level and easy to hear above the music at any volume?

The work starts with the recordings. A singer usually records each line several times. The engineer joins the best parts of each attempt into one performance, with short fades so the joins cannot be heard. Then mouth clicks, bursts of air on "p" sounds, and hum are removed. Breaths are made quieter but kept, so the voice still sounds human.

The clean performance then needs correct pitch and timing. Tuning software measures each sung note and moves it toward the correct note. The setting that decides how fast this happens is called the retune speed. A slow speed is usually not noticed. An instant speed makes notes jump in steps, and some styles use that sound on purpose. Words that are early or late are moved onto the beat.

Correct notes do not help if some words are too quiet to hear. A compressor turns down the loudest words automatically. Often two compressors are connected one after another, each doing a little. That is called vocal serial compression. The engineer also raises quiet words and lowers loud ones by hand.

A voice at a steady level can still be hard to understand. Equalization, which raises or lowers chosen frequency bands, removes low rumble and adds a small boost where words become clearer. The hissing sound of "s" and "sh" is called sibilance. A de-esser lowers the voice at those moments, but too much lowering makes "s" sounds unclear.

A voice recorded close to the microphone has almost no sense of a room. Reverb, the sound of a room, and delay, a repeated echo, add that space on separate channels, so the original voice stays unchanged. Reverb can make words unclear, so it is often lowered while the singer sings. One word at the end of a line can also be sent to an echo alone. That is called a vocal throw delay.

The last part, and a large one, is the background vocals: harmonies and doubled lines that support the lead. They are usually quieter and spread wider than the lead, which normally stays in the center. Layered attempts of one harmony are called a harmony stack. One channel controls the whole group. Their words are aligned in time, and each layer's "s" sounds are reduced, because many layers add more hiss.

The flashcards explain each step, from editing to background vocals. By the end, you will know how each one keeps a voice clear, steady in level and easy to hear above the music.
