Topic Foundations: Drum and Percussion Mixing

Imagine a drummer recording in a studio. One microphone sits close to each drum. A pair hangs above the drum kit, and two more stand far away in the room. How do these many tracks become one kit that is clear and still fits the song?

The first step is balance. Close microphones give a direct sound with little sound reflected from the room. The pair above the kit picks up the cymbals and the whole kit together. Room microphones make the kit sound larger and farther away. More close sound puts the listener near the drums, and more room sound puts them in a large space. Some mixes copy a real kit in a real room, and others sound larger than any real kit.

Next, each drum gets its own tone. A kick drum often has one microphone inside for the click of the beater and one outside for the deep low sound. Mixing the two is called a kick microphone blend. A snare often has one microphone on top and one below for the buzz of the thin wires under it. Toms, the drums around the snare, and kick drums can keep sounding at one note, which makes the low frequencies unclear, so an equalizer cut often reduces that note.

Each close microphone also picks up the other drums. A gate keeps a microphone almost silent until its own drum is hit. A tool can make the start of a hit louder or quieter, and the sound after it longer or shorter. This is called drum transient shaping. Compression brings loud and soft hits closer in level, but too much can remove the sharp start of each hit.

Sometimes a recorded drum is still too quiet or uneven in level. Software can detect each hit and play a stored drum sound together with it. The stored sound must start at exactly the same moment, or the two sounds partly cancel. Lining them up is called sample alignment.

Finally, the kit is treated as one instrument. All drum tracks are sent into one shared channel, called the drum bus. A small amount of compression there helps the drums sound like one kit, an effect called drum-bus glue. Too much there can make the cymbals drop in level on every kick hit. A copy of the drums can also be compressed by a large amount and mixed in at a low level with the normal drums. That is parallel drum compression. It raises the quiet sound that follows each hit, while the normal drums keep their sharp start.

The flashcards explain each part, including room sound, the tone of each drum, gates, stored drum sounds, shared channels and timing edits. By the end, you will know how separate drum tracks are balanced, shaped and joined into one kit that fits the song.
