Topic Foundations: Drum and Percussion Mixing

Imagine a drummer recording in a studio. One microphone sits close to each drum. A pair stands above the kit, and two more stand far away in the room. How do these many tracks become one kit that is clear and still fits the song?

The first step is balance. Close microphones give a direct sound with little room sound. The pair above picks up the cymbals and the whole kit. More close sound makes the drums seem near the listener, and more room sound makes them seem to play in a large space. Room microphones are sometimes compressed heavily to make the room sound louder.

Next, a tone control called an equalizer shapes each drum. It can also lower cymbal frequencies that are too loud and high. A kick drum often has one microphone inside for the click of the pedal's beater, and one outside for the low sound. Mixing the two is called a kick microphone blend. A snare often has one microphone on top and one below for the buzz of the thin wires under it. Toms are drums tuned to different notes, and each may need its ringing shortened.

Each close microphone also picks up the other drums, so a gate is often used to keep it almost silent until its own drum is hit. Another tool can make the first moment of a hit louder or quieter, and the sound after it longer or shorter. This is called drum transient shaping. Compression brings loud and soft hits closer in level, but a fast setting can make that first moment quieter. Gentle distortion, called drum saturation, can add higher frequencies and make hits seem louder at the same level.

Some hits can also be slightly early or late. Editing can move them onto the beat. A recorded drum may also lack low or high frequencies that editing cannot add. Software detects each hit and plays a stored sound with it. The stored sound must start at the same moment as the real hit. Lining up this timing is called sample alignment. Its wave must also move the same way, up when the real hit moves up, or the two can partly cancel.

Finally, all drum tracks are sent into one shared channel, called the drum bus. Other percussion, such as shakers, often gets its own shared channel. A little compression on the drum bus helps the drums sound like one kit, an effect called drum-bus glue. Too much can make the cymbals drop in level on every kick hit. The bus can also be copied, compressed heavily and mixed in quietly. Quiet details then become louder, while the start of each hit stays clear.

The flashcards explain each step in detail, drum by drum. By the end, you will know how separate drum tracks are balanced, shaped and joined into one kit that fits the song.
