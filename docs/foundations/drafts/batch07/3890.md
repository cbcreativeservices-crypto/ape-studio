Topic Foundations: Drum and Percussion Mixing

Imagine a drummer recording in a studio. One microphone sits close to each drum. A pair hangs above the kit, and two more stand far away in the room. How do these many tracks become one kit that is clear and still fits the song?

The first step is balance. Close microphones give a direct sound with little room sound. The pair above picks up the cymbals and the whole kit. A tone control called an equalizer can lower unpleasant high cymbal frequencies. More close sound puts the listener near the drums, and more room sound puts them in a large space. The room microphones are sometimes compressed heavily to make the room sound louder.

Next, the equalizer gives each drum its own tone. A kick drum often has one microphone inside for the click of the pedal's beater, and one outside for the low sound. Mixing the two is called a kick microphone blend. A snare often has one microphone on top and one below for the buzz of the thin wires under it. Toms are drums tuned to different notes, and each one may need its ringing shortened in its own way.

Each close microphone also picks up the other drums, so a gate keeps it almost silent until its own drum is hit. Another tool can make the first moment of a hit louder or quieter, and the sound after it longer or shorter. This is called drum transient shaping. Compression brings loud and soft hits closer in level, but too much can make that first moment quieter.

Even with good tone and level, a hit can be late, or a drum can lack low or high frequencies. Late hits can be moved onto the beat. Software can then detect each hit of that drum and play a stored sound with it. The stored sound must start at the same moment, and its wave must move the same way, up when the real hit moves up. Otherwise the two can partly cancel. Lining them up is called sample alignment.

Finally, all drum tracks are sent into one shared channel, called the drum bus. A little compression there helps the drums sound like one kit, an effect called drum-bus glue. Too much can make the cymbals drop in level on every kick hit. A heavily compressed copy can also be mixed in quietly, so quiet details become louder while each hit keeps its shape. Other percussion, such as shakers, often gets its own shared channel too.

The flashcards explain each step in detail, drum by drum. They also cover gentle distortion, playing drums through a speaker to record them again, and saving track groups as separate files. By the end, you will know how separate drum tracks are balanced, shaped and joined into one kit that fits the song.
