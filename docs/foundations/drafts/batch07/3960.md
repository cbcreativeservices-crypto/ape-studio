Topic Foundations: Vinyl / Disc Mastering

A finished master for streaming is a computer file, delivered as data to every listener. A vinyl record or a compact disc is a physical object with physical limits, and it must also pass through a cutting machine and a factory. So what must happen to a master before it becomes a disc that plays correctly?

A record starts on a machine called a cutting lathe. A metal plate with a soft coating, called a lacquer disc, spins on it. A heated cutting needle, called a stylus, carves one long spiral groove into the coating. The stylus moves with the music, so the groove follows the sound wave. Each wall of the groove carries one stereo channel.

Loud sounds and very low bass make the groove move farther sideways, so they need more space. If two turns of the spiral touch, a record player's needle can jump into the wrong turn of the groove. So the lathe receives the music slightly early and spreads the turns apart before loud parts. The distance between the turns is called the groove pitch.

Very low bass that differs between the left and right channels makes the groove move up and down. This can push the needle out of the groove. So a tool makes the bass the same in both channels below a set frequency. That tool is called an elliptical filter. Loud "s" sounds are also reduced. A side with more minutes of music needs closer turns and a lower level. The turns near the center are shorter, so the needle traces sound there less accurately. That is called inner groove distortion. So louder songs often go near the outer edge.

The lacquer is too soft to press records. Metal copies are made from it by metal plating in several steps, and the last ones press the hot vinyl. The factory first presses a few sample records. People listen for noise, clicks and skips before they approve the full production.

A compact disc has no groove. Its sound is stored as numbers in tiny pits, and every music disc follows one shared standard. The master also carries hidden markers that show where each track starts. It is usually sent to the factory as computer files. There, a laser writes the data onto a coated glass disc, called the glass master, and metal copies of it press the plastic discs. No disc is perfect, so every player corrects small reading errors. Test equipment counts these errors each second. A finished disc should have no errors that this correction cannot repair.

The flashcards explain the cutting machine, the groove, preparing the sound and sides, the metal copies and approval, and the compact disc master and its checks. By the end, you will know how a master becomes a record or a compact disc, and how each is checked before a factory makes copies.
