Topic Foundations: Vinyl and Disc Mastering

A master for streaming is a computer file. A vinyl record or a compact disc is a physical object, so a physical master must be made first and then copied in a factory. What must happen to a master before it becomes a disc that plays correctly?

A record starts on a machine called a cutting lathe. A metal plate with a soft coating, called a lacquer disc, spins on it. A heated cutting needle, called a cutting stylus, carves one long spiral groove into the coating. The stylus moves with the music, so the groove follows the sound wave. Each wall of the groove carries one stereo channel.

Loud sounds and very low bass make the groove move farther sideways, so they need more space. If two turns of the spiral touch, a record player's needle can jump into the wrong turn of the groove. So the lathe receives the music slightly early and spreads the turns apart before loud parts. The distance between the turns is called the groove pitch. A side with more minutes of music needs closer turns and a lower level. The turns near the center are shorter, so the needle traces sound there less accurately. That is called inner groove distortion. So louder songs often go near the outer edge.

Very low bass that differs between the left and right channels makes the groove move up and down. This can push the needle out of the groove. So a tool makes the bass the same in both channels below a set frequency. That tool is called an elliptical filter. Loud "s" sounds are also reduced, because they can sound distorted when the record is played.

The lacquer is too soft to press records. Metal copies are made from it by metal plating in several steps, and the last ones press the hot vinyl. A few test records are pressed first and checked for noise, clicks and skips before full production.

A compact disc has no groove. Its sound is stored as numbers in tiny pits, and music discs follow one shared standard. The master also carries hidden markers that show where each track starts, and sometimes text such as song titles. It is usually sent to the factory as computer files.

At the factory, a laser writes the data onto a coated glass disc, called the glass master, and metal copies of it press the plastic discs. Every player corrects small reading errors. Test equipment counts these errors each second. A finished disc should have no errors that this correction cannot repair.
