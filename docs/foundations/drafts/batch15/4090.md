Topic Foundations: Radio Production & Studio Workflow

Imagine listening to the radio in a car. A song ends and the host speaks briefly. A short clip gives the station's name, an advertisement plays, and the news starts exactly at the hour. There is almost never silence. So how does a station broadcast a live program in the right order and on time?

The work happens in the air studio, the room whose sound is being broadcast at that moment. Microphones, music players and phone callers each arrive on a channel of a mixing board. The board combines them into one finished mix. The person who runs the board is called the board operator, and often the host does this job too. Each item should start the moment the previous one ends. A host often speaks during the music at the start of a song and stops exactly when the singing begins. The point where the singing begins is the post, and ending your words exactly there is called hit the post.

The host does not choose the songs alone. Each hour follows a plan, drawn as a circle with the minutes around its edge. It shows which type of item plays at each point. That plan is called a clockwheel. Software uses it to fill the hour with songs and groups of advertisements. A playout computer then plays each item on time, even with nobody in the room. Stations also measure how many people listen in each hour.

Between the songs, the station plays its own short clips. A clip that says the station's name over music and sound effects is called a sweeper. For the news, a running order lists every item and its length, so the news program ends on time.

Some voices come from outside the studio. A caller must hear the show. But hearing their own voice come back makes talking difficult. So the board sends each caller the whole mix except their own voice. That feed is called mix-minus. At sports events, commentators speak from booths, and a broadcast truck full of equipment sends their sound to the studio. They also hear a mix-minus feed.

Callers can also say words that must not be broadcast. So the program is often delayed by a few seconds. If someone says something wrong, the operator presses the dump button, and those seconds are removed before anyone hears them. Other machines detect silence on the output and start backup audio.

The flashcards explain the rooms, equipment and people, and how each hour is planned and its listeners measured. They also cover station clips and news, work away from the studio, the rules a station must follow, and the systems that protect the broadcast. By the end, you will know how a station plans each hour, how a live program runs second by second, and how it keeps broadcasting when something goes wrong.
