Topic Foundations: Radio Production and Studio Workflow

Imagine sitting beside the host of a live morning radio show. Songs, advertisements and the news must each start on time. So how does a station broadcast a live program in the right order and on time?

The host sits in the air studio, the room whose sound is being broadcast at that moment. Microphones, music players and phone lines each connect to their own channel on a mixing board, with a fader that sets its volume. The person who runs the board, often the host, is called the board operator. A host often speaks over the start of a song and stops when the singing begins. This is called hitting the post.

Each hour follows a plan, drawn as a circle with the minutes around its edge. It shows which type of item plays at each point. That plan is called a clockwheel. Songs are sorted into groups by how new and how popular they are, and each group returns to the air at its own rate. A daily schedule, the traffic log, lists the advertisements, and a playout computer plays everything on time. At larger stations, the studios usually connect to one central room, the master control room. There the sound is checked and switched before broadcast.

Short clips that say the station's name over music are called sweepers. They and the advertisements are usually recorded earlier in production rooms. News is different, because it changes every hour. A newsreader often reads it live from a small booth and plays short recorded reports.

Some voices come from outside the studio. A caller must hear the show, but hearing their own voice come back makes talking hard. So the board sends each caller all the program sound except their own voice. That mix is called mix-minus. Reporters also hear the program in an earpiece, and a producer can talk to them through it. Commentators at sports events are also outside the studio. They often speak from booths, and a broadcast truck usually sends their sound back. At a large event, the main audio operator mixes the program in the truck, while an assistant places the microphones.

A live program can also go wrong. Callers can say words that must not be broadcast, so the program is often delayed by a few seconds. If a caller says such a word, the operator presses the dump button to remove those seconds. Silence during a broadcast is another danger, so a silence detector checks the output and starts backup audio when the sound stops. Stations also have legal duties. Many countries require them to record what they broadcast and to carry emergency alerts.

The flashcards explain each part of this work, including song scheduling and how listeners are counted. By the end, you will know how a station plans each hour, runs a live program, and keeps broadcasting when something goes wrong.
