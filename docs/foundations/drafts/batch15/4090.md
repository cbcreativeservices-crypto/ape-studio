Topic Foundations: Radio Production and Studio Workflow

Imagine sitting beside the host of a live morning radio show. Songs, advertisements and the news must each start on time. So how does a station broadcast a live program in the right order and on time?

The host sits in the air studio, the room that is on air. Microphones, music players and phone lines each connect to their own volume control, called a channel, on a mixing board. The person who runs the board is called the board operator, and often the host does this job too. Each item should start the moment the previous one ends. A host often speaks over the start of a song and stops when the singing begins. This is called hitting the post.

Each hour follows a plan, drawn as a circle with the minutes around its edge. It shows which type of item plays at each point. That plan is called a clockwheel. Music software follows it to pick a song of that type. The advertisements for each break are listed in a daily schedule called the traffic log. A playout computer then plays each item on time.

Short clips that say the station's name over music are called sweepers. They and the advertisements are usually recorded earlier in production rooms, so every hour sounds like the same station. News is often read live from a small booth, with short recorded reports between the stories. The sound from every studio usually passes through the master control room, where it is checked and switched before broadcast.

Some voices come from outside the studio. A caller must hear the show, but hearing their own voice come back makes talking hard. So the board sends each caller all the program sound except their own voice. That mix is called mix-minus. Reporters and commentators also hear the program in an earpiece, and a producer can talk to them through it.

Voices from events reach the studio in a similar way. At sports events, commentators often speak from booths, and a broadcast truck usually sends their sound to the studio. At a large event, the head sound person mixes the broadcast program in the truck, while an assistant places the microphones.

Because callers can say words that must not be broadcast, the program is often delayed by a few seconds. If that happens, the operator presses the dump button to remove those seconds. Silence on the air is another danger, so a silence detector watches the output and starts backup audio. Because the law in many countries requires stations to prove what they broadcast, they often record everything that goes on air. Many must also carry and test emergency alerts.

The flashcards explain each part of this work, including how listeners are counted. By the end, you will know how a station plans each hour, runs a live program, and keeps broadcasting when something goes wrong.
