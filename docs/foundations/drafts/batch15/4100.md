Topic Foundations: Broadcast Air Chain, Processing and Loudness

Imagine listening to the radio in a village. A presenter speaks in a city studio, and you hear the words at a steady volume. On its way to the transmitter, the sound passes through much equipment. That whole path is called the air chain. So what happens to the sound on this path?

Sound sometimes reaches the studio over links from events and news scenes. Then the studio-transmitter link carries it to the transmitter by radio or over the internet. Engineers usually watch and control that transmitter from the studio. Equipment at each end often makes the sound data smaller, then turns it back into sound.

At the transmitter, the sound is used to change a radio wave. In AM radio it changes the strength of the wave, and in FM radio it changes its frequency. The law limits how large that change may be. If the loudest moments, the peaks, go past the limit, the signal spreads into other stations' channels and sounds distorted. That is called overmodulation.

Stations usually want a loud sound, so the on-air processor keeps it close to that limit. It keeps the level steady, in separate frequency bands. Then a limiter usually turns down the highest peaks, and a clipper cuts off the rest.

For FM radio, the processed sound is then combined into one signal. For stereo, left and right are added together so mono radios still play the full sound. The difference between left and right is sent as a second part of the signal, at frequencies too high to hear. A quiet tone, also too high to hear, tells a stereo radio to separate the two. A small data stream, the radio data system, can carry the station name and song titles. Emergency alerts can also travel in this signal as special tones.

Many stations also broadcast digital sound, sometimes on the analog signal's channel. A receiver may switch between the two, so they must match in level and timing. Television sound travels with the picture and must stay in time with it. Television can carry a second soundtrack, such as another language, and online streams often carry the same processed sound.

In many countries, loudness rules apply to television, so an advertisement should not be much louder than the program around it. Loudness meters measure sound close to how our ears hear it, and programs are usually adjusted to one target. In digital television, a number sent with the soundtrack often says how loud the speech is. That number is called dialogue normalization, and the receiver uses it to set the volume. Television stations often keep loudness records as proof.

The flashcards explain each part of the chain for radio, television and streaming. By the end, you will know how a program's sound travels to the listener, what changes it on the way, and which rules it must follow.
