Topic Foundations: Broadcast Air Chain, Processing and Loudness

Imagine switching between radio stations in a car. The voice on each station reaches you at a similar, steady volume. On its way to the transmitter, the sound passes through many pieces of equipment. That whole path is called the air chain. So what happens to the sound on this path?

From the studio, the studio-transmitter link carries the sound to the transmitter by radio or over the internet. Sound from events and news scenes reaches the studio the other way, over phone lines, mobile networks or satellites. Equipment at each end often makes the sound data smaller, then turns it back into sound.

At the transmitter, the sound is used to change a radio wave. In AM (amplitude modulation) radio it changes the strength of the wave, and in FM (frequency modulation) radio it changes its frequency. The law limits how large that change may be. If the loudest moments go past the limit, the signal spreads into other stations' channels and sounds distorted. That is called overmodulation.

Stations usually want a loud sound, so the on-air processor keeps it close to that limit. It keeps the level steady, in separate frequency bands. Then a limiter usually turns down the highest peaks, and a clipper cuts off the rest.

For FM radio, the processed sound is then combined into one signal. For stereo, left and right are added together so mono radios still play the full sound. The difference between left and right is sent as a second part of the signal, at frequencies too high to hear. A quiet tone, too high for most people to hear, tells a radio the station is in stereo, so it can rebuild left and right. A small data stream, the radio data system, can carry the station name and song titles.

Many stations also broadcast digital sound. A receiver may switch between the analog and digital signals, so they must match in level and timing. Online streams often carry the same sound, and television also needs a steady volume.

On television, the sound is sent together with the picture and must stay in time with it. In many countries, loudness rules apply to television, so an advertisement should not be much louder than the program around it. Loudness meters measure sound close to how our ears hear it, and programs are usually adjusted to one target. In digital television, a number sent with the soundtrack often says how loud the speech is. That number is called dialogue normalization, and the receiver uses it to set the volume. Stations often record their output all the time to prove they follow these rules.

The flashcards explain each part of the chain for radio, television and streaming. By the end, you will know how a program's sound travels to the listener, what changes it on the way, and which rules it must follow.
