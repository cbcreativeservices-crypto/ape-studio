Topic Foundations: Broadcast Air Chain, Processing & Loudness

Imagine a presenter speaking into a microphone in a city studio. Seconds later, a listener in a distant village hears the same words at a steady volume. On its way from the studio's mixing board to the transmitter, the sound passes through many pieces of equipment. That whole path is called the air chain. What happens to the sound on this path, and how does it stay free of distortion, at a steady level, and within the law?

The transmitter is often far away, on a hill. A connection called the studio-transmitter link carries the sound there, by radio or over the internet. Other links bring sound in from sports events and news scenes. Equipment at each end makes the sound data smaller and then restores it.

At the transmitter, the sound changes a radio wave, and the law limits how large that change may be. If a loud peak goes past the limit, the signal spreads into other stations' channels and sounds distorted. That is called overmodulation. A station also wants to sound loud, so a processor called the on-air processor keeps it close to the limit automatically. It keeps the level steady, often controlling low, middle and high frequency bands separately. Then a limiter turns down the highest peaks, and a clipper cuts off any that remain.

The processed sound becomes one signal for the transmitter. For stereo radio, left and right are combined so mono radios still play the full sound. A quiet high-frequency tone tells a stereo radio how to separate them again. The signal can also carry a small data stream, called the radio data system, with the station name and song titles. It also carries the data tones that start emergency alerts.

Many stations also send their sound as digital data, sometimes together with the normal analog signal on the same channel. A receiver may switch between the two versions, so they must match in level and timing. Television sound travels with the picture and can carry a second language track. The program also reaches listeners on the internet as a stream.

On every route, loudness rules apply. Without them, an advertisement can be much louder than the program before it. Meters measure loudness the way our ears hear it, and each program and advertisement is set to the same target. In many digital television systems, a number sent with the soundtrack says how loud the speech is, so the receiver can adjust each program's volume. That number is called dialogue normalization, or dialnorm. Stations keep loudness records as proof.

The flashcards explain the links, the processors, stereo signals and the data sent with them, digital radio and television, and the loudness rules and reports. By the end, you will know how a program's sound travels to the listener, what changes it on the way, and how a station proves it followed the rules.
