Topic Foundations: Broadcast Air Chain, Processing and Loudness

Imagine listening to the radio in a distant village. A presenter speaks in a city studio, and you hear the words at a steady volume. On its way to the transmitter, the sound passes through much equipment. That whole path is called the air chain. So what happens to the sound on this path?

Sound sometimes reaches the studio over links from events and news scenes. Then the studio-transmitter link carries it to the transmitter, often far away, by radio or over the internet. Engineers usually watch and control that transmitter from the studio. Equipment at each end often makes the sound data smaller, then turns it back into sound.

At the transmitter, the sound is used to change a radio wave. In AM radio it changes the strength of the wave, and in FM radio it changes its frequency. The law limits how large that change may be. If the loudest moments, the peaks, go past the limit, the signal spreads into other stations' channels and sounds distorted. That is called overmodulation.

Stations usually want a loud sound, so the on-air processor keeps the sound close to that limit. It keeps the level steady, in separate frequency bands. Then a limiter usually turns down the highest peaks, and a clipper cuts off any that remain.

For FM radio, the processed sound is then combined into one signal. For stereo, left and right are added together so mono radios still play the full sound. The difference between left and right is sent as a second part of the signal, at frequencies too high to hear. A low tone, also too high to hear, tells a stereo radio to separate the two. A small data stream, the radio data system, can carry the station name and song titles. Emergency alerts can also travel in this signal, as special tones in the sound.

Many stations also broadcast digital sound, sometimes on the same channel as the analog signal. A receiver may switch between the two, so they must match in level and timing. Television sound travels with the picture, often in the same video cable, and must stay in time with it. Television can carry a second soundtrack, such as another language, and online streams carry the same processed sound.

In many countries, loudness rules apply to television, so an advertisement should not be much louder than the program around it. Loudness meters measure sound in a way close to how our ears hear it, and each program is usually adjusted to the same target. In many digital television systems, a number sent with the soundtrack says how loud the speech is. That number is called dialogue normalization. The receiver uses it to set the volume. Television stations often keep loudness records as proof.
