Topic Foundations: Podcast Production

Imagine listening to a talk show on your phone. Two hosts and a guest talk about music, and a new episode appears in your app every week. Every word sounds clear, with no long pauses or loud clicks. So how does a conversation in a room become an episode that people listen to until the end?

It starts with a room and a plan. Heavy, sealed walls keep outside noise out, and soft panels reduce echo. Each host and guest gets a microphone and headphones. Before the session, the team writes the order of the parts of the episode and the planned length of each part. That plan is called the podcast rundown.

During the session, each voice is recorded on its own track, so the editor can adjust each person separately. Often the speakers are in different cities. A normal internet call reduces sound quality, so each person's own device records their voice in full quality, and the files are combined afterward. A service that does this is called a remote recording platform.

Not every episode is a free conversation. Some shows are read from a script, and so are audiobooks, advertisements and animated films, often by voice actors. Speed, pauses and changes in the pitch of the voice keep the listener interested. When the reader makes a mistake, they listen to the last few recorded words again and continue recording from that point. This is called punch-and-roll recording. Later, someone listens to the whole recording while reading the text, and marks every error to record again.

Next, the editor removes filler sounds such as "um", small mouth clicks and long pauses. Short pieces of music mark the change from one part to the next. Processing reduces hiss and makes the volume more even. Then the whole episode is set to a common overall loudness, so it matches other episodes. That level is called the podcast loudness target.

The finished file is uploaded to a hosting service, which lists every episode in a small text file on the internet, called a Really Simple Syndication (RSS) feed. Podcast apps check it regularly and download new episodes. Information stored with each episode gives it a title, a description, a cover picture and named sections that listeners can go to directly.

Many apps do not report what people actually play, so listening is usually counted from downloads. Those counts help shows sell advertising. An advertisement can be mixed permanently into the file. Or a server can insert it when someone downloads the episode, a method called dynamic advertisement insertion. Some apps also show when listeners stop listening, which tells the editor what to change.

The flashcards explain each of these steps in detail. By the end, you will know how an episode is planned, recorded, edited, published and measured, and why each step affects whether people keep listening.
