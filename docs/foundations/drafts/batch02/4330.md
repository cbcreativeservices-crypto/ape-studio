Topic Foundations: Strings, Guitar & Keys

Imagine a guitarist and a violinist playing the same note. The guitar note starts with a pluck and fades. The violin note can continue for as long as the bow moves. Guitars, violins, pianos and harps all make sound with a stretched string. So how does a string make its sound, and what can a careful listener hear in it?

A tight string vibrates in several patterns at once. The slowest pattern gives the note its pitch. Faster patterns add higher tones, and their mix decides the tone of the note. Real strings are slightly stiff, so those higher tones are a little too high. To correct for this, piano tuners tune high notes slightly higher and low notes slightly lower. This method is called stretched tuning.

But a string alone moves very little air. The bridge carries its vibration into a thin wooden top, which moves much more air. The body vibrates more easily at some frequencies than at others, which is one reason two guitars sound different. When a bowed note matches a strong body vibration, the note can become unsteady and rough. That note is called a wolf tone.

How the string is set moving also changes the sound. A pick, a fingernail and the skin of a fingertip each start a plucked note differently. Muting, tapping and sliding on strings add noises, such as a squeak when skin slides over a string wrapped in wire. When a vibrating string hits a metal fret, you hear a rattle called fret buzz.

A bow keeps the string moving. Rosin, a sticky substance rubbed on the bow hair, makes the hair grip the string, pull it sideways, and release it, many times each second. That grip and release is called stick-slip friction. Too little pressure gives a weak note with a hiss, and too much gives a scratching noise.

On a piano, a key sends a felt hammer up to hit the strings. Releasing the key drops a felt pad back onto the strings to stop them. A harpsichord plucks its strings instead. The moving parts of every keyboard also make clicks and knocks. When a pedal lifts every pad, strings that nobody played can vibrate when a note matches their pitch. This is called sympathetic string resonance. Many instruments from different cultures add extra strings for this reason. Others add a buzz on purpose, or let players slide the pitch up and down between notes.
