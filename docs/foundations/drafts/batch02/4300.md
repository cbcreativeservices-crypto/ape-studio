Topic Foundations: Speech and Voice Perception

Imagine talking with someone in a busy restaurant. Despite the noise, you understand almost every word. At a train station, a loud announcement can be impossible to understand. How does a voice make speech, and what decides whether a listener understands it?

In the throat, air from the lungs makes two flaps of tissue open and close many times each second. This creates a buzz. These flaps are called the vocal folds. Their speed sets the pitch. If they close loosely, air leaks out and the voice sounds breathy. They can also vibrate in different ways, for a low speaking voice or a high singing voice.

The buzz travels through the throat and mouth. This tube of air makes some frequency bands louder than others. These louder bands are called formants. Moving the tongue, jaw and lips moves the formants and changes the vowel. Pitch and formants are separate, so you can sing "ee" and "oo" on the same note.

Between the vowels, the mouth stops or narrows the air, as in "p" or "s". Where and how it does this decides the sound. Each of these sounds is called a consonant. Consonants are usually quieter than vowels, but they carry much of the meaning. Across a sentence, pitch, speed and loudness also change. That pattern is called prosody, and it can show a question or a feeling.

Real speech is not a series of separate sounds. The mouth moves toward the next sound before the last one ends, and every voice is different. Even so, the brain finds the words. It uses what it knows about words, and the speaker's face. What a person sees can even change what they hear.

That help matters most when other sounds are heard at the same time. Noise covers the quiet consonants first, so similar words are confused. In a large room, the reflections of a loud vowel can hide the next consonant. A second voice causes another problem. The brain cannot always tell which words belong to which speaker. That is called informational masking.

So rooms and sound systems are measured for clarity. Listening tests count the words people repeat correctly. A meter is faster. It plays a test sound that rises and falls in loudness, as speech does. It measures how much of that rise and fall is lost to noise and echo. The result is called the speech transmission index. It is only a prediction, so people can still miss words.
