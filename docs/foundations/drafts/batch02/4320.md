Topic Foundations: Attack, Decay, Resonance and Artifacts

Imagine a pianist playing one note in a quiet studio. You hear it begin, then fade. You may also hear the pedal move and the bench creak. Some of these sounds belong to the music, and some are faults. Naming the exact fault you hear is called artifact identification. It matters because each fault has its own cause. This topic asks: what are the parts of a sound, and what else can you hear with it?

The first part is the start. Some sounds reach full level at once, such as a drum hit. Others can begin slowly, such as a violin note. The very short burst of sound at the start of a note is called the initial transient. It often helps you recognize which instrument is playing.

Then the sound fades. A struck bell becomes quieter slowly on its own. This fading is called a natural decay. If a drummer grips the edge of a cymbal right after hitting it, the ringing stops at once. That is called a choked decay.

Why do some objects keep ringing? An object keeps ringing when it vibrates easily at certain frequencies and loses little energy. A guitar's hollow body vibrates with its strings and makes certain notes louder. That is called body resonance. Drum shells, the springs inside some reverberation units and speaker boxes also ring. This ringing is often wanted, but a frequency that rings too long is usually reduced.

Not every sound in a recording comes from the notes. A foot pedal can click or squeak. Footsteps can shake a microphone stand. Singers breathe in before a phrase, and a hard "p" can push air onto the microphone. When something loose, such as a screw, shakes against something else, the buzzing noise is called a rattle. The microphone records these sounds too.

Other faults are added inside the equipment. Any change to the shape of the sound wave, other than a change in level, is called audio distortion. In digital audio, a signal that goes above the highest level the system can store has the tops of its wave cut flat. That is called digital clipping. Very high frequencies that are not removed before recording can appear as false, lower tones. Some methods that make files smaller can make cymbals less clear. Echo and added room sound can also cause their own faults, each with its own name.

The flashcards explain the kinds of attack and the names for different kinds of hits, the ways sounds fade, the parts that resonate, and the noises of players and equipment. They also cover distortion, digital faults, the faults that effects cause, and listening practice. By the end, you will know the parts of a sound, how sounds that belong differ from faults, and the name and usual cause of each fault.
