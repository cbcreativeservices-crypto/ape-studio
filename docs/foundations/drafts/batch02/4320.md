Topic Foundations: Attack, Decay, Resonance and Artifacts

Imagine a pianist playing one note in a quiet studio. You hear it begin, then fade. You may also hear the pedal move and the bench creak. Some of these sounds belong to the music, and some are faults. This topic asks: what are the parts of a sound, and what else can you hear with it?

The first part is the start. Some sounds reach full level at once, such as a drum hit. Others can begin slowly, such as a violin note. The very short burst of sound at the start of a note is called the initial transient. It often helps you recognize which instrument is playing.

Then the sound fades. A struck bell becomes quieter slowly on its own. This fading is called a natural decay. If a drummer grips the edge of a cymbal right after hitting it, the ringing stops at once. That is called a choked decay.

Why do some objects keep ringing? An object keeps ringing when it vibrates easily at certain frequencies and loses little energy. A guitar's hollow body vibrates with its strings and makes certain notes louder. That is called body resonance. Drum shells and speaker boxes also ring. This ringing is often wanted, but a frequency that rings too long is usually reduced.

Not every sound in a recording comes from the notes. A foot pedal can click or squeak. Footsteps can shake a microphone stand. Singers breathe in before a phrase. When something loose, such as a screw, shakes against something else, the buzzing noise is called a rattle. The microphone records these sounds too.

Other faults are added inside the equipment. Any change to the shape of the sound wave, other than a change in level, is called audio distortion. In digital audio, a signal that goes above the highest level the system can store has the tops of its wave cut flat. That is called digital clipping. Digital recording and small file formats can add other false sounds too.

Effects can also change a sound in ways a careful listener hears. A compressor can make the start of a drum hit softer. A delay adds an echo, and its timing can be heard. Hearing a fault and naming its exact type is called artifact identification. It matters because each fault usually has its own cause. Listeners learn these sounds through regular listening practice, called ear training.

The flashcards explain each part in detail, from how sounds start and fade to the noises and faults that come with them. By the end, you will know the parts of a sound, how sounds that belong differ from faults, and the usual causes of common faults.
