Topic Foundations: Tonal and Timbre Descriptors

Imagine two friends listening to the same song. One says the voice sounds thin, and the other says it sounds clear. They hear the same sound but use different words. In a studio, people often describe tone with everyday words like these. Tone is what makes two sounds different even at the same pitch and level. So what does each word mean, and what change does it ask for?

Most sounds mix low, middle and high frequencies, and engineers divide them into named regions. The middle region, where voices and most instruments have most of their sound, is called the midrange. Most tone words say one region has too much or too little sound. Others describe the whole sound, such as clean, natural or vintage. Some describe the balance from low to high, or how sharply a drum hit starts.

The low regions help decide how full or thin a sound seems. With too few low frequencies, a voice seems thin. With too many, low notes continue to ring and make other sounds hard to hear. Just above is the lower midrange, where many instruments have the body of their sound. A little more sound there, with each instrument still clear, is called warm. With too much there, often together with too much low sound, instruments become hard to tell apart. That is called muddy.

Still in the lower midrange, too much sound in one narrow part makes a voice seem to come from inside a small box. People call this a boxy sound. Too much in a narrow band a little higher makes a singer sound as if they are holding their nose. That is called nasal.

Many tone words describe the high frequencies. The very highest part is often called air. When it is missing, the sound seems dull and far away. Just below the high frequencies is the upper midrange. A little more sound there can make a voice seem closer. When it is too loud, the sound is tiring to hear. That is called harsh.

A tone word does not say what caused the sound. A microphone, speaker or room often makes some frequencies louder or softer. That change is called coloration, and it is not always a fault.

A tone word is most useful when people link it to the same frequency region. A request such as "take the mud out" usually means reducing the lower midrange. So engineers practice careful listening. They hear a known change, name the frequency region it is in, and then check their answer.
