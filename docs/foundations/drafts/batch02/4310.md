Topic Foundations: Tonal & Timbre Descriptors

Imagine two singers who sing the same note at the same volume. You can still tell them apart, because each voice has its own tone. In a studio, people describe tone with everyday words, for example "the headphones sound muddy." These words are not measurements. So what does each word mean, and what change does it ask for?

The answer starts with names for parts of the sound. Every sound mixes low, middle and high frequencies, and engineers divide them into named regions. The middle region, where voices and most instruments have most of their sound, is called the midrange. Most tone words say that one region has too much sound or too little. Others describe the whole balance from low to high.

The low regions decide how large or small a sound seems. With too few low frequencies, a voice seems small. With too many, low notes continue to ring and make other sounds hard to hear. Just above is the lower midrange, where many instruments have sound. A little more sound there, with each instrument still clear, is called warm. With too much there, instruments become hard to tell apart. That is called muddy.

Still in the lower midrange, too much sound in one narrow part makes a voice seem to come from inside a small box. That is called boxy. Too much in a narrow band a little higher makes a singer sound as if they are holding their nose. That is called nasal.

There are more words for the high frequencies than for any other region. When the highest frequencies are missing, the sound seems dull and far away. When the upper midrange, just below the high frequencies, is too loud, the sound hurts the ears. That is called harsh, and several other tone words name this problem. Other words tell apart high frequencies that are pleasant from ones that tire the ears.

Not every word describes a frequency region. Some describe texture, such as roughness from added distortion. Others describe time and place: whether a drum hit starts sharply, or whether a sound is hard to hear among the other sounds. Equipment can change tone too, because a microphone, speaker or room often makes some frequencies louder or softer. That change is called coloration, and it is not always a fault.

A word helps only when the listener can find the exact frequency region. A request such as "take the mud out" usually means reducing the lower midrange. So engineers practice careful listening. They hear a change, name the region, and make the same change again by listening.

The flashcards follow the same order, from the regions and their words to texture, equipment, requests and listening exercises. By the end, you will know what engineers usually mean by each tone word, which region it describes, and why a clear word leads to a clear change.
