Topic Foundations: Loudness & Listening Environments

Imagine listening to music on a bus. You turn the volume up so you can hear it above the noise of the bus. At home that night, the same setting seems far too loud. A meter measures sound in the air, but loudness is a judgment made by your ears and brain. How is that judgment made, and how does a place change what we hear?

The judgment begins in the inner ear. It divides every sound into many narrow frequency bands. Each band adds its own share of loudness, called specific loudness. The shares are added into one total. So a sound spread over many frequencies seems louder than the same energy in one band. Computer models copy these steps, and listening tests check the models.

The total also changes with time. A very short sound seems quieter than a longer one. A soft, steady, high tone can seem to fade the longer you hear it. That fading is called loudness adaptation. A loud sound can also make the next soft sound seem softer.

Most of the time we listen with two ears, and a sound then seems louder than with one ear, but not twice as loud. This is called binaural summation. A damaged ear may not hear soft sounds, yet loud sounds seem almost normal. So in that ear, loudness rises very fast as the volume increases. That is called loudness recruitment.

Having two ears also gives us information about the space around us. In a hall, reflections from the side walls make each ear hear a slightly different sound. Early side reflections make the source seem wider. Later ones give the feeling that sound surrounds you, called listener envelopment. Two ears can still confuse a sound in front with one behind, because both ears receive it the same way. With ordinary headphones, much of this information is missing, so the sound can seem to be inside your head.

A place can also sound pleasant or annoying, and level is only one part. Many high frequencies, a regular rise and fall in level, or one clear tone inside a noise all annoy people more. Speech in noise or in a room with many echoes takes extra mental work to understand. That work is called listening effort. Background speech also takes mental work, so it makes thinking harder.
