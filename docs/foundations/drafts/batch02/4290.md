Topic Foundations: Loudness and Listening Environments

Imagine a radio playing in a quiet kitchen. Then someone starts a blender, and you can no longer hear the radio. The radio did not change, but what you hear did, because the new noise hides it. How do your ears and brain decide loudness, and how does a place change what we hear?

The inner ear divides every sound into many narrow frequency bands. Each band adds its own part of the loudness. That part is called specific loudness. When a noise fills the same bands, the radio's part becomes smaller or disappears. The parts add up, so sound spread over many bands often seems louder than the same energy in one band. Loudness models calculate loudness in this same band-by-band way. Time matters too: a very short sound seems quieter than a longer one at the same level.

A damaged ear does this work differently. It may miss soft sounds, yet loud sounds seem almost normal. So loudness rises very fast as the volume increases. That is called loudness recruitment. With two healthy ears, a sound seems louder than with one, but not twice as loud. This is called binaural summation.

Two ears also tell us where a sound is. A sound from one side reaches the nearer ear first. When an echo arrives soon after a sound, we usually hear one sound, placed where the first came from. A much later echo is heard as a second sound. A sound in front and a sound behind reach both ears almost the same way. The shape of the outer ear and small head turns usually tell them apart.

In a hall, side-wall reflections make each ear hear a slightly different sound. Early side reflections make the source seem wider. Later ones give the feeling that sound surrounds you, called listener envelopment. How long reflections last also helps us judge room size.

Some sounds are also more unpleasant than others at the same loudness. Listeners can rate loudness with numbers or words, but loudness is only one part of comfort. Loud high frequencies, a level that keeps rising and falling, or one clear tone inside a noise usually annoys people more than steady noise at the same level. Understanding speech in noise or heavy echoes takes extra mental work. That work is called listening effort, and many hours of it are tiring.

The whole place matters too. In a park or street, sounds such as birds, water or voices are judged together. People rate how pleasant the place is, and how eventful, meaning how much is happening. In a quiet office, nearby speech can take attention away from work.
