Topic Foundations: Loudness and Listening Environments

Imagine a radio playing in a quiet kitchen. Then someone starts a blender, and you can no longer hear the radio. The radio did not change. Only your judgment of it changed. A meter measures sound in the air, but loudness is a judgment made by your ears and brain. How is that judgment made, and how does a place change what we hear?

The judgment begins in the inner ear, which divides every sound into many narrow frequency bands. Each band adds its own part of the loudness. That part is called specific loudness. The parts are added into one total. So a sound spread over many frequencies often seems louder than the same energy in one narrow band. The total also changes with time. At the same level, a very short sound seems quieter than a longer one. A soft, steady, high tone can seem to fade the longer you hear it. That fading is called loudness adaptation.

With two ears, a sound seems louder than with one, but not twice as loud. This is called binaural summation. Hearing damage can also change how each ear builds loudness. A damaged ear may miss soft sounds, yet loud sounds seem almost normal. So loudness rises very fast as the volume increases. That is called loudness recruitment.

Two ears also tell us where a sound is. A sound in front and the same sound behind can be easy to confuse. That is called front-back confusion. Ordinary headphones lack much of this information, so sound can seem inside your head.

A room adds more information. In a hall, reflections from the side walls make each ear hear a slightly different sound. Early side reflections make the source seem wider. Later ones give the feeling that sound surrounds you, called listener envelopment.

Listeners can rate how loud a sound seems, or which level feels most comfortable. Yet level is only one part of comfort. A lot of sound at high frequencies, a level that rises and falls again and again, or one clear tone inside a noise usually annoys people more. Speech in noise or strong echoes takes extra mental work to understand. That work is called listening effort. After many hours of it, the ears and brain become tired. Unwanted speech can also take attention away from a task.

Because comfort depends on more than level, a good public place is often not silent. Its sounds, such as birds, water or voices, are judged together. People rate how pleasant those sounds are, and how eventful the place seems, meaning how much is happening. They also judge whether the sounds fit the place.
