Topic Foundations: Loudness and Listening Environments

Imagine a radio playing in a quiet kitchen. Then someone starts a blender, and you can no longer hear the radio. The radio did not change. A meter measures sound in the air, but loudness is decided by your ears and brain. How do they decide it, and how does a place change what we hear?

The process begins in the inner ear, which divides every sound into many narrow frequency bands. Each band adds its own part of the loudness. That part is called specific loudness. The parts add up, so a sound spread over many frequencies often seems louder than the same energy in one narrow band. A very short sound also seems quieter than a longer one at the same level.

Hearing damage can also change how loud sounds seem to each ear. A damaged ear may miss soft sounds, yet loud sounds seem almost normal. So loudness rises very fast as the volume increases. That is called loudness recruitment. With two healthy ears, a sound seems louder than with one, but not twice as loud. This is called binaural summation.

Two ears also tell us where a sound is. A sound from one side reaches the nearer ear earlier and louder. When a sound and its echo arrive close together, we usually hear one sound, placed where the first sound came from. A much later echo is heard as a second sound. A sound in front and the same sound behind can still be easy to confuse. Two ears also help us follow one voice among many, using the ear that hears it more clearly.

A room adds more information. In a hall, reflections from the side walls make each ear hear a slightly different sound. Early side reflections make the source seem wider. Later ones give the feeling that sound surrounds you, called listener envelopment.

All of this changes how comfortable a place is to listen in. Listeners can rate the most comfortable level, yet level is only one part of comfort. A lot of high-frequency sound, a level that rises and falls again and again, or one clear tone inside a noise usually annoys people more. Speech in noise or strong echoes takes extra mental work to understand. That work is called listening effort, and after many hours of it, the ears and brain become tired.

Because comfort depends on more than level, a good public place is often not silent. Its sounds, such as birds, water or voices, are judged together. People rate how pleasant those sounds are, and how eventful the place seems, meaning how much is happening.
