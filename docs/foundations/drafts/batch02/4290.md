Topic Foundations: Loudness and Listening Environments

Imagine a radio playing in a quiet kitchen. Then someone starts a blender, and you can no longer hear the radio. The radio did not change; the new noise hides it. A meter measures sound in the air, but loudness is decided by your ears and brain. How do they decide it, and how does a place change what we hear?

The inner ear divides every sound into many narrow frequency bands. Each band adds its own part of the loudness. That part is called specific loudness. When a noise fills the same bands, the radio's part becomes smaller or disappears. The parts add up, so sound spread over many bands often seems louder than the same energy in one band. Time matters too: a very short sound seems quieter than a longer one at the same level.

A damaged ear does this work differently. It may miss soft sounds, yet loud sounds seem almost normal. So loudness rises very fast as the volume increases. That is called loudness recruitment. With two healthy ears, a sound seems louder than with one, but not twice as loud. This is called binaural summation.

Two ears also tell us where a sound is. A sound from one side reaches the nearer ear first. When an echo arrives soon after a sound, we usually hear one sound, placed where the first came from. A much later echo is heard as a second sound. A sound in front and the same sound behind reach both ears almost the same way. The shape of the outer ear and small head turns usually tell them apart, but without these the brain can confuse them.

In a hall, side-wall reflections make each ear hear a slightly different sound. Early side reflections make the source seem wider. Later ones give the feeling that sound surrounds you, called listener envelopment. How long reflections last also helps us judge room size.

Where we hear a sound also affects comfort. Loudness, which listeners can rate with numbers or words, is only one part of it. Strong high frequencies, a level that keeps rising and falling, or one clear tone inside a noise usually annoys people more than steady noise at the same level. Understanding speech in noise or strong echoes takes extra mental work. That work is called listening effort, and many hours of it are tiring.

The whole place matters too. In a park or street, sounds such as birds, water or voices are judged together, and nearby speech can take attention away from a task. People rate how pleasant the place is, and how eventful, meaning how much is happening.
