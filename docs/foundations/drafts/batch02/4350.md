Topic Foundations: Winds, Brass and Organ

Imagine blowing across the top of an empty bottle. At first you hear only air. Then a clear note appears. Flutes, trumpets, saxophones and pipe organs all begin with a stream of air. This topic asks how moving air becomes a note, and what else a listener hears while it happens.

Something must start the air vibrating. On a flute, the air hits a sharp edge. On a clarinet, a thin reed opens and closes very fast. On a trumpet, the player's lips buzz. The way a player shapes the mouth on the instrument is called the embouchure. A reed that swings through a slot without closing, as in a harmonica, is called a free reed.

Except in free reeds, the vibration does not choose the note. The air inside the tube sets the note, and a longer tube gives a lower note. The inside shape of the tube, straight or cone-shaped, is called the bore shape. It changes which higher tones, called overtones, are heard, and so does closing one end of the tube. Open holes along the side, called tone holes, make the vibrating column of air shorter. To change the length, brass instruments use valves or a slide.

Each range of notes has its own mix of overtones. Loud brass notes gain more high frequencies, not only more level. High frequencies leave mostly straight out of the bell, so a trumpet sounds different from the side. A mute, an object placed in the bell, also changes the tone.

Players also change the sound on purpose. The tongue can start a note sharply or softly. A note can slide up into place, or slide down at its end. Humming while blowing makes the note rough and buzzy. Some bamboo flutes keep breath noise on purpose, and many instruments play one steady low note while the melody plays.

Other sounds are not planned. A reed can squeak on a higher note than the one the player chose. A brass note can jump to the wrong pitch for an instant. Keys and valves click, and water gurgles in the tubing. So the listener asks: is this sound part of the music, or a mistake?

A pipe organ uses the same physics with many pipes and a fan that pushes air. Many pipes begin with a short puff of noise, called chiff. Controls on the organ switch sets of pipes, called stops, on and off. Choosing and combining stops is called organ registration. Electric organs with spinning speakers add a sound that rises and falls slightly in pitch and level.
