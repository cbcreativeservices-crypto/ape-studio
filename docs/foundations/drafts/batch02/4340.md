Topic Foundations: Drums & Percussion

Imagine a street parade. A large drum plays a low beat, metal plates ring at a higher pitch, and someone shakes a rattle. Each sound comes from an object that is hit, shaken or scraped, and each part of that sound has a name. So when you hear a percussion sound, what made it, and what is each part called?

The answer starts with the object. A stretched skin, a metal plate or a wooden bar moves in many patterns when it is hit. On many drums these patterns do not fit one clear note, so the pitch is weak. Makers can cut wood from under a bar, or add paste to a skin, until a clear note appears. Where the player hits decides which tones you hear. This is called the strike position.

These tones also change over time. On a drum kit, each hit has a short, sharp start, a louder main part, and a fading ring. The kick drum gives a click, then a low sound you feel in your chest. Under the snare drum, metal wires rattle against the skin. That rattle is called snare buzz. Microphones also record the unwanted buzzes and squeaks of the kit.

On cymbals, this change over time is easier to hear. A cymbal moves in so many patterns that it has no clear note. After the start, its sound grows louder, then fades slowly. When it keeps ringing, separate hits join into one continuous sound, called cymbal wash. Two hi-hat cymbals pressed together give a short tick. Held apart, they ring longer.

Hands make even more sounds than sticks. A flat hand in the center of a hand drum gives the lowest sound. Fingers that hit the edge and lift off let the skin ring with a clear note, called an open tone. Fingers that snap onto the edge give a short, loud crack. Many traditions give each stroke a spoken syllable. On the tabla, a pair of drums from India, this syllable is called a bol. Other drums play in groups, and each drum plays a part that responds to the others.

Instruments played with mallets, sticks with a round head, add one more choice: the beater. A hard tip gives a sharper start and more high frequencies. A soft tip gives more low frequencies and less click. This is called mallet hardness. Metal bars ring for a long time, while wooden bars stop quickly. In shakers and scrapers, small beads hit a shell, or a stick moves over ridges, making many fast clicks.

The flashcards follow this path, from why struck objects ring, through the drum kit, cymbals and hand drums, to mallet instruments and small percussion. By the end, you will know what made a percussion sound, which part of the hit you are hearing, and what that part is called.
