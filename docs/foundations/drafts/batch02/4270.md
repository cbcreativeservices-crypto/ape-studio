Topic Foundations: Hearing Anatomy, Health and Disorders

Imagine a friend says your name across a quiet room. You turn toward the voice. This feels simple, but many small parts work in sequence. So this topic asks how the ear turns sound into hearing, and what happens when one part fails.

The path of sound starts at the outer ear, the part you can see. It collects sound and sends it down the ear canal to the eardrum, a thin sheet that moves with the sound. Behind it, in the air space of the middle ear, tiny bones carry the movement inward. These bones are called the ossicles. They also increase the pressure, because sound in air does not easily move the fluid of the inner ear.

The last bone pushes on an opening into the inner ear. There, a coiled tube full of fluid, called the cochlea, turns the movement into nerve signals. High-pitched sounds move a thin strip near its start, and low-pitched sounds near its far end. Hair cells on the strip send a signal when their tiny hairs bend. Very loud sound can destroy these cells, and in people they do not grow back, so the damage is usually permanent. The signals travel along the hearing nerve to the brain.

To find which part has failed, a specialist first looks into the ear canal. Then the person signals each time they hear a soft tone. The softest tone they hear at each pitch is marked on a chart called an audiogram. Tones can also be sent through the skull bone, skipping the outer and middle ear. Other tests do not need the person to respond, so they can be used with sleeping babies.

The air and bone results show where the problem is. If sound through the air is heard poorly but sound through the bone is heard normally, the problem is in the outer or middle ear. That is called conductive hearing loss, and causes such as wax or fluid can often be treated. If both are equally poor, the problem is in the inner ear or the nerve. That is called sensorineural hearing loss. Loud sound, age and some medicines are common causes. Some people hear quiet tones normally but still often miss speech in noise.

Some other problems are hard to measure with tests. A ringing or buzzing with no outside sound is called tinnitus. For some people, ordinary sounds feel far too loud or painful. The inner ear also holds the balance organs, which share its fluids. So some inner ear conditions cause attacks of dizziness, when the room seems to spin, and reduced hearing.
