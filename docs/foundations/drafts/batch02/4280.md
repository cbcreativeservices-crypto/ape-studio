Topic Foundations: Psychoacoustics

Imagine playing a song late at night with the volume very low. The bass seems to disappear. Nothing in the recording changed, and a meter would show the same balance. The difference is in the listener. The study of how people perceive sound is called psychoacoustics. It asks how the ear and brain turn changes in air pressure into what we actually hear.

Loudness comes first. The ear is most sensitive to middle frequencies. Low and high frequencies need more level to seem equally loud, especially at low volume. A graph of sounds that seem equally loud is called an equal-loudness contour. Hearing also has limits: the quietest sound we can hear, the level that causes pain, and the smallest change we can notice.

The inner ear also divides sound into many narrow frequency bands. When a loud sound and a quieter sound are in the same band, the quieter one can become impossible to hear. This is called masking. A loud sound can also hide a quiet sound just after it.

The same bands help the brain hear pitch. Most notes contain many frequencies, spaced evenly above the lowest one. The brain hears the whole pattern as one note. If the lowest frequency is removed, the brain still hears that low pitch from the spacing of the others. This is called the missing fundamental.

Having two ears also lets you find where a sound comes from. A sound from the left reaches the left ear slightly earlier and louder, because the head blocks part of it. The folds of the outer ear change sounds from above, below, in front and behind in different ways. In a room, reflections arrive soon after the direct sound. The brain usually uses the direction of the first arrival. This is called the precedence effect.

Many sounds often arrive at the same time, so the brain must decide which parts belong together. Parts that start together, or fit one evenly spaced pattern, are heard as one source. So you can listen to one voice in a crowded room. The brain also predicts what comes next, such as the next beat or the next note.

Expectation can also change what you hear. A louder version often sounds better, and expensive equipment can seem better because you expect it to. A fair listening test matches the levels and hides which sound is which. When neither the listener nor the person giving the test knows which sound is which, it is called double-blind testing. Many attempts then show whether a difference is real.
