Topic Foundations: Psychoacoustics

Imagine a friend with a low voice calls you on the phone. A phone line does not carry the lowest frequencies of that voice. Yet you still hear the same low pitch, and you know at once who is speaking. The study of how people perceive sound is called psychoacoustics. It asks how the ear and brain turn changes in air pressure into what we actually hear.

The answer begins in the inner ear, which divides sound into many narrow frequency bands. Most notes contain many frequencies, spaced evenly above the lowest one. The brain hears this pattern as one pitch. On the phone, the lowest frequency is gone, but the even spacing remains. So the brain still hears the low pitch. This is called the missing fundamental.

The same bands explain why one sound can hide another. When a loud sound and a quieter sound are in the same band, the quieter one can become impossible to hear. This is called masking.

So the ear does not measure sound the way a meter does. Loudness also depends on frequency. The ear is most sensitive to middle frequencies. Low and high frequencies need more level to seem equally loud, especially at low volume. A graph of sounds that seem equally loud is called an equal-loudness contour. Hearing also has limits, such as the quietest sound we can hear.

With two ears, the brain can also compare what each ear hears to find where a sound comes from. A sound from the left reaches the left ear slightly earlier. The head blocks part of the sound, so the left ear often hears it a little louder. In a room, reflections arrive soon after the direct sound. The brain usually uses the direction of the first arrival. This is called the precedence effect.

Direction is one clue the brain uses to sort sounds. Several sounds usually arrive at the same time, so the brain must decide which parts belong together. Parts that start together, or fit one evenly spaced pattern, are heard as one source. So you can listen to one instrument while a whole band plays. The brain also predicts what comes next, such as the next beat.

Because the brain predicts, expectation can also change what you hear. A louder version often sounds better, and expensive equipment can seem better because you expect it to. A fair listening test matches the levels. When neither the listener nor the person giving the test knows which sound is which, it is called double-blind testing.
