Topic Foundations: Loudspeaker Types and Drivers

Imagine a phone speaker, headphones, and the tall speakers at a concert. They look different, but they do the same job. Each one receives an electrical signal and moves the air, and the moving air reaches your ears as sound. So how does a speaker move the air, and why are there so many kinds?

Most speakers use a magnet and a coil of wire. The coil sits in a narrow gap in the magnet. The music signal flows through the coil, and the magnet pushes it forward and back. That coil is called the voice coil. It moves a light cone, and the cone moves the air. Flexible parts keep the cone centered and bring it back to rest. One complete unit of this kind is called a driver.

The cone should move as one solid piece. At high frequencies, parts of it bend separately and add unwanted extra sounds. So designers choose materials that are stiff and light, and some that absorb their own vibration. Paper, plastic, carbon fiber and metal each balance these needs differently.

Even so, one driver cannot cover all frequencies well. Low frequencies usually need a large cone that moves a lot of air. High frequencies need a small, light part that moves very fast. The large driver for low frequencies is called a woofer. The small driver for high frequencies is called a tweeter. A circuit sends each driver mainly its own frequencies. Some add a third driver for the middle, and a subwoofer plays the lowest notes. Others place the tweeter in the center of the woofer. For very loud sound, a horn makes a small driver louder and controls where its sound goes.

All of these drivers use a coil to move a cone or a small dome. Other designs move a thin sheet instead. A speaker with a thin charged sheet between two metal grids is called an electrostatic loudspeaker. The signal moves the whole sheet at once. Other designs use a sheet with a printed wire pattern, a thin metal strip, or a crystal that bends. Some vibrate a flat panel.

Drivers are built into speakers for different places. A speaker that changes the sound as little as possible, so engineers can judge a mix, is called a studio monitor. On stage, speakers on the floor point back at the musicians. For a large audience, many cabinets are hung or stacked in a tall line. Other speakers fit into ceilings and walls, and headphones put the sound at the ears.
