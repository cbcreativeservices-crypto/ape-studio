Topic Foundations: Close-Miking Guitars, Amps and Strings

Put your ear close to an acoustic guitar while a friend plays. Near the round hole, you hear more low frequencies. Near the neck, you hear more high frequencies. Engineers use the word miking for placing microphones. A microphone very close to an instrument picks up mostly that instrument. But it also records these differences, because each part sends out a different sound. So where should the microphone point, and why does a small move change the recording?

These differences are easiest to see with an electric guitar, because its sound comes from one speaker in the amplifier's cabinet. The center of the speaker sends the most high frequencies straight ahead, and the outer edge sends fewer. So moving the microphone from the center toward the edge records fewer high frequencies. That movement is called speaker-cone traversal. Turning the microphone to point beside the center does something similar. This method is called off-axis cabinet miking.

A second microphone can sit beside the first, farther back in the room, or behind an open-back cabinet. But sound reaches each microphone at a slightly different time, so some frequencies can cancel when the signals are mixed. A bass is often recorded with a microphone on its amplifier and a second signal taken straight from the instrument. These two signals also usually work best when they arrive together.

An acoustic guitar has no speaker. Its whole wooden body vibrates, and the round hole in the top pushes out many low frequencies. A microphone aimed straight at that hole often records too many, so the low frequencies cover the rest of the sound. Keeping the microphone away from it is called sound-hole avoidance. A common starting point is the twelfth fret, a metal strip across the neck. This is called twelfth-fret miking. The same ideas often guide miking a ukulele or a banjo.

On bowed instruments, from the violin to the double bass, the f-shaped holes push out low frequencies. Near the bridge, a microphone picks up more of the start of each note and the noise of the bow. Over the fingerboard, it picks up fewer high frequencies. Because the sound comes from the whole instrument, the microphone usually stays farther away. Miking bowed instruments like these is called string miking.

On a loud stage, even a close microphone picks up other instruments. One solution is a tiny microphone inside the instrument's body, called an internal microphone. It picks up less from other instruments, but its sound is less natural, so it is often mixed with a microphone outside the instrument.

The flashcards explain each of these positions in detail, from the speaker cabinet to the inside of an instrument. Each is a common starting point, not the one correct setting. By the end, you will know how the position of a close microphone changes the sound of guitars, amplifiers and strings.
