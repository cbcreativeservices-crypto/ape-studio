Topic Foundations: Close-Miking Guitars, Amps and Strings

Imagine a guitarist playing in a small room. A microphone across the room picks up the guitar, the walls and every other instrument. A microphone very close picks up mostly the guitar. But at that short distance, each part of an instrument sends out a different sound. So where should the microphone point, and why does a small move change the recording?

An electric guitar is the clearest example, because its sound comes from a speaker in the amplifier's cabinet. The center of the speaker sends the most high frequencies straight ahead, and the outer edge sends fewer. So moving the microphone from the center toward the edge records fewer high frequencies. That movement is called speaker-cone traversal. Turning the microphone to point beside the center does something similar. Miking means placing microphones, and this method is called off-axis cabinet miking.

One microphone is often not enough. A second one can sit beside it, farther back in the room, or behind an open-back cabinet. But sound reaches each microphone at a slightly different time, so some frequencies can cancel when the signals are mixed. A bass is often recorded with a microphone on its amplifier and also directly from the instrument's output, and these two signals usually need to arrive together.

An acoustic guitar has no speaker. Its whole wooden body vibrates, and the round hole in the top pushes out many low frequencies. A microphone aimed straight at that hole often records too many, so the notes sound unclear. Keeping the microphone away from it is called sound-hole avoidance. A common starting point is the twelfth fret, a metal strip across the neck. This is called twelfth-fret miking. Other plucked instruments, such as the ukulele or the banjo, are usually miked in a similar way.

On bowed instruments, from the violin to the double bass, the f-shaped holes push out low frequencies. Near the bridge, a microphone picks up more of the start of each note and the noise of the bow. Over the fingerboard, it picks up fewer high frequencies. Because the sound comes from the whole instrument, the microphone usually stays a little farther away. Placing microphones on bowed instruments like these is called string miking.

On a loud stage, even a close microphone picks up other instruments. One solution is a tiny microphone inside the instrument's body, called an internal microphone. It picks up less from other instruments, but its sound is less natural, so it is often mixed with a pickup.

Each is a common starting point, not the one correct setting. By the end, you will know how the position of a close microphone changes the sound of guitars, amplifiers and strings.
