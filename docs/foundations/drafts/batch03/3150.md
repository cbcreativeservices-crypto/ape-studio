Topic Foundations: Close-Miking Guitars, Amps and Strings

Put your ear close to an acoustic guitar while a friend plays. Near the round hole, you hear more low frequencies. Near the neck, you hear more high frequencies. A microphone very close to an instrument picks up mostly that instrument, but it also records these differences. So in miking, the placing of microphones, where should each one point, and why does a small move change the recording?

These differences are easiest to see on an electric guitar amplifier, where the microphone usually aims at one speaker. The center of the speaker sends the most high frequencies straight ahead, and the outer edge sends fewer. So moving the microphone from the center toward the edge records fewer high frequencies. That movement is called speaker-cone traversal. Turning the microphone away from the center does something similar. This method is called off-axis cabinet miking.

A second microphone can sit beside the first, farther back, or behind an open-back cabinet. But sound reaches each one at a slightly different time, so mixing them can cancel some frequencies. A bass is often recorded with a microphone on its amplifier and a second signal taken straight from the instrument. These two usually work best when they arrive together. A guitar can also be recorded clean and later played through an amplifier to be miked.

An acoustic guitar has no speaker. Its wooden body vibrates, and the round hole pushes out many low frequencies. A microphone aimed straight at that hole often records so many that the rest of the sound is hard to hear. Keeping the microphone away from it is called sound-hole avoidance. A common starting point is the twelfth fret, a metal strip across the neck. This is called twelfth-fret miking. The same ideas often guide miking a ukulele, an oud and other small instruments with a sound hole.

On bowed instruments, from the violin to the double bass, the f-shaped holes push out low frequencies. Near the bridge, a microphone picks up more of the start of each note and the noise of the bow. Over the fingerboard, it records fewer high frequencies. Because the sound comes from the whole instrument, the microphone usually stays farther away. Miking these instruments is called string miking.

On a loud stage, a close microphone still picks up other instruments. An amplifier can sit in a closed, padded box with its microphone. An acoustic instrument can hold a tiny microphone inside, called an internal microphone. Its sound is less natural, so it is often mixed with an outside microphone, or with a pickup that senses the strings.
