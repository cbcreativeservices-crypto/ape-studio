Topic Foundations: Stereo and Ensemble Miking Techniques

Imagine sitting in a concert hall with your eyes closed. You can still point to the violins on the left and the cellos on the right. One microphone usually records one channel, so left and right are lost. Its distance also decides how much of the room it picks up. So how can several microphones record where each player is, and the room around them?

Your ears use two clues. A sound on your left is a little louder in your left ear, and arrives there earlier. Two directional microphones can sit at almost the same point, aimed left and right. Sound reaches both at the same time, so only the level difference shows direction. This is called a coincident pair. In another coincident setup, one microphone faces the front. A second one picks up the left and right sides equally, but not the front or back. Adding and subtracting their signals makes left and right. This is called mid-side.

Other setups use the second clue. Two microphones stand apart, so a sound reaches the nearer one first. This is called a spaced pair. A very large gap can leave the center sounding empty, with instruments pulled toward the left or right. Angled pairs set a short distance apart record both clues. A model head with a microphone in each ear also records both clues. This is called binaural recording, and it is made for headphones.

For a large orchestra, a common solution hangs three spaced microphones above the players. The middle one sits a little forward, so the center does not sound empty. Extra microphones far to each side, called outriggers, often widen the outer sections.

For a soft flute at the back, engineers add a close microphone on one player or section, called a spot microphone. Every microphone also picks up other instruments. That unwanted sound is called bleed. Placing each microphone much closer to its own instrument than to others keeps bleed low. When the same sound reaches two microphones at different times, mixing them cancels some frequencies.

Timing matters in a second way. Sound reaches a spot microphone before the main microphones. We hear a sound as coming from where it arrives first, so the flute can seem to move forward. So engineers often delay each spot microphone's signal to arrive with the main ones, or slightly later.

Close and main microphones record little of the room itself, so microphones farther back record its reflections and applause. For surround sound, more microphones face the back of the hall, and some point upward. They are spaced apart, so each loudspeaker receives a different signal.
