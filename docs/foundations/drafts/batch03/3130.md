Topic Foundations: Stereo & Ensemble Miking Techniques

Imagine sitting in a concert hall with your eyes closed. You can still point to the violins on the left and the cellos on the right. A microphone close to an instrument picks up mostly that instrument, and one farther away picks up the room too. But one microphone gives one channel, without left and right. So how can several microphones record where each player is, and the room around them?

Your ears use two clues. A sound on your left is a little louder in your left ear, and it arrives there a little earlier. Two directional microphones can sit at almost the same point, aimed left and right. Sound reaches both at the same time, so only the level difference shows direction. This is called a coincident pair.

Other setups use the second clue. Two microphones stand apart, so a sound reaches the nearer one first. That arrangement is called a spaced pair. A very large gap can make instruments in the center sound weak. Angled pairs set a short distance apart record both clues. The angle and distance between the microphones decide how wide the recording sounds.

One pair may not cover the whole width of a large orchestra. A common solution hangs three spaced microphones above the orchestra. The middle one sits a little forward, so instruments in the center do not sound weak. Extra microphones far to the left and right, called outrigger microphones, often widen the outer sections.

Even then, a soft flute at the back may sound too distant. So engineers add a close microphone on one player or section, called a spot microphone. Every microphone also picks up other instruments. That unwanted sound is called bleed. When the same sound reaches two microphones at different times, mixing them cancels some frequencies and changes the tone.

That timing difference matters in a second way. Sound reaches a spot microphone before the main microphones. We hear a sound as coming from where it arrives first, so the flute can seem to move forward. Engineers therefore delay each spot microphone, so its sound arrives together with the main microphones, or slightly later.

The last part is the sound of the room. Microphones far back in the hall, in a balcony, or facing the audience each record a different mix of room reflections and applause. For surround sound, more microphones face the back of the hall. Some point upward to record sound from above. They are spaced apart, so each loudspeaker receives a different signal.

The flashcards explain each part: placing microphones and controlling bleed, the stereo pairs, main arrays, spot microphones, their timing, and room and surround setups. By the end, you will know how microphones record direction, width and depth, and how many of them combine into a natural recording of a whole ensemble.
