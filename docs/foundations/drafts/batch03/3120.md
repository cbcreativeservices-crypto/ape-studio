Topic Foundations: Microphone Specs and Characteristics

Imagine two microphones in a shop. They look almost the same, but one records much better. Each comes with a sheet of numbers that describes how it performs. So this topic asks two questions. What does the sheet tell you, and what else helps a microphone work well?

The first number answers a simple question. When a sound of a fixed loudness reaches the microphone, how much electrical signal comes out? That number is called the sensitivity rating. A higher rating gives a stronger signal, so the next amplifier must increase it less. The sheet also lists impedance, which describes how the output resists electrical current, and which inputs suit it. A graph shows how evenly it responds to low and high frequencies.

A strong signal is not enough, because every microphone makes a faint hiss of its own. That hiss is called self-noise. On the sheet, a lower self-noise number means a quieter microphone. At very high levels, the signal distorts, which means its shape changes. The range between the hiss and the loudest clean sound is called the total dynamic range.

These numbers are only useful when every maker measures them the same way. So international standards describe each test. Even then, two microphones of the same model can differ slightly. For stereo recording, two microphones are often chosen so their level and tone match closely. Test benches also check distortion, noise, and how microphones handle heat and damp air.

The sheet describes one setting, but many microphones have switches that change it. A switch that lowers the signal by a fixed amount is called a pad. It stops a very loud drum from overloading the circuit inside the microphone or the next amplifier. A low-cut filter removes low rumble from traffic or footsteps. A pattern switch changes the directions the microphone picks up best. Some digital microphones let you change these settings from the mixer.

A good setting can still fail because of what touches the microphone. A stand, a long arm, and a counterweight hold it in position. But the vibration of footsteps and bumps passes up the stand and is recorded as low sounds. So the microphone often sits in a holder with elastic bands, called a shockmount. Moving air also causes noise. Outdoors, foam, fur, or a hollow cage keeps most wind away from the microphone. Indoors, a thin screen called a pop filter stops the bursts of air in "p" and "b" sounds. Covers protect it from rain, dust, and moisture.

The flashcards explain sensitivity and noise, how microphones are tested and matched, their switches and remote controls, stands and mounts, and protection from wind, breath, dust, and the weather. By the end, you will know what the sheet tells you, and how settings, mounting, and protection help a microphone perform as its sheet describes.
