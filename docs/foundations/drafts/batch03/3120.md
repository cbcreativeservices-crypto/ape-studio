Topic Foundations: Microphone Specs and Characteristics

Imagine two microphones in a shop. They look almost the same, but one is quieter and handles loud sounds better. Each comes with a sheet of numbers that describes how it performs. So what does the sheet tell you, and what else helps a microphone work well?

The first number answers a simple question. When a sound of a fixed loudness reaches the microphone, how much electrical signal comes out? That number is called the sensitivity rating. A higher rating gives a stronger signal, so the next amplifier must increase it less. Next to it, a graph shows how evenly the microphone responds to low and high frequencies. The sheet then lists the output impedance. Most professional microphones have a low impedance, which lets their signal travel along long cables with little loss.

A strong signal is not enough, because every microphone makes a faint hiss of its own. That hiss is called self-noise. A lower self-noise number means a quieter microphone. At very high levels, the signal distorts, which means its shape changes. The range between the hiss and the loudest clean sound is called the total dynamic range.

These numbers are most useful when every maker measures them the same way. So international standards describe each test. Makers also test how heat, damp air and shocks change these numbers. Even then, two microphones of the same model can differ slightly. So for stereo recording, makers often test and sell microphones in pairs whose level and frequency response match closely.

The sheet describes one setting, but many microphones have switches that change it. A switch that lowers the signal by a fixed amount is called a pad. It stops a loud drum from overloading the microphone's own circuit or the next amplifier. A low-cut filter removes low rumble from traffic or footsteps. A pattern switch changes the directions it picks up best. Some digital microphones let you change these settings from the mixer.

Even with the right settings, a microphone can record noise from its stand and from moving air. A stand, or a long arm or pole called a boom, holds the microphone in position. But the vibration of footsteps and bumps can pass up the stand and be recorded as low sounds. So the microphone often sits in a holder with elastic bands, called a shockmount. Outdoors, a cover made of foam or fur keeps most wind away from it. In front of a singer, a thin screen called a pop filter stops the bursts of air in "p" and "b" sounds. Other covers protect it from rain and dust.

The flashcards explain the numbers on the sheet, the switches on the microphone, and the parts that hold and protect it. By the end, you will know what the sheet tells you, and how settings, mounting, and protection help a microphone perform as its sheet describes.
