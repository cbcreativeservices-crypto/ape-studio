Topic Foundations: Microphone Specs and Characteristics

Imagine two microphones in a shop. They look almost the same, but one records much better. Each comes with a sheet of numbers that describes how it performs. So what does the sheet tell you, and what else helps a microphone work well?

The first number answers a simple question. When a sound of a fixed loudness reaches the microphone, how much electrical signal comes out? That number is called the sensitivity rating. A higher rating gives a stronger signal, so the next amplifier must increase it less. A graph also shows how evenly the microphone responds to low and high frequencies. The sheet also lists impedance, which describes how strongly the output resists the flow of current. Most professional microphones have a low impedance, so their signal can travel along long cables.

A strong signal is not enough, because every microphone makes a faint hiss of its own. That hiss is called self-noise. A lower self-noise number means a quieter microphone. At very high levels, the signal distorts, which means its shape changes. The range between the hiss and the loudest clean sound is called the total dynamic range.

These numbers are only useful when every maker measures them the same way. So international standards describe each test. Even then, two microphones of the same model can differ slightly. So for stereo recording, makers often test and sell two microphones as a pair whose level and frequency response match closely.

The sheet describes one setting, but many microphones have switches that change it. A switch that lowers the signal by a fixed amount is called a pad. It stops a very loud drum from overloading the circuit inside the microphone or the next amplifier. A low-cut filter removes low rumble from traffic or footsteps. A pattern switch changes the directions the microphone picks up best. Some digital microphones let you change these settings from the mixer.

Even with the right settings, a microphone can record noise from its stand and from moving air. A stand, often with a long arm called a boom, holds the microphone in position. But the vibration of footsteps and bumps passes up the stand and is recorded as low sounds. So the microphone often sits in a holder with elastic bands, called a shockmount. Outdoors, a cover made of foam or fur keeps most wind away from the microphone. Indoors, a thin screen called a pop filter stops the bursts of air in "p" and "b" sounds. Other covers protect it from rain and dust, and makers test how heat and damp air change its performance.

The flashcards explain the numbers on the sheet, the switches on the microphone, and the parts that hold and protect it. By the end, you will know what the sheet tells you, and how settings, mounting, and protection help a microphone perform as its sheet describes.
