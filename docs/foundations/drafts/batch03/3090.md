Topic Foundations: Microphone Transducer Principles

Imagine you record a voice message on your phone. Your voice makes tiny, fast changes in air pressure. Before the phone can store your voice, those changes must become an electrical signal. A device that changes one form of energy into another is called a transducer. So this topic asks: how does a microphone turn moving air into electricity?

Most microphones start with a very thin sheet of plastic film or metal. Air pressure pushes it forward and back with the sound. That sheet is called the diaphragm. The diaphragm and the parts that turn its movement into a signal make the capsule. Its size, and the small spaces and openings behind it, affect which frequencies it responds to most easily.

One way to turn that movement into electricity uses a magnet. A wire that moves near a magnet gets a small voltage. A coil of wire on the diaphragm moves inside a magnet's narrow gap. This is called a dynamic microphone. It needs no power. In a ribbon microphone, a very thin strip of metal hangs between magnets. Its signal is very small, so a transformer, a part made of two coils of wire, usually increases its voltage.

A second way uses electric charge instead. The diaphragm sits very close to a fixed metal plate with small holes. When sound moves the diaphragm, the gap changes, and so does the voltage. This is called a condenser microphone. The charge comes from an outside voltage, or from a material that keeps its charge for years.

A condenser capsule's signal is very weak and cannot push current along a long cable without losing much of it and adding noise. So a small amplifier sits right behind it. This is usually a transistor, a tiny part that controls current, or sometimes a vacuum tube. The mixer usually powers it with a steady voltage along the cable that carries the sound. That is called phantom power. Others use a battery, or a much lower voltage from a camera. Older systems send power in other ways, and the wrong one can damage a microphone. A faulty cable can also send phantom power into a ribbon microphone and damage its thin strip.

The charge method can also be made very small, and most microphones in phones use it. They are built like computer chips, with a tiny diaphragm and plate cut into silicon. A second chip in the same package strengthens the signal, and often turns it into digital data.

A few microphones use neither method. They use a crystal or ceramic that makes a voltage by itself when sound bends it.

The flashcards follow this order, from the capsule through each design to the electronics and power behind it. By the end, you will know how each type of microphone turns sound into a signal, and how that method affects its power and behavior.
