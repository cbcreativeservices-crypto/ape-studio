Topic Foundations: Microphone Transducer Principles

Imagine you sing into a microphone at a school concert. Your voice makes tiny, fast changes in air pressure. A moment later, it comes out of the loudspeakers. In between, moving air became an electrical signal. A device that changes one form of energy into another is called a transducer. So this topic asks one question: how does a microphone turn moving air into electricity?

It starts with a very thin, light sheet of plastic film or metal. Air pressure pushes it forward and back with the sound. That sheet is called the diaphragm. The diaphragm and the parts that turn its movement into a signal make the capsule. The diaphragm's size and the air behind it affect which frequencies it responds to most easily.

One way to turn that movement into electricity uses a magnet. When a wire moves near a magnet, a small voltage appears in the wire. A coil of wire can be attached to the diaphragm, inside a magnet's narrow gap. This is called a dynamic microphone. It is hard to damage and needs no power. In another design, a very thin strip of metal, the ribbon, hangs between magnets. Its signal is so small that a part called a transformer must increase its voltage.

A second way uses electric charge instead. The diaphragm sits very close to a fixed metal plate with small holes. Together they store charge. When sound moves the diaphragm, the gap changes, and so does the voltage. This is called a condenser microphone. It needs a steady charge on the diaphragm and the plate. Some receive it from an outside voltage. Others use a special material that keeps its charge for many years.

A condenser capsule's signal is very weak and cannot travel far along a cable. So a small amplifier sits right behind it, usually a tiny transistor, sometimes a vacuum tube. This amplifier needs power. Usually the mixer sends a steady voltage along the same cable that carries the sound. That is called phantom power. Older systems put the voltage on the wires differently, and the wrong system can damage a microphone. A faulty cable can also send this voltage into a ribbon microphone and damage its thin strip.

The same charge idea also works at a very small size. The microphones in phones are made by etching a silicon chip. A second chip in the same package strengthens the signal, and often turns it into digital data. A few designs use other physical methods. Some crystals make a voltage by themselves when sound bends them.

The flashcards follow this same order, from the capsule through each design to the electronics and power behind it. By the end, you will know how each type of microphone turns sound into a signal, and why that method affects how it is powered and behaves.
