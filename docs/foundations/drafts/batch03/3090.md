Topic Foundations: Microphone Transducer Principles

Imagine you record a voice message on your phone. Your voice makes tiny, fast changes in air pressure. In between, moving air became an electrical signal. A device that changes one form of energy into another is called a transducer. So this topic asks one question: how does a microphone turn moving air into electricity?

Most microphones start with a very thin, light sheet of plastic film or metal. Air pressure pushes it forward and back with the sound. That sheet is called the diaphragm. The diaphragm and the parts that turn its movement into a signal make the capsule. The diaphragm's size, and the small spaces and openings behind it, affect which frequencies it responds to most easily.

One way to turn that movement into electricity uses a magnet. When a wire moves near a magnet, a small voltage appears in the wire. A coil of wire on the diaphragm can move inside a magnet's narrow gap. This is called a dynamic microphone. It is hard to damage and needs no power. In another design, a very thin strip of metal, the ribbon, hangs between magnets. Its signal is very small, so a transformer usually increases its voltage.

A second way uses electric charge instead. The diaphragm sits very close to a fixed metal plate with small holes. Together they store charge. When sound moves the diaphragm, the gap changes, and so does the voltage. This is called a condenser microphone. It needs a steady charge on the diaphragm and the plate. Some receive it from an outside voltage. Others use a material that keeps its charge for years.

A condenser capsule's signal is very weak, so a long cable would lose much of it and add noise. So a small amplifier sits right behind it. This is usually a transistor, a tiny electronic part that controls current, or sometimes an older glass vacuum tube. Usually the mixer powers it with a steady voltage along the same cable that carries the sound. That is called phantom power. Older powering systems differ, and the wrong one can damage a microphone. A faulty cable can also send this voltage into a ribbon microphone and damage its thin strip.

A few microphones use neither method. They use a crystal or ceramic that makes a voltage by itself when sound bends it. Most microphones in phones use the charge method at a very small size. They are built like computer chips, with a tiny diaphragm and plate cut into silicon. A second chip in the same package strengthens the signal, and often turns it into digital data.

The flashcards follow this order, from the capsule through each design to the electronics and power behind it. By the end, you will know how each type of microphone turns sound into a signal, and why that method affects how it is powered and behaves.
