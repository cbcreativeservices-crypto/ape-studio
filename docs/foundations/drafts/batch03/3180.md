Topic Foundations: Amplifiers

Imagine a phone playing music through earbuds. Its signal is far too weak to move the heavy cones of concert loudspeakers. A device that makes a weak signal strong enough to move loudspeakers is called a power amplifier. So this topic asks one question: how does an amplifier make a signal larger without changing it or damaging anything?

An amplifier takes power from the wall outlet. Its output parts, tubes or transistors, let that power through in the shape of the input signal. Power that does not reach the speaker becomes heat. The ways of controlling the output parts are called amplifier classes. In some, the parts are always on, which changes the signal very little but wastes much of the power. In others, two parts share each wave, one for each half. Many modern amplifiers switch their parts fully on and off very fast, so they waste little power.

A larger copy must also be accurate. Many amplifiers compare part of the output with the input, and correct their own errors. This is called negative feedback. But the output cannot go above the supply voltage. When the signal is too large, the tops of the wave are cut flat. That is called amplifier clipping. It adds unwanted tones and can damage the small speakers that play high frequencies.

The power an amplifier delivers also depends on the speaker. A speaker that resists the current less takes more power, but it makes the amplifier work harder. Accurate ratings state the steady power under fixed test conditions. Very large advertised numbers are often not accurate. Two channels, the separate outputs of one amplifier, can be joined to drive one speaker with more power. That is called bridge mode.

So much power also needs protection. When sensors find that the amplifier is too hot, it turns the level down or switches off, so its vents must stay clear. Other circuits disconnect the speaker after certain faults, or limit the current when the two wires of a cable touch. The output can also give an electric shock, so speakers are connected with the amplifier switched off.

Amplifiers for instruments have a different purpose. A guitar amplifier often changes the tone on purpose, and adds distortion when its level is set very high. Many have a connection between the tone part and the power part for effects such as echo. It is called the effects loop. Software can copy the sound of an amplifier and its speaker, so a guitar can be recorded quietly.
