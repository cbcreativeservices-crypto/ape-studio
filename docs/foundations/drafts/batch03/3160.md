Topic Foundations: Close-Miking Keys, Winds, Brass and Vocals

Imagine a session with a piano, a trumpet, a saxophone and a singer. A microphone close to each one picks it up much more than the others. Many instruments are recorded this way. But each one sends its sound out from different places. So where does the sound leave each instrument, and where should the microphone go?

A grand piano is the largest source here. Hammers strike strings inside it, and a big lid above them reflects the sound outward. Microphones placed inside, close to the strings, record mostly the strings and pick up less of the other instruments. This is called inside-piano miking. Near the hammers, the sound of the hammer at the start of each note is louder. Under the piano, there are more low frequencies.

A piano is wide, so one microphone often goes over the low strings and one over the high strings. But the sound reaches each microphone at a slightly different time. When the two are mixed, some frequencies cancel. This is called piano phase interaction, and careful placement reduces it. An organ is also played from keys. Its speaker cabinet often has spinning parts, one for high sounds and one for low sounds, so each part gets its own microphone.

Brass instruments send most of their sound out of the wide end, the bell. The highest frequencies come straight out of its center, loud and narrow. A microphone pointed straight in receives too many of these high frequencies. So it usually goes a little to the side or back. This is called bell-blast avoidance. A French horn's bell points backward, and a trombone's slide moves forward, so these need other positions.

Woodwinds work the opposite way. On a clarinet or a saxophone, most notes leave through the open finger holes. Only the lowest notes come mainly from the bell. A microphone aimed at the holes gives a more even level across all the notes. This is called tone-hole-side miking. Close up, it can also pick up clicking keys, and near a flute it can pick up the player's breath.

The voice also sends out moving air. Sounds such as p and b stop the air and then release it suddenly. Each of these sounds is called a plosive. The burst of air can cause a loud, low sound in the microphone, so a thin screen often sits in front of it. Many microphones also add low frequencies when the singer is very close. Singers often move closer for quiet words and back for loud ones. This is called microphone working.

The flashcards describe each placement for pianos, organs, brass, woodwinds and voices. These are common starting points, not fixed rules. By the end, you will know where each instrument's sound leaves it, and where a close microphone usually goes to record it evenly with little unwanted noise.
