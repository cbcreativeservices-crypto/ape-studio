Topic Foundations: Close-Miking Drums and Percussion

Imagine standing in a room while a drummer plays. You hear the kick drum, snare, cymbals and the room's reflected sound as one instrument. One microphone where you stand would record that whole sound, but nobody could then make the snare louder on its own. So where do microphones go on a drum kit, and how do many of them still sound like one kit?

Most setups start with two matching microphones hung over the kit. They record the whole kit from left to right, and they are called an overhead pair. They can be spaced apart or close together. A common starting point is the same distance from each one to the snare, so the snare stays in the center. Some engineers record a whole kit with only these and one or two more microphones.

Overheads pick up each drum from far away, so each drum, and often the hi-hat, gets its own close microphone. Then its level can be changed separately. A second microphone under the snare records its metal wires. It faces the opposite direction, so its signal is usually reversed electrically, or the two can cancel.

The kick drum needs special care, because most of its sound is very low. A microphone inside the drum, near the beater that strikes it, records a sharp click at the start of each hit. It is called the kick in. A second microphone in front of the drum records more of the low frequencies. It is called the kick out.

Each close microphone also picks up nearby drums. Engineers often point the side of a microphone that picks up least toward the loudest nearby cymbal or drum. The same hit reaches each microphone at a slightly different time. When the signals are mixed, some frequencies add and others cancel, and the drums can lose low frequencies. This is called phase interaction. Careful distances or a reversed signal help keep the low frequencies.

Close microphones record little of the room's sound, so engineers often add it back. Two microphones set farther back record the kit with the room's reflections. They are called a room pair. Placed nearer, they record more drums. Placed farther away, they record more reflected sound.

The same ideas apply to other percussion. A cajón is a wooden box drum. Its deep sound leaves through a hole in the back, and the sharp sound of the hand comes from the front board. So it often gets two microphones. A microphone near the tubes under a marimba's bars records notes with more low frequencies and less mallet noise.
