Topic Foundations: Polar Patterns and Microphone Types

Imagine a singer on a small stage, with drums behind her. The microphone in front of her mouth does not pick up only her. It also picks up the drums and the room. How much of each it picks up depends on its design. This topic asks two questions. From which directions does a microphone pick up sound, and which type suits which job?

Every microphone has a front. The direction straight in front, where it picks up best, is called on-axis. Engineers play sound from every angle and draw the results as a round graph, called the polar pattern. Some microphones pick up all directions about equally. Others pick up the front well, the sides less, and almost nothing from behind. This graph has the shape of a heart, so this pattern is called a cardioid. Another pattern picks up the front and back equally, but not the sides.

These shapes come from the thin moving part inside. If its back is closed, only the pressure at the front moves it, so every direction moves it about equally. If both sides are open, it moves with the difference in pressure between them. Sound from the side reaches both faces at once and does not move it. Mixing the two ways gives the cardioid and narrower patterns. Designs that use this difference add more low frequencies when the source is close. This is called the proximity effect.

Each pattern also has directions where it picks up the least. A cardioid picks up least from directly behind, so a loudspeaker placed there is picked up less. Narrower patterns reject more room sound but still pick up a small area behind. Sound from the side is often quieter and has fewer high frequencies.

These patterns lead to types built for different jobs. A film crew records an actor from outside the area the camera shows. A long tube with side slots in front of the moving part makes sound from the sides cancel. That design is called a shotgun microphone. A presenter who walks around wears a tiny microphone on clothing or a headset. A flat microphone on a table avoids the hollow sound of a microphone above it.

One microphone has only one fixed pattern. So some devices combine many small microphones. A processor delays and adds their signals, so sound from one direction becomes louder and other sound partly cancels. Software can point this direction toward a person speaking. This is called an array microphone. Other arrays record the space in every direction for surround sound.

The flashcards follow the same order. They explain how direction is drawn, how each pattern is made and measured, and which types suit which jobs, including arrays. By the end, you will know why a microphone picks up some directions more than others, and why each type is chosen for its job.
