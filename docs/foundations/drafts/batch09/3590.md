Topic Foundations: System Tuning and Alignment

In the topic on system deployment, you learned how speakers are hung and aimed. Now imagine them in a large hall. Near the stage, the music is loud and clear. At the back, it is quieter, and in one corner the bass is too loud. The goal of tuning is a similar balance of low and high frequencies everywhere, and sound about equally loud in every seat. That even loudness is called uniformity. So how does a crew make a system reach this goal?

Before any tuning, the crew makes sure nothing is broken. They send a test signal to each speaker in turn. They check that it plays, that the wiring is correct, and that nothing distorts. This check is called a verification measurement. They also load the maker's settings for each speaker. Then they set limiters, tools that keep loud peaks below a set level, so the equipment is not damaged.

With every speaker working, the crew measures what the audience hears. Software compares the signal sent into the system with the sound at a measurement microphone. But one position can give a misleading result, because sound changes over a short distance. So the crew measures at several seats and combines the results. That method is called spatial averaging.

These measurements also show a timing problem. Sound needs time to travel, so sound from a nearer speaker arrives first. Where two speakers play the same sound, this difference can make parts of it cancel. So the crew delays the nearer speaker until both arrive together at a chosen seat. This is called delay alignment. It is especially important wherever two speakers cover the same seats, such as subwoofers, the large speakers for low notes, and the main speakers. The crew also checks polarity, so both speakers push the air in the same direction at the same moment.

With the timing set, the crew adjusts the balance of low and high frequencies. A measurement can show the same level at every frequency. In a room, a system that measures this way often sounds to listeners as if it has too many high frequencies. So crews usually tune toward a planned balance, often with slightly fewer high frequencies and more low ones. That planned balance is called the target curve. Air also reduces high frequencies over long distances, so speakers for far seats often get more of them.

Measurements still cannot show everything. So the crew plays songs they know well and listens around the hall. They stand at the front, the back, the corners and under a balcony. This is called a walk test.

The flashcards explain each of these checks and adjustments in detail, with the words crews use for them. By the end, you will know how a system is checked, measured, aligned and adjusted, and how the result is confirmed by listening before the show.
