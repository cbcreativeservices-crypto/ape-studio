Topic Foundations: System Tuning and Alignment

In the topic on system deployment, you learned how speakers are hung and aimed. Now imagine them in a large hall. Near the stage, the music is loud and clear. At the back, it is quieter, and in one corner the bass is too loud. The goal of this topic is a similar balance of low and high frequencies everywhere, and sound about equally loud in every seat. That even loudness is called uniformity. So how does a crew make a system reach this goal?

First, the crew makes sure nothing is broken. They send a test signal to each speaker in turn. They check that it plays, that the wiring is correct, and that nothing distorts. This check is called a verification measurement. They also load the maker's settings for each speaker. Then they set limiters, tools that keep loud peaks below a set level, so the equipment is not damaged.

Next, the crew measures what the audience hears. Software compares the signal sent into the system with the sound at a measurement microphone. But one position can give a wrong result, because sound changes over a short distance. So the crew measures at several seats and combines the results. That method is called spatial averaging.

These measurements also show a timing problem. Sound needs time to travel, so sound from a nearer speaker arrives first. Where two speakers play the same sound, this difference can make parts of it cancel. So the crew delays the earlier speaker until both arrive together at a chosen seat. This is called delay alignment. It matters most where subwoofers, the large speakers for low notes, overlap with the main speakers. There the crew also checks polarity, so both push the air in the same direction at the same moment. Temperature also changes the speed of sound, so delays may need resetting.

Next, the crew adjusts the tone of the whole system. In a room, a measurement with the same level at every frequency often gives listeners too many high frequencies. So crews usually tune toward a planned shape, often with slightly fewer high frequencies and more low ones. That shape is called the target curve. Air also reduces high frequencies over long distances, so speakers for far seats often get more of them.

Measurements still cannot show everything. So the crew plays songs they know well and listens around the hall. They stand at the front, the back, the corners and under a balcony. This is called a walk test.

The flashcards explain each part: checking and protecting the system, where to measure, even sound in every seat, timing, tone, air and weather, and listening checks. By the end, you will know how a system is checked, measured, aligned and adjusted, and how the result is confirmed by listening before the show.
