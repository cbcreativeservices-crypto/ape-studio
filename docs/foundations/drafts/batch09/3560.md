Topic Foundations: Analog Live Sound

Imagine a student singing at a school concert in a large hall. People near the front hear her well, but in the back rows her voice is too quiet. Microphones, a mixing console, amplifiers and loudspeakers can make her louder for everyone. That work is called sound reinforcement. How does a live system reach the whole room and still not make a loud ringing tone?

The problem is a loop. The loudspeakers send sound into the room, and the microphone picks up part of it again. The system makes it louder and sends it out once more. At first you may hear a faint ringing after each word. Turn the system up more, and one frequency gets louder each time it goes around until it becomes a loud, continuous tone. That is called feedback.

So every system has a limit. The amount you can turn it up before feedback starts is called gain before feedback. It depends mostly on distances, and also on where each microphone points and how many are switched on. A microphone close to the singer and far from the loudspeakers allows a higher volume.

Each microphone that is switched on, or open, adds its own loop and its own noise. So engineers keep only the needed microphones on. Microphones also pick up sounds they were not meant to pick up, such as drums, the crowd, or the noise of wind hitting the microphone outdoors. Sound from stage speakers and amplifiers that reaches the vocal microphones is called stage spill. Some stage speakers face the performers so they can hear themselves. These speakers, or earpieces worn by performers, are called the monitor system.

With so many microphones, the show needs a plan. Before the show, the sound team writes a chart of every microphone, which person wears it, and which channel it uses. That chart is called the microphone plot. During a show, the operator follows the plan and, in theater, often turns on each performer's wireless microphone only while that performer speaks or sings. A stage manager tells each team over headsets when the music and sound effects begin.

A plan is just as important outside a hall. Weddings, parades, marching bands and sports fields each need their own plan. Outdoors no walls reflect sound back, so sound gets quieter quickly with distance, and wind changes where it goes. Indoors or outdoors, keeping people safe is more important than the sound. Cables never block exits, stage edges are marked, the weather is watched, and everyone agrees in advance who can stop the show.

The flashcards explain each part, from feedback and open microphones to the stage, the show itself, events outside a hall, and safety. By the end, you will know how a live system reaches the whole audience, why it rings when turned up too far, and how a show is planned and run safely.
