Topic Foundations: Analog Live Sound

Imagine a student singing at a school concert in a large hall. In the back rows, her voice is too quiet. Microphones, amplifiers and loudspeakers can make her louder for everyone. That work is called sound reinforcement. How does a live system reach the whole room without a loud ringing tone, and how is a show planned around it?

The ringing comes from a loop. The loudspeakers send sound into the room, and the microphone receives part of it again. The system makes that sound louder and sends it out again. If the system is turned up too far, one frequency becomes louder each time it passes through the loop, until it becomes a loud, continuous tone. That is called feedback.

So every system has a limit. The amount you can turn it up before feedback starts is called gain before feedback. Several things set this limit: the distance between microphones and loudspeakers, where each microphone points, how much the room reflects sound, and how many microphones are on. A microphone close to the singer and far from the loudspeakers usually allows more volume.

Each microphone that is switched on, or open, adds its own loop and its own noise. So engineers usually keep only the needed microphones open. Performers also need to hear themselves, so some speakers on stage face them, or they wear earpieces. These are called the monitor system. Sound from these speakers and from amplifiers that reaches the vocal microphones is called stage spill.

With so many microphones, a play or musical needs a detailed plan. The sound team writes a chart of every microphone, which cast member wears it, and which channel it uses. That chart is called the microphone plot. Small pieces of tape on the stage floor show where each performer and stand goes. Each piece is called a spike mark.

The plan is then practiced. Work begins with the load-in, when the crew carries the equipment into the hall and sets it up. In a tech rehearsal, the crew practices every planned change of light and sound. During the show, a stage manager tells each team over headsets when to make each change. In theater, the operator often opens each actor's microphone only while that actor speaks or sings.

Other events need their own plans. Weddings need clear speech, and parades often carry speakers on trucks. Outdoors no walls reflect sound back, so it gets quieter quickly with distance. At every event, keeping people safe is more important than the sound. Cables never block exits, stage edges are marked, the weather is watched, and everyone agrees in advance who can stop the show.
