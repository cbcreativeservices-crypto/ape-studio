Topic Foundations: RF Wireless Systems

Imagine a presenter walking among a conference audience with a handheld microphone that has no cable. A small transmitter inside it sends her voice on a radio wave. A receiver tuned to the same frequency recovers the sound. That frequency is called the carrier frequency. In-ear monitor systems work the same way in reverse, sending sound to a performer's earphones. So how can one show use many of these systems together without losing sound?

Laws divide radio frequencies (RF) into ranges for different users, and only some ranges are allowed for microphones. Many wireless microphones use the range of television stations, but only the channels that are empty in that area. These empty channels are called white space. Some uses also need a license.

Inside these ranges, each system needs its own channel, but that is not always enough. When several radio signals pass through one circuit, they can mix and create new signals at other frequencies. These are called intermodulation products, and one can appear on a channel in use. So the equipment usually first scans the range for channels already in use. Software then chooses channels that avoid these new signals, plus spare channels. This planning is called frequency coordination.

Even on a clear channel, the signal must reach the receiver. It becomes weaker over distance, and the performer's body absorbs part of it. Walls and floors reflect it. At some points a reflection cancels the direct signal, so the sound stops for a moment. This is called multipath.

Antennas are placed to reduce these losses. Directional antennas often point at the stage, and one shared pair of antennas can feed many receivers. Two antennas placed apart rarely lose the signal at the same moment. A receiver that uses the better of two antennas is called a diversity receiver.

A strong signal still needs correct settings at both ends. An analog transmitter changes the radio wave in step with the sound. A digital transmitter first turns the sound into data and sends that data on the radio wave. If the transmitter's input level is too low, hiss is heard, and if it is too high, the sound distorts. More transmitter power reaches farther but empties the battery faster. When the signal is too weak, the receiver mutes its sound instead of playing loud noise. This is called squelch.

Before the show, someone carries a transmitter around the stage to find weak places in the signal. This check is called an RF walk. Batteries are checked too. Damaged rechargeable lithium-ion batteries can overheat and catch fire, so crews remove them. During the show, screens show each transmitter's signal and battery, and spare transmitters stay ready.

The flashcards explain each of these ideas in detail. By the end, you will know how a wireless system carries sound, and how a crew keeps many systems working through a show.
