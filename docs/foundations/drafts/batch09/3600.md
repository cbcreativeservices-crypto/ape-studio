Topic Foundations: RF Wireless Systems

Imagine a singer walking across a stage with no microphone cable. A small transmitter in the microphone uses a steady radio wave to carry the sound. A receiver is set to the same frequency and recovers the sound. That frequency is called the carrier frequency. In-ear monitor systems use the same idea in the opposite direction, sending a mix to a performer's earphones. So how can a large show use many of these systems at once without losing the sound?

First, laws divide radio frequencies (RF) into ranges for different users, and not all are allowed for microphones. Many wireless microphones use the range used by television stations, but only the channels that are empty in that area. These empty channels are called white space. Some uses also need a license.

Inside these ranges, each system needs its own channel, but that is not always enough. When several radio signals pass through one circuit, they mix and create new signals at other frequencies. These are called intermodulation products, and one can appear on a channel in use. So the equipment first measures the whole range to find channels already in use. Then software chooses channels that avoid the new signals, plus spare channels. This planning is called frequency coordination.

Next, the RF signal must reach the receiver. The signal becomes weaker over distance, and the performer's body absorbs part of it. Walls also reflect it, and at some points a reflection cancels the direct signal, so the sound stops briefly. Directional antennas are often pointed at the stage, and one pair of antennas often feeds many receivers through a shared box. Two antennas placed apart rarely lose the signal at the same moment. A receiver that uses the better of two antennas is called a diversity receiver.

Transmitters send sound as a changing radio wave or as digital data, and both ends have settings. If the transmitter's input level is too low, hiss is heard, and if it is too high, the sound distorts. More transmitter power reaches farther but empties the battery faster. When the signal becomes too weak, the receiver switches its sound off instead of sending loud noise out. This is called squelch.

Finally, the system is checked before and during the show. Before the show, someone carries a transmitter around the stage to find places where the signal is weak, a check called an RF walk. During the show, screens display the signal and battery level of every transmitter, and spare transmitters stay ready. Damaged rechargeable lithium-ion batteries can start to burn, so crews remove them before the show.

The flashcards explain each part, from radio ranges and their rules to channels, antennas, settings, and the checks and safety of a show. By the end, you will know how a wireless system carries sound, and how a crew keeps many systems working through a show.
