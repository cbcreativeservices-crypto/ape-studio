Topic Foundations: System Deployment and Rigging

Imagine an arena full of people, from the front row to the highest seats. Everyone hears the singer clearly, at a similar loudness. A crew placed and pointed the speakers, and lifted heavy equipment above the crowd safely. So how do you place and point speakers for every seat, and keep everyone safe?

The work starts with the main speakers. Most shows use one set on each side of the stage, and some add a group in the center. In large spaces, each set is often a tall column of matching boxes linked together. The column works as one large speaker and sends sound farther than a single box.

The column is heavy, but it usually works best high above the floor. A speaker that hangs from chains and metal frames above the stage is called flown. A falling load gives no warning, so this is the most dangerous part of the job. Crews use only hardware marked with a safe maximum load, and that load is far below the weight that breaks it. Nobody stands under a load while it moves. When speakers are not flown, they sit on the floor, on a wide, stable base, often with extra weight. Outdoors, crews also measure the wind, because it can make floor speakers and towers fall.

The column must then point at the people, not the ceiling. The crew sets its tilt and the small angle between each pair of boxes. That angle is called the splay angle. Top boxes point almost straight ahead to reach the back rows, and lower boxes point more downward at close seats. Low frequencies, from separate large speakers, are harder to point. When these stand on both sides of the stage, their sounds combine and become much louder along the middle. That loud area is called the power alley.

These choices are tested before anything is lifted. Prediction software uses a computer model of the room to show the loudness at each seat. The colored result is called a coverage map. The same color everywhere means the same loudness. Other colors show seats that are too loud or too quiet.

Quiet areas are usually seats the main speakers cannot reach. People near the stage edge sit below where the main speakers point, so small speakers along the stage front cover them. These are called front fills. Seats under a balcony or far back get extra speakers nearer to them. Their sound is delayed slightly so it arrives together with the sound from the main speakers. Such a speaker is called a delay speaker.

The flashcards explain each part, from the main speakers and their lifting equipment to the small extra speakers. By the end, you will know how a sound system is planned, placed, pointed and lifted so that every seat hears the show clearly.
