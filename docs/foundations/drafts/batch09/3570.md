Topic Foundations: Digital Live Sound

Imagine a band playing in a club. An engineer stands at a mixing console. It blends all the microphone signals into the mixes that the loudspeakers play. Today that console is often digital. It has the same kinds of controls as an analog console, but inside it is a computer. So what changes when the console is a computer?

First, the system is often divided into parts. The part the engineer touches has screens, buttons and faders, the sliding controls that set each channel's volume. This part mainly sends commands. A separate box does the mixing and effects. Its processing power is one main limit on the number of channels.

Next, the sound must travel from the stage to that box. Microphones connect to a stage box. Inside it, a small amplifier first makes each weak microphone signal stronger. This preamplifier is controlled from the far-away console, so it is called a remote head amp. The box then changes the signals into digital data. One network cable carries all the channels. That cable is called a digital snake. Two consoles can share one stage box. If one engineer changes a shared gain, the other console adjusts its own level to keep its blend the same. That is called gain compensation.

Every step in this digital path takes a little time. Changing sound into numbers, processing it, and changing it back each add a short delay. The total delay is called latency. If it is too large, performers can notice it on stage. This timing also depends on one shared timing signal, the clock. If the clock is unsteady, each sample is taken slightly early or late. This can add small errors to the sound.

A computer can also change what its controls do. There are usually fewer faders than channels, so the faders work in layers. Choosing another layer makes the same faders control different channels.

The computer can also store settings. A scene is a saved copy of the console's settings, and recalling it changes many settings at once. A scene can also block chosen settings, so they stay the same when it is recalled. That is called a scene filter. Laptop software can copy the console's screens to prepare a show in advance. That is called offline editing.

That software is usually made for one family of consoles. A family usually includes consoles of many sizes and the processing boxes they command. It can also include extra input boxes and cards, an effects computer, and apps for performers' own monitor mixes. Some consoles also have helper tools that can set input levels or reduce feedback. Most families share the ideas in this lesson.

The flashcards explain these families and their parts. By the end, you will know how a digital live system is divided into parts, how sound and commands travel through it, and what most consoles share.
