Topic Foundations: Grounding and Electrical

Imagine you connect a keyboard to a mixer. Before anyone plays, a low, steady tone comes from the speakers. A plug's third prong connects a metal case to the building's ground wire. That same ground system is often where this noise comes from. So what is ground, where is it connected, and how can it keep people safe and the sound quiet?

Ground has two main meanings. For safety, it is a wire from a metal case to the power panel. This safety wire is never removed, not even to stop noise. For sound, it is the zero point that the audio voltage is measured against. That point is called the signal ground. The two are not always the same point.

Normally, current flows out on one wire, called the hot conductor, and back on another, called neutral. The safety wire carries almost none. If a damaged hot wire touches the metal case, current takes a wrong path to ground. That is called a ground fault. The safety wire then carries a large current, and the breaker switches the power off. A missing or wrong wire can look normal, so outlets are checked with testers.

But a much smaller current through a person can kill. So a more sensitive device compares the current going out with the current coming back. If some is missing, it switches off almost instantly. This device has two common names: residual current device, and ground fault circuit interrupter.

At the main panel, the neutral wire usually joins the ground system at one place. Pipes, steel and equipment racks are all connected with heavy wires. That is called bonding. It keeps everything you might touch at the same voltage. The panel also connects to metal buried in the soil. This helps against lightning, but the soil usually cannot carry enough current to make a breaker switch off.

These connections can also create more than one path between devices. The keyboard and mixer connect to ground through their power cords, and again through the audio cable. The two grounds are often at slightly different voltages, so a small current flows around the circle and enters the audio. That circle is called a ground loop. You hear hum at the power frequency, or buzz with many higher frequencies added.

The safety wire always stays connected, so safe solutions break the loop on the audio side instead. One example is a transformer that passes sound with no wire between the grounds. Shielding, a metal layer around the signal wires, blocks electric fields and radio signals, but does little against magnetic hum.

The flashcards cover kinds of ground, safety wiring and faults, protective devices, earth connections, ground loops and safe fixes, hum and buzz, and shields. By the end, you will know what ground is, why hum appears, and how a system is kept both safe and quiet.
