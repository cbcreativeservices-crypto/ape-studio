Topic Foundations: Frequency, Pitch and Spectral Concepts

Imagine a guitarist plucking one string, which moves back and forth very fast. The number of these movements in one second is the frequency. Its unit is called the hertz. This topic asks how that number becomes a note we hear, and why two instruments sound different on the same note.

The ear hears only a middle range of frequencies, which engineers divide into standard bands. Inside this range, a higher frequency is heard as a higher note. When a frequency doubles, the new note sounds like the same note, only higher. That distance is called an octave, and notes an octave apart share a name. Much of today's music divides each octave into small steps, the same steps as the white and black keys of a piano. Smaller units show how far a note is out of tune.

But a note from a real instrument is not one frequency. The string also vibrates in halves, thirds and smaller parts at the same time. The lowest frequency is called the fundamental, and it usually decides which note you hear. The others are whole-number multiples of it, or very close to them, called harmonics. Bells and drums also make frequencies outside this pattern.

A flute and a violin can play the same fundamental, but their harmonics have different levels. The ear joins them into one note and hears the difference as a quality of the sound, called timbre. A graph of every frequency and its level shows this. Its overall outline is called the spectral envelope.

Harmonics also explain how two notes sound together. The distance between them is described as a ratio of their frequencies. When the ratio uses small whole numbers, many harmonics of the two notes match, and the notes usually blend. With complicated ratios, fewer match, and the notes sound rough together. Two notes that are almost the same frequency make the loudness rise and fall in a regular pulse.

This creates a problem for instruments tuned in advance, such as the piano. Notes tuned with simple ratios do not fit into one set of steps that repeats in every octave. Each way of choosing the exact frequencies is a tuning system. Some keep the simple ratios but work well only for scales that start on a few notes. The most common system today makes all the steps the same size, so every starting note works equally. This is called equal temperament. Musicians also agree on one reference frequency, so all instruments tune to the same pitch.
