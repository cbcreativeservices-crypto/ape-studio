Topic Foundations: Frequency, Pitch and Spectral Concepts

Imagine tuning a violin with a phone. You play one string, and the screen shows a note name. The string moves back and forth very fast. The number of these movements in one second is the frequency. Its unit is called the hertz. This topic asks how that number becomes a note, and why two instruments sound different on the same note.

The ear hears only a middle range of frequencies. Sound below this range is infrasound, and sound above it is ultrasound. Inside the range, a higher frequency is heard as a higher note. When a frequency doubles, the new note sounds like the same note, only higher. That distance is called an octave, and notes an octave apart share a name. Much of today's music divides each octave into small, equal steps, the steps between neighboring piano keys. Smaller units show how far a note is out of tune.

But a note from a real instrument is not one frequency. The string also vibrates in halves, thirds and smaller parts at the same time. The lowest frequency is called the fundamental, and it usually decides which note you hear. The others are whole-number multiples of it, or very close to them, called harmonics. Bells and drums also make frequencies outside this pattern.

A flute and a violin can play the same fundamental, but their harmonics have different levels. The ear joins them into one note and hears the difference as a quality of the sound, called timbre. A graph of every frequency and its level shows this. Its overall outline is called the spectral envelope. Engineers also describe a spectrum with single numbers, such as its balance point or how fast it changes.

Harmonics also explain how two notes sound together. Their distance is described as a ratio of frequencies. When the ratio uses small whole numbers, many of their harmonics match, and the notes usually blend. With complicated ratios, fewer harmonics match, and the notes blend less. When two frequencies are almost the same, the loudness rises and falls in a regular pulse.

This is a problem for instruments tuned in advance, such as the piano. If every note is tuned with simple ratios, the steps between neighboring notes are not all the same size. Each way of choosing the exact frequencies is a tuning system. Some systems keep the simple ratios, but then the instrument sounds in tune only in a few keys. The most common system today, called equal temperament, makes all the steps the same size, so every key works equally well. Musicians also agree on one reference frequency, so all instruments tune to the same pitch.
