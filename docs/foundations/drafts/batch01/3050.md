Topic Foundations: Digital Audio

When you record your voice on a phone, the microphone turns changes in air pressure into a voltage that rises and falls continuously with the sound wave. But a computer can only store numbers. So how does a continuous wave become numbers, and how do the numbers become sound again?

First, the voltage is measured many thousands of times every second. The number of measurements per second is called the sample rate. A higher rate captures higher frequencies, but the highest frequency it can hold is just below half the rate. A tone above that frequency appears as a false, lower tone. This fault is called aliasing. To prevent it, a filter removes those high tones before measuring. On playback, another filter joins the measurements into one continuous wave, not a set of steps.

Each measurement is then rounded to the nearest level in a fixed set. This rounding is called quantization, and its small error usually sounds like faint noise. The number of bits, digits that are each a zero or a one, for each measurement is called the bit depth. More bits give smaller steps and less noise. When a recording is later saved with fewer bits, a faint random noise is added before rounding. This is called dither noise. It turns the rounding error on quiet sounds into faint, even noise instead of distortion.

Recording programs often calculate in a number format that changes its scale for very loud and very quiet values. So a level that goes too high inside a mix often does not distort. The final file usually has a fixed maximum level.

The measuring is done by a converter. One converter changes the voltage into numbers, and another changes numbers back into a voltage. A clock tells it exactly when to measure. If the clock ticks at slightly uneven moments, noise is added. Real converters also add circuit noise, so they often do not reach the quality their bits allow.

The numbers often travel to other devices. Most digital cables carry the bits and the timing together. Connected devices usually use the timing of one main clock. If they do not, measurements are dropped or repeated, and you hear clicks.

Finally, the numbers are saved in a file. Uncompressed files keep every measurement, and their size grows with the sample rate, bit depth, channels and length. A codec, a method that codes and decodes the data, can make files smaller. Lossless methods give back every original measurement. Lossy methods remove sounds the ear is unlikely to notice, and each new lossy copy removes more.

The flashcards cover sampling, rounding and dither, number formats, converters and clocks, connections, file formats, and ways to make files smaller. By the end, you will know how sound becomes numbers, how the numbers are stored and moved, and how they become sound again.
