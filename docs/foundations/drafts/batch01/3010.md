Topic Foundations: Propagation, Reflection and Room Behavior

Imagine playing a song on a small speaker in a park and then in a tiled bathroom. In the park, each sound stops when it ends. In the bathroom, each sound continues for a moment after it ends. The speaker did not change, but what the sound met on its way did. So this topic asks: what happens to sound after it leaves its source?

Sound spreads out from a small source in all directions. The area grows with the square of the distance, so the level drops quickly as you move away. This rule is called the inverse square law. It works best away from the source. Very close to it, the level can change unevenly from spot to spot.

The air also turns some sound energy into heat, and high frequencies lose much more than low ones. Sound travels faster in hotter air, and wind adds to its speed or reduces it. When temperature or wind changes with height, the sound's path bends. This is called refraction. A moving source also changes what you hear. When a source comes toward you, its waves arrive closer together, so its pitch sounds higher. As it moves away, its pitch sounds lower.

Objects in the way change sound too. Low sounds have long waves and bend around objects easily. High sounds have short waves, so more of them are blocked. This bending around edges and through gaps is called diffraction. At a large wall, sound divides. Some bounces back, some is absorbed, and some passes through. A hard, flat wall reflects sound in one direction, and a rough surface scatters it. Soft materials full of small holes, such as foam, absorb more, especially high frequencies.

A reflection travels a longer path, so it reaches you after the direct sound. When the two combine, some frequencies become louder and others cancel. This is called comb filtering. The result depends on the delay. Very close to a wall, both arrive almost together, so the low frequencies become louder. From a far wall, the reflection can be heard as an echo.

In a closed room, sound reflects between the walls. Some low notes have a wavelength that fits evenly into the space between two walls. Those notes become louder in some places and cancel in others. These notes are called room modes.

The many later reflections blend into one fading sound. The time it takes this reverberation to fade by a set amount is called reverberation time. Near the source, you hear mostly the direct sound. Farther away, the reverberation becomes louder than the direct sound, so the source seems more distant.
