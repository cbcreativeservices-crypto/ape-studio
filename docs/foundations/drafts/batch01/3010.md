Topic Foundations: Propagation, Reflection and Room Behavior

Imagine playing one song on one small speaker in a park and then in a tiled bathroom. In the park, each sound stops when it ends. In the bathroom, each sound continues for a moment after the speaker stops playing it. The speaker did not change, but what the sound met on its way did. So this topic asks: what happens to sound after it leaves its source?

Sound leaves a small source in all directions, covering a larger and larger area. The area grows with the square of the distance, so the level drops quickly as you move away. This rule is called the inverse square law. Sound also takes time to travel, so a far listener hears it later.

Air also turns some sound energy into heat. High frequencies lose much more than low ones. Air can change the direction of sound too. Sound travels faster in hotter air, and wind adds to its speed or reduces it. When temperature or wind changes with height, the path of the sound curves. This bending is called refraction.

Low sounds have long waves and bend around objects easily. High sounds have short waves, so more of them are blocked. This bending around edges and through gaps is called diffraction. It is one reason you hear a voice around a corner.

At a wall, sound divides. Some bounces back, some is absorbed, and some passes through. A hard, flat wall reflects sound in one direction. A rough surface scatters it. Soft materials full of small holes, such as foam, absorb more, especially high frequencies. A heavy, sealed wall lets little sound into the next room.

A reflection reaches you a little later than the direct sound. When the two combine, some frequencies become louder and others cancel. This is called comb filtering. Very close to a wall or in a corner, both arrive almost together, so the low frequencies become louder. A reflection from a far wall can be heard as a separate echo.

Inside a closed room, reflections continue between the walls. For some low notes, exactly half a wavelength, or a whole number of half wavelengths, fits between two walls. Those notes become louder in some places and cancel in others. These low notes that build up in the room are called room modes. The later reflections blend into one fading sound. The time it takes to fade by a set amount is called reverberation time.

The flashcards cover distance and travel time, air and weather, bending, and what surfaces do. They also cover how reflections combine, room modes, reverberation and its balance with direct sound, and simple calculations that predict how a room will sound. By the end, you will know what happens to sound between its source and your ears, and why one speaker sounds different in every room.
