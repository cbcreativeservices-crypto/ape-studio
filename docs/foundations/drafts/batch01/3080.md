Topic Foundations: Electrical Power, Distribution and Safety

Imagine plugging a phone charger into a wall outlet. Behind the wall, wires bring power from far away. A concert stage needs far more. This topic answers one question: how does power travel from its source to every piece of equipment, safely and in the right amount?

Electricity is the movement of tiny charged particles through a wire. The push that moves them is called voltage, and the flow it causes is called current. Power from a wall outlet is alternating current, which changes direction many times each second. Inside, most audio equipment uses direct current, which flows in one direction only. A power supply inside the equipment makes this change.

The same current can also pass through a person. Even a small amount can stop the heart from pumping properly, or make your hand close tightly on a wire so you cannot let go. A fault can also make current jump through the air as a very hot flash. This is called an electrical arc. So some rules are never broken. Before anyone works on a circuit, it is switched off, locked, labeled, and tested to prove no voltage is present. Only qualified people, with permission and protective clothing, work on power equipment that is switched on.

The metal case of most equipment connects to a protective earth wire. If a fault connects the case to a powered wire, current flows through the earth wire instead of a person. Then the power usually switches off. That earth connection is never removed. A second safety switch can also turn off the power when a small current leaks to earth.

Then the crew plans the supply. A house usually receives one alternating wave, called single phase. Large shows often use three phase, where three wires carry waves that peak at different times. Heavy feeder cables bring this power to a portable box, called a power distro. Each of its outlets has a circuit breaker, which switches off when too much current flows.

Every cable also loses some voltage along its length. This loss is called voltage drop. Some equipment draws extra current that does no useful work, so the supply must deliver more. The ratio of useful power to delivered power is called power factor. So crews add up the power needed before choosing cables and breakers.

Even a good supply has voltage spikes, electrical noise, and brief outages. Surge protectors absorb spikes, power conditioners filter noise, and battery units keep equipment running. An isolation transformer separates the equipment's power from the building wiring, which can reduce noise. Power sequencers switch amplifiers on last, so speakers do not make a sudden loud sound.
