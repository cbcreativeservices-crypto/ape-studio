Topic Foundations: Electrical Power, Distribution and Safety

Imagine a concert in a large, empty field. The amplifiers, lights, and video screens all need electricity. A generator supplies it, and thick cables carry it to the stage. This topic answers one question: how does power travel from its source to every piece of equipment, safely and in a large enough amount?

Electrical pressure pushes charge through a wire. That pressure is called voltage, and the flow it pushes is called current. Every wire resists that flow a little, which makes heat. Power from a wall outlet is alternating current, which changes direction many times each second. Inside, most audio equipment uses direct current, which flows in one direction only.

The same current can also pass through a person. Even a small amount can stop the heart from pumping properly, or make your hand close tightly on a wire so you cannot let go. A fault in large power equipment can cause an explosion of heat and light. So some rules are never broken. Before anyone works on a circuit, it is switched off, locked, labeled, and tested to prove no voltage is present. Only qualified people open power equipment that is switched on.

A house usually receives one alternating wave, called single phase. Large shows often use three phase, where three wires carry waves that reach their peaks at different times. This delivers a lot of power efficiently.

A heavy feeder cable carries power to a portable box with many outlets, each with its own circuit breaker. That box is called a power distro, short for power distribution box. A breaker switches off when too much current flows, so cables do not overheat. Every cable also loses some voltage along its length, so equipment at the far end receives less. This loss is called voltage drop. So crews add up the power the equipment will use before choosing cables, breakers, and the generator.

Some equipment also draws extra current that flows in the cables but does no useful work. So the supply must deliver more than the useful power. The comparison between the two is called power factor.

Even a good supply is not perfect. Short voltage spikes, electrical noise, and brief power cuts happen. Surge protectors absorb spikes, power conditioners filter noise, and battery units keep equipment running during a cut. Power sequencers switch equipment on in order, with amplifiers last, so speakers do not make a loud, sudden sound.
