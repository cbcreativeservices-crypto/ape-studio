Topic Foundations: Electrical Power, Distribution and Safety

Imagine plugging a phone charger into a wall outlet. A concert stage needs far more power. This topic answers one question: how does power travel from its source to every piece of equipment, safely and in the right amount?

Electricity is the movement of tiny charged particles through a wire. The push that moves them is called voltage, and the flow it causes is called current. Every wire also resists the flow a little, which turns some energy into heat. Power from a wall outlet is alternating current, which changes direction many times each second. Most audio equipment uses direct current, which flows in one direction only. A power supply inside makes this change.

Current can also pass through a person. Even a small amount can stop the heart, or make a hand grip a wire so tightly it cannot let go. A fault can also make current jump through the air as a very hot flash. This is called an electrical arc. So some rules are never broken. A circuit is normally switched off, locked, labeled, and tested to prove no voltage is present before anyone works on it. Work on powered equipment is rare, and only qualified people with written permission and protective equipment may do it.

The metal case of most equipment connects to a protective earth wire. If a fault connects the case to a powered wire, most of the current flows through the earth wire instead of a person. Then the power usually switches off. That earth connection is never removed. A different kind of safety switch cuts the power when even a small current leaks to earth.

Next, power must reach the stage. A house usually receives one alternating wave, called single phase. Large shows often use three phase, where three wires carry waves that peak at different times. Feeder cables bring it to a portable box called a power distro. Locking connectors join these cables. A transformer can change the voltage or keep two systems electrically separate. Each outlet has a circuit breaker or fuse, which cuts the power when too much current flows, before the wires overheat.

Every cable loses some voltage along its length. This loss is called voltage drop. Some equipment draws extra current that does no useful work. Power factor compares the useful power with the total power the supply must deliver. So crews add up the power needed before choosing cables and breakers.

Power also has spikes, noise, and brief outages. A surge protector absorbs spikes. A power conditioner filters noise. A battery backup keeps equipment running during a brief outage. Power sequencers switch amplifiers on last, so speakers do not make a sudden loud sound.
