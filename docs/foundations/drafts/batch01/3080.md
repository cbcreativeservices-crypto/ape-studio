Topic Foundations: Electrical Power, Distribution and Safety

Imagine plugging a phone charger into a wall outlet. A concert stage needs far more power. How does power travel from its source to every piece of equipment, safely and in the right amount?

Electricity is the movement of tiny charged particles through a wire. The push that moves them is called voltage, and the flow it causes is called current. Electricity from a wall outlet is alternating current, which changes direction many times each second. Most audio equipment uses direct current, which flows in one direction only. A power supply inside makes this change. Voltage and current together give power, which is measured in watts. Every wire also resists current a little, which turns some energy into heat.

Current can also pass through a person. Even a small amount can stop the heart, or make a hand unable to let go of a wire. A fault can also make current jump through the air as a very hot flash. This is called an electrical arc. So some rules are never broken. A circuit is normally switched off, locked, labeled, and tested to prove no voltage is present before anyone works on it. Work on powered equipment is rare, and only qualified people with written permission and protective equipment may do it.

The metal case of most equipment connects to a protective earth wire. If a fault connects the case to a powered wire, most of the current flows through the earth wire instead of a person. Then the power usually switches off. That earth connection is never removed. A different kind of safety switch cuts the power when even a small current leaks to earth.

Power must then reach the stage. A house usually receives one alternating wave, called single phase. Large shows often use three phase, where three wires carry waves that peak at different times. Feeder cables bring it to a portable box called a power distro.

Each outlet is protected by a circuit breaker or fuse, which cuts the power when too much current flows, before the wires overheat. Every cable also loses some voltage along its length. This loss is called voltage drop. Some equipment takes extra current that does no useful work. Power factor compares the useful power with the total power the supply must deliver. Crews add up the current all the equipment needs, then choose cables and breakers that can carry it.

The supply can also carry sudden short rises in voltage or unwanted electrical noise, and it can briefly stop. A surge protector absorbs these rises. A power conditioner filters noise. A battery backup keeps equipment running during a brief outage.
