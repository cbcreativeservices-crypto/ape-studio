Topic Foundations: Electrical Power, Distribution and Safety

Imagine plugging a phone charger into a wall outlet. Behind the wall, wires bring power from far away. A concert stage needs the same on a much larger scale. This topic answers one question: how does power travel from its source to every piece of equipment, safely and in a large enough amount?

Electricity is the movement of tiny charged particles through a wire. The push that moves them is called voltage, and the flow it causes is called current. Every wire resists that flow a little, which makes heat. Power from a wall outlet is alternating current, which changes direction many times each second. Inside, most audio equipment uses direct current, which flows in one direction only.

The same current can also pass through a person. Even a small amount can stop the heart from pumping properly, or make your hand close tightly on a wire so you cannot let go. So some rules are never broken. Before anyone works on a circuit, it is switched off, locked, labeled, and tested to prove no voltage is present. Only qualified people open power equipment that is switched on.

The metal case of most equipment connects to a protective earth wire. If a fault makes the case live, current flows through that wire instead of a person, and the power usually switches off. That earth connection is never removed. Some equipment has two layers of insulation instead, so it needs no earth wire. In many places, a ground-fault device also switches off the power when a small current leaks away.

With these rules in place, a crew can plan the supply. A house usually receives one alternating wave, called single phase. Large shows often use three phase, where three wires carry waves that peak at different times. Heavy feeder cables, with one large connector for each wire, bring this power to a portable box. That box is called a power distro. Each of its outlets has a circuit breaker, which switches off when too much current flows. Every cable also loses some voltage along its length. This loss is called voltage drop. Some equipment draws extra current that does no useful work, so the supply must deliver more. The ratio of useful power to delivered power is called power factor. So crews add up the power needed before choosing cables, breakers, and the generator.

Even a good supply has voltage spikes, electrical noise, and brief outages. Surge protectors absorb spikes, power conditioners filter noise, and battery units keep equipment running. Power sequencers switch equipment on in order, with amplifiers last, so speakers do not make a loud, sudden sound.
