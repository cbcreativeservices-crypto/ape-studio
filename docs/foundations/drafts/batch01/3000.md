Topic Foundations: Sound and Wave Fundamentals

Imagine a door closing on the other side of a quiet room. A moment later you hear it. This topic answers one question: what is sound, and what travels from the door to you?

When the door hits its frame, it shakes back and forth very quickly. As it moves forward, it pushes the nearby air together into higher pressure. As it moves back, it leaves lower pressure behind. These two areas are called compression and rarefaction. Each part of the air pushes on the next, so the pattern moves outward. The air itself only moves back and forth in place, along the same line the sound travels. This kind of wave is called a longitudinal wave. Sound always needs a material, such as air, water or wood.

One compression and one rarefaction make one cycle. The number of cycles in each second is called the frequency. The length of one cycle is called the wavelength. The time of one cycle is called the period. In the same air, every frequency travels at the same speed. So low notes have long waves, and high notes have short waves.

A held musical note repeats the same cycle, so it has a clear pitch. The simplest repeating wave has only one frequency and a simple, regular shape. It is called a sine wave.

The size of the pressure change is called the amplitude. A bigger amplitude usually sounds louder. In audio, each sound's pressure is compared with a fixed reference, close to the quietest sound a young ear can hear. Levels on this scale are measured in decibels. The energy a source sends out each second is its sound power. As sound moves away, this energy spreads over a larger area. So the sound intensity, the energy passing through each area each second, drops with distance.

When two waves reach the same place, their pressures add together. This is called superposition. Where two compressions arrive together, the sound becomes louder. Where a compression meets a rarefaction, they partly or fully cancel. This adding and canceling is called wave interference. The position of a wave in its cycle, compared with another wave, is called the phase.

The material also matters: sound travels faster in stiffer materials. If two materials are equally stiff, it travels slower in the denser one. Each material also resists the movement of sound by a different amount. When sound reaches a material that resists very differently, such as water after air, most of it reflects. Objects also vibrate most strongly at certain frequencies, set by their stiffness and mass. Such a frequency is called a resonant frequency.
