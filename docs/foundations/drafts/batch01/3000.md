Topic Foundations: Sound & Wave Fundamentals

Imagine someone plucking a guitar string on the other side of a quiet room. A moment later you hear the note. Nothing visible crosses the room, but something reaches your ears. This topic answers one question: what is sound, and what travels from the string to you?

The string moves back and forth. Each time it moves forward, it pushes the nearby air together. That area of higher pressure is called a compression. Each time it moves back, it leaves an area of lower pressure. That area is called a rarefaction. Each part of the air pushes on the next, so the pattern moves outward. The air itself only moves back and forth in place, and only the energy travels. Without a material such as air, water or wood, there is no sound.

One compression and one rarefaction make one cycle. The number of cycles in each second is called the frequency. The length of one cycle in the air is called the wavelength. In the same air, every frequency travels at the same speed. So low notes have long waves, and high notes have short waves. The size of the pressure change is called the amplitude, and a bigger one usually sounds louder.

These pressure changes are very small, but the loudest sounds make changes far bigger than the quietest sounds do. To keep the numbers small, audio compares each sound with a fixed reference, close to the quietest sound a young ear can hear. This comparison counts how many times bigger a sound is, and its unit is called the decibel.

A source sends out a certain amount of energy each second. As the sound moves away, that energy spreads over a larger area. So less energy passes through each part, and the sound is quieter. The source's power stays the same, but the level you hear depends on where you stand.

Sound often arrives from more than one source. When two waves meet, their pressures add together at each point. Where two compressions arrive together, the sound becomes louder. Where a compression meets a rarefaction, they partly or fully cancel. The position of a wave in its cycle, compared with another wave, is called the phase.

Air is only one material. More stiffness makes sound travel faster, and denser material makes it slower. Each material resists the movement of sound by its own amount. When sound reaches a very different material, such as water after air, most of it reflects. Objects also vibrate most easily at certain frequencies, set by their stiffness and mass.
