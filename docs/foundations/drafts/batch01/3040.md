Topic Foundations: Sound, Waveform, Level and Phase

Imagine you open a recording on a computer. On the screen, a line moves up and down. This topic answers three questions about it. What does the line show? How do we measure its size? And what happens when two copies of a signal meet?

Sound is small changes in air pressure, added to the steady pressure of the air. The wave moves across the room, but the air it passes through stays where it was. A microphone turns these changes into a matching voltage. A picture of that voltage over time is called a waveform display. A steady shift of the wave away from zero is called an offset.

How big is the wave? One answer is its highest point, the peak. Another is a kind of average. The peak decides when equipment starts to distort. The average is closer to how loud a sound seems. Sizes in audio range from tiny to huge, so we compare them by how many times bigger one is. On this scale, each equal step multiplies by the same amount instead of adding it. This is called a logarithmic scale, and the decibel is built on it.

So a decibel is always a comparison. Alone, it shows a change. With an agreed starting value, shown by letters after the number, it states a real level. A meter reading is not loudness, which is how loud a sound seems.

Decibel levels cannot be added like normal numbers. Two unrelated sounds, such as two fans, raise the total only a little. Two equal copies of one signal, exactly aligned in time, raise the decibel level twice as much as two equally loud fans do. Two copies that oppose each other can cancel. The result depends on each signal's size and on its position in its cycle.

That position is measured as an angle inside the wave's cycle. Two ideas here are often confused. Turning a signal upside down, so every rise in pressure becomes a fall, reverses its polarity. It affects every frequency equally. Moving a wave to a different point in its cycle is a phase shift, and it can differ at each frequency.

Phase shifts often come from delay. The same delay is a larger part of a short cycle than of a long one, so it shifts high frequencies more. When a sound mixes with a slightly late copy of itself, some frequencies cancel and others add. The result is a regular pattern of low and high levels across the frequencies, called a comb filter. The cancellation is rarely complete, because the copies are usually not exactly equal and opposite.
