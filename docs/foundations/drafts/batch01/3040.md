Topic Foundations: Sound, Waveform, Level and Phase

Imagine you open a recording on a computer. On the screen, a line moves up and down. This topic asks three questions about it. What does the line show? How do we measure its size? And what happens when two copies of a signal meet?

Sound is small changes in air pressure, added to the steady pressure of the air. A microphone turns these changes into a matching voltage. A picture of that voltage over time is called a waveform display. Some waveforms, such as a held note, repeat almost the same shape, so the next cycle can be predicted. Others, such as noise, never repeat and are described by averages. Some sounds stay steady, and others, such as a drum hit, are short and change quickly.

How big is the wave? One answer is its highest point, the peak. Another is its average size over a short time. The peak decides when equipment starts to distort. The average is closer to how loud a sound seems. Audio sizes range from tiny to huge, so we compare them as ratios. On this scale, each equal step multiplies by the same amount instead of adding it. This is called a logarithmic scale, and the decibel is built on it.

So a decibel always compares two values. Alone, it shows a change. With an agreed starting value, shown by letters after the number, it states a real level. Because of this, decibel levels cannot be added like normal numbers. Two unrelated sounds, such as two fans, raise the total level only a little. Two equal copies of one signal, exactly aligned in time, raise it twice as much. If one copy rises exactly when the other falls, the two can cancel. The result depends on each signal's size and on its position in its cycle.

That position is measured as an angle, where one cycle is a full circle. Two ideas here are often confused. Turning a signal upside down, so every rise in pressure becomes a fall, reverses its polarity. This affects all frequencies equally. Moving a wave to a different point in its cycle is a phase shift, and it can differ at each frequency.

Phase shifts often come from delay. The same delay is a larger part of a short cycle than of a long one, so it shifts high frequencies more. When a sound mixes with a slightly late copy of itself, some frequencies cancel and others add. The result is a regular pattern of low and high levels across the frequencies, called a comb filter. The cancellation is rarely complete, because the copies are usually not exactly equal and opposite.
