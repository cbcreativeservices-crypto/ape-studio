Topic Foundations: Signal Path and Levels

Imagine a teacher speaking into a microphone in a school hall. Her voice comes out of speakers at the back. On the way, it travels as an electrical signal through cables and equipment. Each piece of equipment can pass it on unchanged, or add noise and distortion. So this topic asks: how does the signal travel, and what level should it have at each point?

The microphone sends its signal to a small amplifier that raises its weak level. This amplifier is usually built into one channel of a mixing console. Many channels are then added together into one shared path, which goes to the speakers. The whole route is called the signal path.

The signal is not the same size everywhere. A microphone makes a very weak signal, and an electric guitar makes a slightly larger one. Most equipment sends and expects a much larger, standard signal called line level. A signal that goes into an input made for a different size sounds too quiet and noisy, or distorted. Most outputs also have a very low impedance, or opposition to the signal, and most inputs a much higher one, so little signal is lost.

To compare these sizes, engineers use decibels. A decibel number always compares two levels. When one of them is an agreed reference level, which counts as zero, the number states a real level. In analog equipment, which works with voltages, levels often go above the reference. Digital equipment has a fixed maximum level, so digital levels are measured downward from it.

Every piece of equipment has two limits. At the bottom, there is a steady hiss from its own circuits. At the top, the signal cannot go higher, so the device flattens the tops of the wave. That is called clipping. The extra level kept free above the normal level, for sudden loud moments, is called headroom. Setting the level at each stage, well above the hiss and below clipping, is called gain staging. The first stage often matters most, because noise or distortion added there passes to every later stage.

A long cable can also pick up hum from nearby electrical equipment. A connection with two matched wires greatly reduces this. The noise reaches both wires almost equally, and the input keeps only the difference, so the noise cancels. This is called a balanced signal.

You cannot hear a signal inside a cable, so meters show its level. Some move slowly and show the average level. Others react fast and show the short peaks that cause clipping. Loudness meters show how loud a whole program sounds to people.
