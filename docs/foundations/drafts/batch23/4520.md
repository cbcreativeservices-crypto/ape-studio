Topic Foundations: Psychoacoustics, Hearing and Audiology

Imagine a hearing test at a clinic. You wear earphones and press a button each time you hear a faint tone. The result is a chart of your hearing at each pitch. A clinic in another country should get the same chart. So how can a hearing measurement mean the same thing everywhere?

It starts with the faintest sound a person can hear. This level changes with pitch, so the chart needs a zero point for each pitch. The zero is the average faint level that healthy young adults can barely hear. It is called the audiometric reference zero. Each type of earphone sits on the ear differently, so each type has its own zero levels.

Those zero levels only help if the earphone produces the right level. So test earphones are checked on a small closed space with a microphone inside. It affects the earphone's sound in the same way a human ear does. That device is called an ear simulator. Hearing aids and devices that test hearing by vibrating the bone behind the ear have their own test equipment.

The test must also follow the same steps everywhere. The tester raises and lowers the tone in set steps. The room must be quiet enough that its noise does not hide the faint tones. A tone sent to one ear can also travel through the skull to the other ear. So the tester often plays noise into the other ear.

That noise works because one sound can hide another. This hiding has many named forms, such as temporal masking. The inner ear divides sound into overlapping frequency bands. Noise in the same band as a tone hides it strongly. The width of each band is often given as the equivalent rectangular bandwidth. A loud sound can also hide a quiet sound just after it. Two ears often make a hidden sound easier to hear.

These bands also explain why a meter reading does not show how loud a sound seems. Loudness calculations add up the sound in each band and include the effect of masking. To judge sound quality, trained listeners compare versions of a recording. The original, placed among them without a label, is called the hidden reference. If a listener gives it a low score, that listener's scores are not used.

Very loud sound can also damage hearing permanently. Earplugs and earmuffs are tested with the same hearing test, first with open ears and then with protection. Real wearers usually get less protection than the label shows. A quick test on the person wearing them, called fit testing, shows the real amount.
