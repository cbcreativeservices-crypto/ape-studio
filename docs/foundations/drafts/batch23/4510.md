Topic Foundations: Immersive Audio, Machine Listening and Loudness

Imagine a sound team finishing the mix of a nature film. It may play in a cinema with many speakers, on a television, or on headphones. On television, it may follow a louder advertisement. For one program to work everywhere, makers follow shared rules, called standards. So how is a modern program described, played correctly on any device at a steady loudness, and understood by computers?

Older mixes were made for one fixed speaker layout. Each sound went to a particular speaker, a method called channel-based audio. Modern formats can instead keep each sound separate, with data that says where it should be and how loud it is. All these sounds and their data together form the audio scene.

One widely used model sorts this data from large to small. At the top is the whole program, then its parts, such as dialogue or music. At the bottom, small blocks of data give one sound's position and level for a short moment. A broadcast can carry several versions of one show for the viewer to choose, such as another language. Each is called an audio preselection.

At home, the device can receive these sounds and their description, instead of finished speaker signals. It calculates what each speaker plays, then adjusts for where the speakers are. For headphones, each sound is filtered differently for each ear, so it seems to come from a direction. This is called binaural rendering. In virtual reality, the scene can also move with the listener. Some scenes are instead recorded as the sound arriving from all directions at one point, as a set of channels. The recorder and player must agree on the order and level of these channels.

The level should also stay steady between programs. The highest peaks do not show how loud a program seems. So the sound is filtered to match how the ear hears frequencies, and near-silent moments are left out so they do not lower the result. The result is one number for the whole program, called the integrated loudness. Broadcasters and streaming services set targets for it. A second reading, the true peak level, finds the highest point of the wave, which can fall between stored samples.

A loudness meter measures only one fact about the sound. Software can go much further and describe what a program contains. It turns each short moment of sound into a few numbers about its frequencies. From them, it finds where notes begin, estimates the beat and key, and marks sections such as the chorus. Newer methods learn from many examples, and can also separate a mixture into its sources, such as a voice and the instruments.

The flashcards explain each part in detail. By the end, you will know how a modern program is described and played anywhere, how its loudness is measured, and how computers analyze and separate sound.
