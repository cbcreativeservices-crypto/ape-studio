Topic Foundations: Acoustic Units and Reference Values

Imagine reading the information sheet for a loudspeaker. It gives the weight and how loud it can play. An engineer in another country understands every number the same way. That works because everyone uses the same units. So who defines these units, and how do they stay the same everywhere?

The agreement starts with a small set of basic units, for length, mass, time, electric current, temperature and a few more. Every other unit is built from these. This shared system is called the International System of Units. An international office keeps its definitions, and member countries vote on any change.

Audio uses these units at many sizes. A microphone signal is a tiny part of a volt, and a radio signal repeats millions of times each second. So people add a short word part to the front of the unit. "Milli" makes a unit a thousand times smaller, and "kilo" makes it a thousand times larger. In the short symbols, a capital letter and a lowercase letter can mean different sizes.

Not every number uses this system. Older equipment and books still use units such as the inch or the horsepower. Some old units are only former names, such as cycles per second for the hertz. So a careful reader checks the unit and converts it when needed.

Measuring sound has a special problem. The quietest and the loudest sounds we hear differ by a huge amount of pressure. So in acoustics, people usually give a ratio instead: how much larger a value is than an agreed fixed value, which serves as the reference. A unit for such a ratio is called the bel, and the decibel is a smaller part of it. Agreed rules also describe filters that adjust a reading to match human hearing, and short lists of rounded numbers for sizes and frequency bands. A list in which each step is the same ratio larger than the last is called a Renard series.

These agreements are written down and published as standards by national, regional and international organizations. A standard passes through drafts and votes, and later it may be corrected or withdrawn. The parts that state the rules are called normative, and other parts only explain. A standard often refers to another one. A reference that names the exact year is called a dated reference, so only that version counts. A reference with no year is called an undated reference, and the newest version applies.

The flashcards cover the basic units, the word parts for size, and older units. They also cover ratio units, reference values, hearing filters, rounded numbers, the organizations, and how a standard is written. By the end, you will know where the units behind every audio number come from, and which parts of a standard are rules.
