Topic Foundations: Audio Coding and Codecs

Imagine a full music album saved as raw recorded numbers. That is far more data than a busy network can carry easily. So an encoder makes the data smaller, and a decoder rebuilds the sound at the other end. Together they form a codec. But how can sound lose most of its data and still sound almost the same?

The answer starts with hearing. A loud sound can hide quieter sounds near it in frequency. An encoder uses a model of hearing to find these hidden parts and uses little data for them. This method is called perceptual audio coding.

To find the hidden parts, the encoder splits the sound into frequency bands. The part that does this is called a filter bank. The numbers in each band are then rounded, which saves data but adds a little noise. Each band gets a value that sets how coarsely its numbers are rounded. That value is called a scalefactor. The encoder usually rounds more coarsely where louder sound will hide the noise.

The encoder works on short blocks of sound. A sudden drum hit can spread the rounding noise across its whole block, even before the hit. That faint noise is called pre-echo. Shorter blocks for sudden sounds reduce it.

At low data rates, encoders send short descriptions instead of full sound. The left and right channels are often similar. So an encoder can send one channel plus a little data that describes how left and right differ. That method is called parametric stereo. In the same way, the decoder can recreate the high frequencies from the low ones, guided by a little extra data.

Phone calls carry mostly speech, so they usually use a different method. Speech codecs model the voice as a filter that copies the shape of the throat and mouth, with a chosen signal sent into it. The encoder tests many possible signals and keeps the one that sounds closest. This idea is called code-excited linear prediction. Calls need very little delay, and they often replace lost data with a likely sound.

Each standard combines some of these methods for one job, such as streaming or phone calls, and describes how the coded data must be read. The coded data is usually organized in small blocks, each called an audio frame. A setup header tells the decoder the coding type, sample rate and channels. Some standards also offer lossless coding, which makes the data smaller without changing the sound at all. To compare codecs, listeners and computer models judge how close each one sounds to the original.

The flashcards explain each method, the main codec families, and how people and computer models judge coded sound. By the end, you will know how codecs make sound data smaller, why it still sounds close to the original, and how standards keep encoders and decoders compatible.
