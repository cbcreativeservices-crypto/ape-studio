Topic Foundations: Audio Coding and Codecs

Imagine streaming a song on your phone. A full recording is a long list of numbers, more data than a phone network can carry easily. So an encoder makes the data smaller, and a decoder rebuilds the sound at the other end. Together they form a codec. Standards describe exactly how the coded data must be read, so any matching decoder can play it. But how can sound lose most of its data and still sound almost the same?

A loud sound hides quieter sounds near it in frequency. An encoder uses a model of hearing to find these hidden parts and uses very little data for them. This method is called perceptual audio coding.

The encoder cuts the sound into short blocks and splits each block into frequency bands. The part that splits the sound is called a filter bank. Each band gets a value that sets how exactly its numbers are stored. That value is called a scalefactor. Less exact numbers add noise, so they are used only where louder sound hides that noise. A sudden drum hit spreads coding noise across its whole block, even before the hit. That faint noise is called pre-echo, and shorter blocks for sudden sounds reduce it.

The channels of stereo and surround sound give another chance to save data, because they are often similar. At low data rates, an encoder can send one channel plus a small amount of extra data that describes how left and right differ. That method is called parametric stereo. The decoder can also rebuild the high frequencies from the low frequencies and a little extra data.

Phone calls carry mostly speech, so they usually use a different method. Speech codecs model the voice as a filter that copies the shape of the throat and mouth, with a simple signal sent into that filter. The encoder compares many possible signals and chooses the one that sounds closest. This idea is called code-excited linear prediction. Calls also need little delay and must replace lost data with a likely sound.

Each standard combines some of these methods for one job: music for broadcast and streaming, phone calls, or wireless headphones. Some codecs keep every detail, so the decoder rebuilds the exact original. Every codec also organizes its data in small blocks, each called an audio frame. A header tells the decoder which method was used, so it can decode the sound correctly.

The flashcards cover the hearing model, bands and rounding, stereo and surround, speech coding, the main codec families, and how a coded stream is organized. They also cover how listeners and computer models judge quality. By the end, you will know how codecs make sound data smaller, why it still sounds close to the original, and how standards let encoders and decoders work together.
