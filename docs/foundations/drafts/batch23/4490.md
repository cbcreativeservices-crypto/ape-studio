Topic Foundations: Audio Coding and Codecs

Imagine a music album saved as raw recorded numbers. That is often too much data to send quickly. So an encoder makes the data smaller, and a decoder rebuilds the sound. Together they form a codec. But how can sound lose most of its data and still sound almost the same?

The answer starts with hearing. A loud sound can hide quieter sounds near it in frequency. An encoder uses a model of hearing to find these hidden parts and uses little data for them. This method is called perceptual audio coding.

To find them, the encoder splits the sound into frequency bands, using a part called a filter bank. Each band's numbers are then rounded, which saves data but adds a little noise. Each band gets a value that sets how coarsely its numbers are rounded. That value is called a scalefactor. The encoder usually rounds more coarsely where louder sound will hide the noise. The encoder works on short blocks of sound. A sudden drum hit can spread rounding noise across its block, even before the hit. That faint noise is called pre-echo. Shorter blocks reduce it.

At low data rates, an encoder can describe some parts of the sound instead of coding them in full. The left and right channels are often similar. So an encoder can send one channel plus a little data about the differences. That method is called parametric stereo. The decoder can also rebuild the high frequencies from the low ones and a little extra data. Wireless headphones use their own codecs, built for low delay and little computing power.

Phone calls carry mostly speech, so they usually use a different method. Speech codecs treat the voice as two parts: a sound source, and a filter that shapes it the way the throat and mouth do. The encoder picks the source signal that sounds closest from a stored list of codes. This idea is called code-excited linear prediction. Calls need little delay, and often fill lost data with a likely sound.

All these methods must work the same way on every device. So each standard combines some of them and describes exactly how the coded data must be read. The data usually comes in small blocks, each called an audio frame. Each frame usually starts with a fixed pattern that helps the decoder find it.

Some standards offer lossless coding, which makes the data smaller without changing the sound. The perceptual methods above change the sound a little. To judge how much, listeners score coded sound against the original, and computer models of hearing estimate that score.

The flashcards explain each method, the main codec families, how coded data is organized, and how quality is judged. By the end, you will know how codecs make sound data smaller, why it still sounds almost the same, and how standards keep them compatible.
