Topic Foundations: Formats, Metadata, Transport and Sync

Imagine a voice recorded in a studio for a television program. Later you hear it on your phone, in time with the lips of the person speaking. On the way, the sound is only a long list of numbers. Every machine must know how to read them, what they are, and when to play them. So what information must be sent with the sound?

The first need is a file that contains its own description. A common studio sound file has labeled parts. One part states the sample rate, the bits in each sample, and the number of channels. That part is called the fmt chunk; fmt is short for format, and a chunk is a labeled part. The part that holds the sound itself is called the data chunk. Files for film can hold the picture and many sound tracks in the same way.

Information that describes the sound is metadata. Some of it records who owns a recording and how it may be used. Some of it marks special versions of the sound. One, made for people who have difficulty hearing, raises speech above the music and effects. It is called clean audio. Other metadata describes what the sound contains, such as its pitch, so people can search an archive by sound.

Next, the sound must move between devices. A studio digital cable sends samples in a fixed, repeating pattern. A few extra bits are sent with each sample. Collected over many samples, they describe the signal. Connections to computers and wireless headphones work differently: both ends agree on the settings before any sound is sent.

Many studios now send sound over computer networks. Devices list what they can send, and control software connects them. The sound is sent in small units called packets. Special network rules make sure each packet arrives within a known time. On the public internet, packets can be lost or late. So the receiver asks for missing packets again, or rebuilds them from extra data. Streaming services offer a program at several quality levels, and the player chooses one the connection allows.

Television adds one more need: the sound must match the picture. A broadcast carries many programs in one stream. Combining several signals into one stream is called multiplex. The sender also adds clock readings, so the receiver can set its own clock to match the sender's clock. These readings are called the program clock reference. Each piece of sound carries a time value for when to play it. That value is called the presentation timestamp. People notice sound that comes before the picture more easily than sound that comes after it.

The flashcards explain each step, from the inner parts of a file to the clocks that keep sound and picture together. By the end, you will know how audio is packaged, labeled, carried and kept in time.
