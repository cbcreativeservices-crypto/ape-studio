Topic Foundations: Formats, Metadata, Transport and Sync

Imagine a reporter recording an interview at a sports stadium. The recording is sent to a studio across the city, edited, and broadcast with the picture. On the way, the sound is only a long list of numbers. Every machine must know how to read them, what they are, and when to play them. So what information must be sent with the sound?

First, a file must describe itself. A common studio sound file has labeled parts. One part states the sample rate, the bits in each sample, and the number of channels. That part is called the fmt chunk; fmt is short for format, and a chunk is a labeled part. The part that holds the sound itself is called the data chunk. Film formats hold the picture and many sound tracks together.

A file can also describe the recording for the people who use it later. This information is metadata. It can say who owns the recording and how it may be used. It can describe what the sound contains, such as its pitch, so people can search an archive by sound. It can also mark special versions of a program. One version, made for people who have difficulty hearing, raises speech above the music and effects. It is called clean audio.

Next, the sound must move between devices. A studio digital cable sends samples in a fixed, repeating pattern. With each sample, it also sends a few extra bits. Over many samples, these bits form a message that states facts such as the sample rate. Connections to computers and wireless headphones work differently: both ends agree on the settings before any sound is sent.

Many studios send sound over computer networks. Devices list what they can send, and control software connects them. The sound is sent in small units called packets. Special network rules make sure each packet arrives within a known time. On the public internet, packets can be lost or late. So the receiver asks for missing packets again, or rebuilds them from extra data. Streaming services offer a program at several quality levels, and the player chooses one the connection allows.

Television adds one more need: the sound must match the picture. A broadcast carries many programs in one stream. Combining several signals into one stream is called multiplex. The sender also adds clock readings, so the receiver can set its own clock to match. These readings are called the program clock reference. Each piece of sound carries a time value for when to play it. That value is called the presentation timestamp. Viewers notice sound that arrives early more easily than sound that arrives late.

The flashcards explain each step, from the inner parts of a file to the clocks that keep sound and picture together. By the end, you will know how audio is packaged, labeled, carried and kept in time.
