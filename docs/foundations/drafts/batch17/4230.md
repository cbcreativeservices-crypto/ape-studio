Topic Foundations: Dubbing & Localization

Imagine watching a film made in another country. The actors open their mouths, and you hear your own language. The words seem to fit their lips. A team planned, recorded, and checked all of it. This topic answers one question: how does a film get new voices in a new language, so that it feels made for its new audience?

The first decision is how much of the original to replace. Some countries usually use subtitles. Others replace every line with new voices that match the mouths on screen. That is called lip-sync dubbing. A simpler method keeps the original voices quietly underneath, while a reader speaks the translation over them. That is called voice-over translation.

Before anyone records, a specialist writes down every original line and marks each mouth movement, pause, and breath at its exact time in the picture. This step is called detection. The film is then divided into short pieces of dialogue, so actors can record one piece at a time.

Those marks guide the new words. A direct translation is usually too long or too short for the mouths on screen. So a writer changes the lines until they fit the timing and keep the meaning. That rewritten version is called the adapted script. Jokes, local names, accents, and swear words can each be kept, explained, or replaced with something the new audience knows.

Next, each character needs a voice that suits the age, size, and manner of the actor on screen. Often the same voice actor speaks for the same character in every episode and sequel. That is called voice continuity. In the studio, guided by a director, the actor watches a short piece several times and then records it in time with the picture. Actors also record breaths, laughs, and other sounds without words, so these match the new voice.

The new voices still need the rest of the soundtrack. The original team usually delivers a separate track of music and sound effects, with no speech. If the track still has original speech, or is missing sounds recorded together with the old voices, it is repaired. Then the new voices are mixed with it, and each language gets its own finished soundtrack.

Last, every version is checked. Native speakers listen for wrong meanings and unnatural wording. Reviewers look for lines that start or end at the wrong time, voices that suddenly change, and small parts of the original speech that can still be heard. This final check is called localization quality control.

The flashcards cover each stage: ways to replace speech, preparation, adapting words and cultural details, casting and recording, the music and effects track, final mixes, and checks. By the end, you will know how a film is prepared, rewritten, voiced, mixed, and checked so it works in a new language.
