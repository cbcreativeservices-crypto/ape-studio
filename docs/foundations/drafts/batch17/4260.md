Topic Foundations: Immersive Formats and Delivery

Imagine sitting in a home cinema during a storm scene. Speakers are placed in front, at the sides and behind you, so rain seems to fill the room. Some cinemas also place speakers in the ceiling, so a plane can pass over your head. Sound that comes from around and above the listener is called immersive audio. But a mix is made in one studio. So how does it reach a cinema, a living room and a pair of earbuds, and still sound correct?

The older answer used a fixed set of channels. Each channel was sent to one speaker in a known position, such as left, center or right. Later layouts added more speakers, and then a row of speakers in the ceiling. But each layout needed its own mix, and a home with a different speaker arrangement could not play it as intended.

So newer formats separate the sound from the speakers. Steady sounds, such as background sound and much of the music, still go to a fixed group of speaker channels. These are called bed channels. Sounds that need an exact place, or need to move, are kept as separate audio objects. Each object is one sound plus data that says where it should be, how large it should seem and how loud it should play.

That data is stored with the sound in one main file. A program on the listener's system reads it and calculates what each real speaker should play. Rendering guided by this data is called metadata-driven rendering. Another method, called scene-based audio, stores the sound arriving from every direction at one point, and converts it for any speaker layout later.

Headphones have only two channels, but the same program can still use them. Our head and outer ears change a sound slightly, depending on its direction. The program copies these changes with filters, so sounds seem to come from around and above us. With head tracking, a sensor follows the listener's head, so a sound from the front of the room stays there when the head turns.

Before delivery, the person mixing must hear the mix correctly. Mix rooms place their speakers at set angles and heights, at equal levels. Their timing is adjusted so the sound from every speaker arrives at the same time. Then the program makes fixed versions from the main file, for smaller surround systems, stereo and headphones. A mix that still sounds correct with fewer channels has good fold-down compatibility. Every version is checked for level and position before release.

The flashcards explain each part in detail. They cover speaker layouts and older systems, beds and objects, main files and the programs that read them, headphones, sound fields, monitoring rooms and delivery. By the end, you will know how one immersive mix is built, checked and delivered.
