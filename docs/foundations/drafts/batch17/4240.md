Topic Foundations: Dubbing Adaptation, Sync and Deliverables

In the topic on dubbing and localization, you learned how a film gets new voices in a new language. The work starts from the dialogue list, a record of every line with its start and end times. A translator writes the meaning. Then a writer changes it so actors can speak it in time with the picture. This is called dialogue adaptation. So what must a new line match on screen, how much of its meaning can stay, and what is delivered at the end?

The first limit is time. The new line should start when the mouth opens and stop when it closes. This rule of equal length is called isochrony. One opening and closing of the mouth is called a flap, and the writer fits the new sounds to each flap. The writer shortens a translation that is too long, and lengthens one that is too short. When the mouth is hidden, the timing can be less exact.

Within that time, the shape of the mouth also matters, most of all when the face fills the screen. The sounds "p", "b" and "m" close both lips, so the new line usually needs a lip-closing sound there. A wide-open mouth needs an open vowel, such as "ah". The mouth shape you can see for a sound is called a viseme. Several sounds share one viseme, so a writer can choose another word whose mouth shapes look the same.

Viewers also watch the body and listen to the voice. When an actor points and says "that one", the pointing word should come at that moment. Often the actor also stresses one important word by saying it louder or longer. The dubbed line should stress the word with the same meaning at the same moment. A question should still be heard as a question.

All these limits can conflict with meaning and with natural speech. To fit the mouth, a writer may copy the original wording too closely. Dialogue that sounds planned, reuses set phrases and stays this close to the original is called dubbese.

Finally, the recorded and checked dub is delivered as a set of files and documents. A final list of every shot and every line, with its times, keeps all teams working from one version of the film. A credit screen that names the voice actors and the studio is called a dub card. Some television systems play a film slightly faster. This raises the pitch, so the pitch is often corrected. Often only the new language tracks are sent, with instructions to use the picture from the original files.

The flashcards follow the same path, from the timed scripts through each kind of match and each common mistake, to the delivered files. By the end, you will know how a translated line is fitted to the picture, and what is delivered with a finished dub.
