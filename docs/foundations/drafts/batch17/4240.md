Topic Foundations: Dubbing Adaptation, Sync and Deliverables

In the topic on dubbing and localization, you learned how a film or television show is recorded in a new language. The work starts from a written list of every line, with the exact start and end time of each. A translator writes the meaning, and then a writer changes it so actors can speak it in time with the picture. This is called dialogue adaptation. So what must a new line match on screen, and how much of its meaning can stay?

The first limit is time. The new line should start when the mouth opens and stop when it closes. This rule of equal length is called isochrony. When a translation is too long, the writer removes words that are not needed. When it is too short, the writer adds a few words. When the speaker is far from the camera or the mouth is hidden, the timing can be less exact.

Inside that time, the shape of the mouth matters, most of all when the face fills the screen. The sounds "p", "b" and "m" close both lips, so the new line needs a lip-closing sound there. A wide-open mouth needs an open vowel, such as "ah". The mouth shape you can see for a sound is called a viseme. Several sounds share one viseme, so a writer can replace a word with another word whose mouth shapes look the same. One opening and closing of the mouth is called a flap.

Viewers also watch the body. When an actor points and says "that one", the pointing word must come at the same moment. The words must also agree with the picture, so a line cannot mention a door that is not in the shot.

The voice must also match the original performance. The actor says one important word louder and longer, and the voice rises and falls in a pattern. The dubbed line should make the same idea louder and longer at the same moment. A question should still be heard as a question.

All these limits can conflict with meaning and with natural speech. To fit the mouth, a writer may copy the original wording too closely. Dialogue that sounds planned and foreign in this way is called dubbese. Local names, such as foods or famous people, cause another problem, because any replacement must also fit the mouth. The writer can keep, replace or remove them. The team balances an exact lip match against natural speech.

The flashcards follow the same path, from the timed scripts through each kind of match and mistake, to the final check and the files delivered with the dub. By the end, you will know how a translated line is fitted to the picture, and how the finished dubbed files are checked and delivered.
