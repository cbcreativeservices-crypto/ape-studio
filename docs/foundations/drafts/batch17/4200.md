Topic Foundations: Film Scoring Composition and Orchestration

Watch a frightening scene in a film without its music, and the scene often feels less frightening. Viewers may not notice the music, but it can guide their feelings. So how does a composer decide where music goes, what it expresses, and how it fits with the picture and the voices?

Music changes how we understand a picture. The same face can look sad or calm, depending on the music. Sound that the characters can hear, such as a band at a party, belongs to the world of the story. That is called diegetic sound. The score, the music written for the film, is heard only by the audience. Before films had recorded sound, live players in cinemas often chose ready-made pieces sorted by mood, such as fast music for a chase.

The score usually matches the feeling of a scene. But calm music during a terrible moment can make that moment feel worse. So the composer and the director watch the film together and decide where music starts and stops. These decisions are written in a spotting note. Each piece of music written for a scene is called a scoring cue. Each cue gets a number and a start and end time.

Within a cue, some moments must match the picture exactly, such as a slamming door. Each one is called a hit point. A click that marks the beat plays in the players' headphones. Its speed can change so the music reaches each hit point on time.

Music can also stand for something in the story. A short tune can be linked to a character or an idea and return when they matter. That is called a leitmotif. When it returns slower or on other instruments, listeners hear how the story has changed.

The chords under a tune shape its meaning too. A major-key piece can borrow notes from the minor key, so a happy scene can also carry a little sadness. The chords at the end of a phrase can make a moment feel finished or leave it open. A cue can also end on one sudden chord, called a sting ending. Composers also plan how two tunes can play at the same time and both stay clear.

The music must also leave room for voices, because it often plays while people speak. So it often becomes quieter, uses fewer instruments, and avoids the voice's range of pitches. That space is called the dialogue pocket. Players may also mute their instruments or play them in unusual ways. Once the music is planned, another musician often turns the composer's short version into a full score, with every instrument in a fixed order.
