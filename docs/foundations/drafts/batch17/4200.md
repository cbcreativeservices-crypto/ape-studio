Topic Foundations: Film Scoring, Composition and Orchestration

Watch a frightening scene in a film without its music, and the scene often feels less frightening. Viewers may not notice the music, but it can guide their feelings. So how does a composer decide where music goes, what it expresses, and how it fits with the picture and the voices?

Music changes how we understand a picture. The same face can look sad or calm, depending on the music. Sound that the characters can hear, such as a band at a party, belongs to the world of the story. That is called diegetic sound. The score is the music that only the audience hears.

In silent films, cinema players often chose ready-made pieces sorted by mood. Today the score is usually written for each film and matches the feeling of a scene. But calm music during a terrible moment can make that moment feel more disturbing.

The composer and the director usually watch the film together and decide where music starts and stops. These decisions are written in a spotting note. Each piece of music is called a scoring cue. Each cue gets a number and a start and end time. Within a cue, some moments must match the picture exactly, such as a slamming door. Each one is called a hit point. A click that marks the beat plays in the players' headphones. Its speed can change so the music reaches each hit point on time.

A short tune can be linked to a character or an idea and return when they matter. That is called a leitmotif. Chords, several notes played together, shape the meaning too. A cue in a major key, a set of notes that often sounds happy, can borrow a few notes from the minor key to add a little sadness. Its last chords can sound finished or open. A cue can also end on one short, sudden hit instead of fading out. That is called a sting ending.

The music must leave room for voices, because it often plays while people speak. So it often becomes quieter, uses fewer instruments, and avoids the voice's range of pitches. That space is called the dialogue pocket.

Once the music is planned and timed, the composer chooses which instruments play each line. The same tune sounds different on strings, brass or a single flute. Players may use a mute to make the sound softer, or play in unusual ways for new sounds. The composer often writes a short version first. Another musician, called the orchestrator, then turns it into a full score, with the instruments in a fixed order. A copyist then prepares a separate part for each player.
