Topic Foundations: Film Scoring, Composition and Orchestration

Watch a frightening scene in a film without its music, and the scene often feels less frightening. Viewers may not notice the music, but it can guide their feelings. So how does a composer decide where music goes, what it expresses, and how it fits with the picture and the voices?

One face can look sad or calm, depending on the music. Sound that the characters can hear, such as a band at a party, belongs to the world of the story. That is called diegetic sound. The score is the music that only the audience hears.

In silent films, that music often came from ready-made pieces sorted by mood. Today the score is usually written for each film and often matches the feeling of a scene. Sometimes calm music during a terrible moment makes that moment feel more disturbing.

The composer and the director usually watch the film together and decide where music starts and stops. These decisions, from the opening titles to the end titles, are written in a spotting note. Each piece of music is called a scoring cue. Each cue gets a number and a start and end time. Within a cue, some moments must match the picture exactly, such as a slamming door. Each one is called a hit point. A click that marks the beat plays in the players' headphones. Its speed can change so the music reaches each hit point on time.

Inside each cue, a short tune can be linked to a character or an idea and return when they matter. That is called a leitmotif. Chords, several notes played together, shape the meaning too. A major key, a set of notes, often sounds happy. A minor key often sounds sad. A major-key cue can borrow a few notes from the minor key on its starting note. Its last chords can sound finished or unfinished. A cue can also end on one short, loud chord. That is called a sting ending.

The music usually leaves room for voices while people speak. So it often becomes quieter, uses fewer instruments, and avoids the voice's range of pitches. That space is called the dialogue pocket.

Once the music is timed, the composer chooses which instruments play each line. The same tune sounds different on strings, brass or flute. Players may use a mute to make the sound softer, or play in unusual ways for new sounds. The composer often writes a short version first. Another musician, called the orchestrator, then turns it into a full score, showing every instrument in a fixed order. A copyist then prepares a separate part for each player.

The flashcards explain each idea, from the music's meaning and timing to harmony, instruments and the players' parts. By the end, you will know how a film score is planned, timed and written to support the story.
