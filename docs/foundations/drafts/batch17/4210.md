Topic Foundations: Film Scoring Technology and MIDI Mockups

Imagine a composer at a desk with a keyboard, a computer and a film on the screen. On the screen a door opens, and the music must become louder at that exact moment. Later, other people must make sure it still fits after the film changes. So how does technology keep music exactly in time with a picture, from the first sketch to the finished film?

It starts before any musician is hired. The composer plays each part on a keyboard into a computer. The computer stores the notes as Musical Instrument Digital Interface (MIDI) data, and plays them with recordings of real instruments. The result, a full sketch of the music made only on the computer, is called a MIDI mockup. The director hears it with the film and asks for changes.

To fit the film, the music must change at exact moments. A film is a series of still pictures, and each picture has its own time label. So the software can keep a plan of the music's speed that matches those labels. Chosen beats can be fixed to chosen moments, and the software calculates the speed between them. This plan is kept in the conductor track. It holds no sound, but the numbered groups of beats, called bars, and the steady beat come from it.

That steady beat matters again when real musicians record. Each player hears it in headphones as a click, so everyone follows the planned speed. A few clicks before the music begins, called a count-in click, tell the players when to start. Some conductors lead without a click. For them, a line moves across the screen and reaches the edge at an important moment. That line is called a streamer.

The film often keeps changing while the music is written and recorded. The picture editor removes, adds or moves shots, and the music no longer fits. Reshaping the music to fit the new version of the picture is called a music conform. It can happen before or after recording.

This fitting is done by careful editing. An editor usually cuts or repeats whole bars, or cuts where a musical phrase ends, so the music still sounds complete. Moving a whole piece earlier or later without changing it is called a music slip. Finally, every piece is placed in one project, and a list records each piece's name, timing and length.

The flashcards explain each part in detail. They cover the composer's computer setup, speed plans and how many pictures the film shows each second, and clicks and screen signals for the conductor. They also cover fitting music to a changed picture, editing methods, and the final music files. By the end, you will know how film music is planned, recorded and edited so it stays in time with a changing picture.
