Topic Foundations: Music Business Operations and Marketing

Imagine you start recording songs for local bands in a small room. As more bands pay, you become a business owner. You must decide what to charge, when to get paid, how much tax to save, and how to find new clients. This topic answers one question: how does an independent music business earn enough and keep finding work?

The first decision is what kind of business it is in law. In the simplest kind, the law treats the owner and the business as the same person, so every business debt belongs to the owner. That kind is called a sole proprietorship. Another kind, called a limited liability company (LLC), usually keeps the owner's home and savings separate from business debts.

Next, the owner needs prices. Every price must first cover the costs of being open, such as rent and insurance, which are paid even when there is no work. These costs are called overhead. A price must also pay for the owner's time and skill. For each job, the owner writes a fixed price for clearly described work, called a quote.

A quote depends on a plan for the job. The owner plans the dates, hires the musicians and lists each stage with its cost. After each stage, the owner sends a bill, called an invoice. Often part of the price is paid before the work starts, to reserve the date. That payment is called a deposit. Written terms can also add fees for late payment or late cancellation.

Then the owner checks whether the business really makes money. Profit is only what is left after costs. A business can earn a profit over a year but still have no money for rent when a client pays late. The timing of money coming in and going out is called cash flow. Freelancers usually pay their own taxes, so many save part of every payment.

All of this needs clients. So the owner decides who to serve and what makes the work different. A short statement of what a client gains, and why it is worth the price, is called a value proposition. A website, a set of photos and facts for reporters, and emails help possible clients find the owner. But much audio work comes from recommendations, so careful service keeps clients returning.

The same questions apply to artists who release their own music. A distribution company usually delivers their songs to streaming services. A release campaign tells fans about the music before the release day. A manager often guides the artist's career and is paid a share of the earnings.

The flashcards explain each part, from business types and prices to project plans, taxes, marketing and releases. By the end, you will know how an independent music business is set up, how it charges and collects money, and how it finds and keeps clients.
