Topic Foundations: Music Business Operations and Marketing

Imagine you play keyboard at weddings and parties. Soon you also hire other musicians. Now you run a small business. You must decide what to charge, how to get paid and how to find clients. This topic answers one question: how does an independent music business earn enough and keep finding work?

The first decision is the legal form of the business. In the simplest form, the owner and the business are the same in law. If the business owes money, the owner must pay it. That form is called a sole proprietorship. Another form, a limited liability company, usually protects the owner's home and savings from business debts.

Next, the owner needs prices. Every price must first cover the costs of running the business, such as rent and insurance, which are paid even when there is no work. These costs are called overhead. A price must also pay for the owner's time and skill. For each job, the owner writes a fixed price for clearly described work, called a quote.

Once the client accepts the quote, the owner plans the dates, hires the musicians and lists each stage with its cost. After a stage or at the end, the owner sends a bill, called an invoice. Often part of the price is paid before the work starts, to reserve the date. That payment is called a deposit.

Then the owner checks whether the business makes money. Profit is only what is left after costs. A business can make a profit over a year but still lack money for rent when a client pays late. The timing of money coming in and going out is called cash flow.

Part of the profit goes to taxes. Freelancers usually pay their own taxes, often in several payments during the year, so many save part of every payment. In some countries, clients also report what they paid on a yearly form. Business costs reduce the income that is taxed. The cost of expensive equipment is often spread over several years.

All of this needs clients. So the owner decides who to serve and what makes their work different from other musicians' work. A short statement of what a client gains, and why it is worth the price, is called a value proposition. A website, emails and social media help the right clients find the owner. Many owners also release their own music. They usually send it to streaming services through a distribution company and tell fans before the release day. Still, much audio work comes from recommendations, so careful service keeps clients returning. Happy clients may also write reviews that new clients read.

The flashcards explain each part, from business types and prices to project plans, taxes, marketing and releases. By the end, you will know how an independent music business is set up, charges and collects money, and finds and keeps clients.
