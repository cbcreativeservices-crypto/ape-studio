Topic Foundations: Industry Foundations

Imagine a song playing in a coffee shop. Someone recorded it in a studio. One company built the microphone, and another sold it. Someone installed the speakers in the ceiling. Each works in a different part of one large industry. So how is the audio industry organized, and what does it expect from the people in it?

The first answer is where the work happens. Studios record and finish music. Record labels pay for many recordings and find new artists. Online services offer the music to listeners and pay its owners. Broadcasters send sound to radio and television. Other companies install permanent systems in schools, offices and places of worship. Work that makes sound for an audience in real time, with no second attempts, is called live sound.

The equipment reaches these places in several steps. A manufacturer designs and builds it. A distributor often sells it to shops. A company that combines products from many makers into one working system in a building is called an integrator. The maker publishes a suggested price, but the real selling price is often lower.

One system can contain products from many companies, so they must work together. That ability is called interoperability. It is possible because makers follow the same written rules, called industry standards. Some are approved by recognized groups through a formal vote. Others become normal because most of the industry uses them. Governments also decide which radio frequencies wireless microphones may use.

The makers and workers also organize themselves. Trade groups represent manufacturers, studios and broadcasters, and many hold large equipment shows. Unions agree with employers on minimum pay for musicians and voice actors. Some professional societies publish research and hold conventions. Other groups give yearly awards for work such as film sound editing.

All of these people work for clients, and most clients are not engineers. So a client often describes a sound by naming a style of music. A type or style of music, such as jazz or rock, is called a genre. Songs in one genre usually share instruments and rhythms, so the name shows what the client expects. Many of these words come from music history, such as names of old dances and of groups of players.

Working with clients also brings duties. A written agreement lists what you will deliver, by when, and for how much. A situation in which your own gain could affect a fair decision is called a conflict of interest, and you tell the people affected early. Before delivery, you check the work completely. That final check is called quality control.

The flashcards cover each part in detail, from kinds of audio work and equipment sales to standards, groups, music styles and professional duties. By the end, you will know how the audio industry is organized, which groups influence it, and what clients expect from a professional.
