Topic Foundations: Industry Foundations

Imagine a song playing in a coffee shop. Someone recorded it in a studio. One company built the microphone, and another sold it. Someone installed the speakers in the ceiling. Each works in a different part of one large industry. So how is the audio industry organized, and what does it expect from the people in it?

The first answer is where the work happens. Studios record and finish music. Broadcasters send sound to radio, television and streaming listeners. Other companies install permanent systems in schools, offices and places of worship. Work that makes sound for an audience in real time, with no second attempts, is called live sound.

The equipment reaches these places in several steps. A manufacturer designs and builds it. A distributor often buys it and sells it to shops. A company that combines products from many makers into one working system in a building is called an integrator. The maker publishes a suggested price, but the real selling price is usually lower.

One system can contain products from many companies, so they must work together. That ability is called interoperability. It is possible because makers follow the same written rules, called industry standards. Some standards are approved by recognized groups through a formal vote. Others become normal because most of the industry uses them. Governments also decide, for example, which radio frequencies wireless microphones may use.

The makers and workers also organize themselves. Trade groups represent manufacturers, studios and broadcasters. Unions agree on the lowest pay with employers of musicians and voice actors. Professional societies publish research and hold large equipment shows. Some groups give yearly awards for technical work, such as film sound editing.

Groups, makers and workers all serve clients, and clients usually talk about music, not technology. A client rarely describes a sound in technical words. More often, the client names a style. A type or style of music, such as jazz or rock, is called a genre. Many of these words come from music history, such as names of old dance styles and groups of players.

Working with clients also brings duties. A written agreement lists what you will deliver, by when, and for how much. A situation in which your own gain could affect a fair decision is called a conflict of interest, and you tell the people affected early. Before you deliver, you check the work completely. That final check is called quality control.

The flashcards cover each part in detail. They explain the kinds of audio work, how equipment is sold, the rules, the groups and awards, the language of music, and the habits clients expect. By the end, you will know how the audio industry is organized, which groups influence it, and what clients expect from a professional.
