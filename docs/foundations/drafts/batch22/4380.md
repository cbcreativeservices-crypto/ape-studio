Topic Foundations: Audio Career Exploration

Imagine the list of names at the end of a film. Many of those people worked only on sound. Concerts, radio shows, video games and meeting rooms need sound workers too, but most people never see these jobs. So this topic asks: what work exists in audio, and how does a person start working in it?

The best-known place is the recording studio. The person who places the microphones and records each performance is called a recording engineer. Others mix the recorded parts, prepare finished songs for release, or guide the artist through creative decisions. Many studio careers start with a helper job, setting up cables and learning by watching.

Recorded sound is also made for films, television and games. After filming, a team removes noise from the recorded speech and adds effects and music. A person who performs everyday sounds, such as footsteps, while watching the picture is called a foley artist. Radio and television need engineers who keep the broadcast sound clear and without breaks. Games need people who make sounds play in response to what the player does.

Other sound happens only once, in front of an audience. At a concert, one engineer mixes for the crowd from a mixing console among the listeners. That person is called the front of house engineer. Another mixes separate sound for the performers on stage. Theaters, festivals, clubs with disc jockeys and business events have similar teams. Other workers design, install and repair permanent systems in arenas, schools and offices.

Across all these jobs, work is arranged in two main ways. Some people have a staff job with one employer and a regular income. Many others work for themselves, hired one job at a time. A person who works this way is called a freelancer. Freelancers often charge a set price per day and find their own clients. In some fields, a union agreement sets the lowest pay and the working rules.

Because so much work is arranged job by job, employers and clients want proof before they choose someone. A short written record of your jobs and results comes first, with examples of your finished work. A short recording that plays your best examples one after another is called a demo reel. In an interview, employers often ask you to describe a real problem you solved. People learn the work by studying, or by working beside an experienced professional, usually for pay, and getting more tasks over time. That is called an apprenticeship. Certificates from outside groups show knowledge of particular systems.

The flashcards describe each role, the ways work is arranged and paid, the materials that represent you, and the training and certificates along the way. By the end, you will know what people in each part of audio do, how they are hired, and how a career can start and grow.
