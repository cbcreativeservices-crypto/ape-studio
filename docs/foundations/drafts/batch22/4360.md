Topic Foundations: Project Management

Imagine a band asks you to help record some songs and then play a concert in another city. The money is limited and the dates cannot change. Work like this, with a clear start, a clear end and a result, is a project. So how do people plan it so it finishes on time, costs no more than planned, and gives the agreed result?

The first step is to agree what the job is. A short document states the goal, the money and the final date. It also lists what the work includes and excludes. That list is called the project scope. The people who care about the result, such as the artist and the person who pays, approve it.

The next question is when each part happens. The job is divided into smaller tasks, each with a cost and a length of time. Some tasks must wait for others; for example, mixing usually waits until recording ends. The longest chain of tasks that must happen in order sets the earliest finish date. It is called the critical path. If any task on it is late, the whole project is late. During the work, the team compares real progress and spending with the plan.

A concert adds a second place to the plan, the venue. Before the day, the touring crew and the venue staff exchange details about times, power, equipment and how the crew brings it into the building. This planning is called the advance. It produces shared documents, such as a map of the stage, a list of every sound source and its mixer channel, and a plan for each day with start times.

Those documents also show what equipment the show needs. Crews collect it from written lists, test it and pack it. Missing items are rented, with or without people to operate them. Tours also plan travel days, hotels and crew rest.

Every plan can have problems, so the team lists what could go wrong and judges how likely and how serious each risk is. For the most important risks, they decide in advance what to do. That is called a contingency plan. A plan can also fail slowly, when people ask for more. Small extra requests, added without more time or money, make the project larger than agreed. That is called scope creep. So every change is requested in writing and recorded.

Near the end, the team writes a list of every small fix still left. When every item is finished, the client, or the person allowed to approve, accepts the work in writing. That approval is called the sign-off. The flashcards explain each stage in detail, and the planning methods teams use, from step-by-step plans to short repeated rounds. By the end, you will know how a project goes from a first idea to a finished job.
