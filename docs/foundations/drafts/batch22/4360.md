Topic Foundations: Project Management

Imagine a company asks you to provide sound for its yearly meeting in a hotel in another city. The money is limited and the date cannot change. Work like this, with a clear start, a clear end and an agreed result, is a project. So how do people plan a project so it finishes on time, within its budget, and with the agreed result?

The first step is to agree what the job is. A short document states the goal, the money and the final date. It also lists what the work includes and excludes. That list is called the project scope. The client and the person who pays approve it.

Next, the team estimates what the work will cost and how long it will take. The job is divided into smaller tasks. The team can compare the job with a similar past job. Or it can price every small task and add them up, which is slower but more exact. Some teams give a best case, a likely case and a worst case.

With the tasks known, the team puts them in order. Some tasks must wait for others; for example, the sound check usually waits until the speakers are in place. The longest chain of tasks that must happen in order sets the earliest finish date. It is called the critical path. If a task on it is late, the whole project is usually late.

To carry out this ordered plan, teams choose a method. Some finish each stage before the next one starts. Others work in short repeated rounds and check the result after each one. Either way, the team checks progress and spending against the plan.

This job is also in a venue in another city. So the plan also sets travel, hotel rooms and rest for the crew. Before the day, the crew and the venue staff exchange details about times, power and equipment. This planning is called the advance. Its shared documents, such as a stage map, also show which equipment to pack and which items to rent.

Even a good plan can meet problems. So the team lists what could go wrong and judges how likely and how serious each risk is. For the biggest risks, they decide in advance what to do. That is called a contingency plan. A project can also grow slowly. Small extra requests, added without more time or money, make it larger than agreed. That is called scope creep. So changes are usually requested and approved in writing.

Finally, the client accepts the finished work in writing. That approval is called the sign-off. The flashcards explain each stage in detail, from the first agreement and the people who approve it to methods for improving quality and the final approval. By the end, you will know how a project goes from a first idea to a finished job.
