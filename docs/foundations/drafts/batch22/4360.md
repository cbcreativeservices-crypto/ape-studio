Topic Foundations: Project Management

Imagine a company asks you to provide sound for its yearly meeting in a hotel in another city. The money is limited and the date cannot change. Work like this, with a clear start, a clear end and an agreed result, is a project. So how is a project planned to finish on time, within budget, and with the agreed result?

The first step is to agree what the job is. A short document states the goal, the money and the final date. It also lists what the work includes and excludes. That list is called the project scope. The main people with an interest in the result, such as the client and the person who pays, approve it.

Next, the team estimates what the work will cost and how long it will take. The job is divided into smaller tasks. The team can compare it with a similar past job, or price every small task and add them up, which is slower but usually more exact.

Then the team puts the tasks in order. Some tasks must wait for others. The sound check usually waits until the speakers are in place. The longest chain of tasks that must happen in order sets the earliest finish date. It is called the critical path. If a task on it is late, the whole project is usually late.

The tasks also need a way of working. Some teams finish each stage before the next one starts. Others work in short rounds and check the result after each. Either way, the team checks progress and spending against the plan, often on charts.

Because the venue is in another city, the plan includes travel, hotel rooms and crew rest. Before show day, the crew and venue staff share details about times, power and equipment. They agree which equipment the venue provides and which must be rented. This planning is called the advance. It produces the show-day documents. A stage plot shows where each performer, instrument and monitor goes. An input list shows everything plugged into the mixer. A call sheet gives arrival times. The run of show lists the event in order.

Problems can still happen, so the team lists what could go wrong and judges how likely and serious each risk is. For the biggest risks, they plan a response. That is called a contingency plan. Small extra requests, added without more time or money, can also make a project larger than agreed. That is called scope creep. So changes are usually requested and approved in writing. At the end, the client accepts the finished work in writing. That approval is called the sign-off.

The flashcards explain each stage, from the first agreement to schedules, recording session plans, show-day documents, risks and the final approval. By the end, you will know how a project goes from a first idea to a finished job.
