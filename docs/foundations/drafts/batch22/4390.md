Topic Foundations: Music Rights, Publishing and Licensing

Imagine a friend writes a song and records it at home. A video maker wants it for a film. A shop wants to play it. Each use involves her rights and can earn money. So this topic asks: who owns music, who may use it, and who gets paid?

Ownership comes first. In most countries, the law gives the creator ownership as soon as a work is written down or recorded. This ownership is called copyright. It controls copying the work, selling copies, playing it in public and making new versions. One recorded song usually has two copyrights. The song itself, its tune, chords and words, is called the composition. The recording is a second work, and it often has a different owner. Ownership can be sold, usually only in a signed written agreement.

Anyone else needs the owner's permission. Permission to use a work while the owner keeps it is called a license. Copying a song onto records or streaming services needs one kind. Playing it in public needs another. Putting it in a film needs permission for the song and for the recording. Sometimes the law gives automatic permission at a set price.

Each license brings ongoing payments. For playing songs in public, they are called performance royalties. This money is often divided into two halves. One half goes to the writer. The other goes to the publisher, the company that manages the song's business. The recording earns its own money for its owner and performers.

One writer cannot collect from every radio station and shop alone. So owners join organizations that license many works, collect the money and pay it to the owners. An organization that handles the public playing of songs is called a performing rights organization. Others handle copies of songs or the digital playing of recordings. The money reaches the right person only if the work is registered with correct facts. These include the title, the writers and each writer's share. This set of facts is called metadata. When it is wrong or missing, the money often stays unpaid.

Contracts decide how the money is shared. Co-writers usually write down each person's share. A record label may pay an advance and the recording costs first, then keep the artist's royalties until those costs are covered. That process is called recoupment. The artist usually repays nothing, but earns no more until then. Contracts also set how long a deal lasts and whether rights return to the creator. Using music with no permission is called copyright infringement.
