Topic Foundations: Music Rights, Publishing and Licensing

Imagine you write a short song and record it at home. A friend wants to use it in a video, and a dance school wants to play it in class. Do they need to ask you first? And if they earn money from it, should part of it go to you? So this topic asks: who owns music, who may use it, and who gets paid?

Ownership comes first. In most countries, the creator usually owns a work as soon as it is written down or recorded. This ownership is called copyright. It controls copying the work, selling copies, playing it in public and making new versions. When a work is made as part of a job, the employer may own it instead. One recorded song usually has two copyrights. The song is one work: its tune, chords and words. The recording is a second work, often with a different owner.

Anyone else needs the owner's permission. Permission to use a work while the owner keeps it is called a license. Copying a song onto records or into downloads needs one kind. Playing it in public needs another. Streaming a song that the listener chooses often needs both. A film needs permission for both the song and the recording. Using protected music without permission or a legal exception breaks the law.

Many licenses bring ongoing payments. For playing songs in public, they are called performance royalties. This money is often split in half between the writer and the publisher, the company that manages the song's business. In many countries, the recording also earns separate money for its owner and performers.

One writer cannot collect from every radio station and shop. So owners join organizations that license many works and pay the money to them. An organization that handles the public playing of songs is called a performing rights organization. Others handle copies of songs or digital plays of recordings. The money reaches the right person only if the work is registered with correct facts, such as the title, the writers and each writer's share. These facts are called metadata. When it is wrong or missing, the money often stays unpaid.

Contracts decide how the money is shared. Co-writers usually write down each person's share. A record label may first pay the artist money in advance and pay the recording costs. It then keeps the artist's royalties until those costs are covered. That process is called recoupment. If sales stay low, the artist usually does not repay the rest. A contract also sets how long the deal lasts and whether rights later return to the creator.
