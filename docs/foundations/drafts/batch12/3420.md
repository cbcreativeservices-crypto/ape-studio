Topic Foundations: Acoustic Principles and Room Behavior

Imagine you play one guitar note in an empty concrete hall. The sound continues for a moment. In a bedroom with curtains and carpet, it stops almost at once. So this topic asks: what does a room do to sound?

The answer starts with reflections. Some sound reaches you directly, and the rest comes back from the room's surfaces. The first reflections arrive so soon that they seem part of the direct sound. They are called early reflections. Later reflections join into one long, fading sound.

The sound becomes quieter with each reflection, because every surface absorbs some sound. The time the sound takes to fade away is the most common measure of a room. A graph of the level falling after the sound stops is the decay curve. A larger room, or one with less absorption, usually takes longer to fade.

The fade also changes what you hear. If most sound arrives early, words and notes stay separate. If the late part is louder, they mix together. This balance is called clarity. Speech usually needs more of it than music. Concert halls are also judged by how loud and how close the sound seems.

Low notes behave differently, because their waves can be as long as the room. A low note becomes louder when a whole number of half waves fits between two opposite walls. The bass then changes from place to place. These bass frequencies, set by the room's size, are called room modes. Each mode is a resonance, so its note fades slowly unless absorption damps it. At higher frequencies, so many modes overlap that the level becomes more even. The frequency where this begins is called the Schroeder frequency. A related problem happens near a wall: the wall's reflection can cancel some of the speaker's direct bass.

Designers control all of this with surfaces. Soft material full of small air spaces absorbs sound by turning it into heat. Low frequencies are hardest to absorb, so they usually need thick material or a panel built to vibrate at low frequencies. Uneven hard surfaces absorb little sound and spread each reflection in many directions. Such a surface is called a diffusor. It keeps sound reflecting without strong single echoes.

A room must also keep out sound from other places. Heavy walls with no gaps block more sound, but low frequencies pass through more easily. Sound can also go around a wall, through floors, ceilings or ducts. That route is called a flanking path. Heating and cooling machines inside the building add steady background noise. This noise is often rated against standard curves, with a recommended limit for each kind of room.
