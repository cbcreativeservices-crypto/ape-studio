Topic Foundations: Acoustic Principles and Room Behavior

Imagine you play one guitar note in an empty concrete hall. The sound continues for a moment. In a bedroom with curtains and carpet, it stops almost at once. The room made the difference. So this topic asks: what does a room do to sound?

The answer starts with reflections. Some sound reaches your ears directly, and the rest comes back from the walls, floor and ceiling. The first reflections arrive so soon that we hear them as part of the direct sound. They are called early reflections. Later reflections join into one long, fading sound.

The sound becomes quieter with each reflection, because every surface absorbs some sound. This fading time is the most common measure of a room. A graph of the level dropping after the sound stops is called the decay curve. A larger room, or one with less absorption, usually takes longer to fade. This time can be estimated from the room's size and surfaces.

The fade also changes what you hear. If most sound arrives early, words and notes stay separate. If the late part is louder, they mix together. This balance is called clarity. Speech usually needs more of it than music.

Low notes have long waves, sometimes as long as the room. A low note becomes louder when exactly half of its wave, or two or more halves, fits between two opposite walls. The bass is then too loud in some places and too quiet in others. These bass frequencies, set by the room's size, are called room modes. Above the Schroeder frequency, so many modes overlap that the level becomes more even. The same kind of canceling happens near a wall, where a speaker's reflection can weaken some bass.

Designers control all of this with surfaces. Soft material full of small air spaces absorbs sound by turning it into heat. Low frequencies are hardest to absorb, so they usually need thick material or a panel built to vibrate across a narrow range of low frequencies. Uneven hard surfaces absorb little sound and spread each reflection in many directions. Such a surface is called a diffusor. It keeps the room lively without strong single echoes.

A room must also stop sound coming in from other places. Heavy walls with no gaps block more sound, but low frequencies pass through more easily. Sound can also go around a wall, through floors, ceilings or ducts. That route is called a flanking path. Other noise starts inside the building. Heating and cooling machines add steady background noise. This noise is often rated against standard curves, and each kind of room usually has a recommended limit.
