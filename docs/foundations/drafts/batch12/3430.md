Topic Foundations: Room Treatment

Imagine setting up two speakers on a desk in a new apartment to mix music. Some low notes sound much louder than others, and the singer's voice seems to come partly from the side wall. The sound reflects from the walls, the ceiling and the desk. Materials added to control a room's reflections are called acoustic treatment. This topic answers one question: which materials control a room's sound, and where do they go?

One tool removes sound instead of reflecting it. Sound can enter a soft material full of small air spaces, such as mineral wool or foam. There the moving air rubs against the material, and some of the sound's energy becomes heat. A material that works in this way is called a porous absorber. A thin one reduces mostly high frequencies. A thicker one, or one mounted with air behind it, also reduces lower frequencies.

The lowest frequencies need more than this. They collect in the corners of a room, where some low notes become much louder than others. A deep absorber placed in a corner to reduce them is called a bass trap. Some bass traps are thick blocks of soft material. Others are a sealed box with a flexible front sheet. Low sound moves the sheet, and this movement uses the sound's energy.

But a room with almost no reflections sounds unnatural. So another tool keeps the sound energy and changes its direction. A hard surface with blocks or slots of different depths sends sound out in many directions. That surface is called a diffuser. Hard panels can also aim sound toward an audience.

Placement matters as much as the tool. In a mixing room, reflections from the side walls and ceiling reach your ears just after the direct sound. The spot on the surface where each of these reflections bounces is called the first reflection point. One way to find it is the mirror method. You sit in the listening seat while a helper moves a mirror along the wall. Wherever you see a speaker in the mirror, place a panel there. Often the front of the room absorbs sound and the back spreads it in many directions.

Treatment can also be hung, printed, or moved. Panels hang below the ceiling, and rugs cover hard floors. Some panels have pictures printed on them. Anything hung overhead needs hardware rated for its weight, because a falling panel can hurt someone. Some treatment is movable. Screens around drums reduce sound reaching other microphones. Curtains let a hall become more or less reflective.

The flashcards cover planning and placement, absorbing materials, bass traps and resonant panels, and diffusers and reflectors. They also cover ceiling and decorative panels, mounting hardware, and movable treatment. By the end, you will know what each type of treatment does to sound, and how a room is planned so it sounds controlled and natural.
