Topic Foundations: Signal and Transfer-Function Analysis

Imagine you put a new speaker on your desk and play a familiar song. It sounds different from your old one, but you cannot tell which frequencies changed. So this topic asks: what does a system, such as a speaker, do to a signal, and how do you know the measurement is correct?

The answer starts with a comparison. An analyzer receives two signals: the one sent into the system, and the one recorded by a microphone. The analyzer first finds the microphone's delay and lines up the two signals. At each frequency, it divides what came out by what went in. The result is called the transfer function.

The transfer function has two parts. How much the system raises or lowers each frequency is its level. The time shift of each frequency is called the phase response. Phase and time are linked. The faster the phase changes from one frequency to the next, the later those frequencies arrive. This delay is called group delay.

Not every reading is equally reliable. Noise, late echoes or distortion can reach the microphone. So the analyzer gives each frequency a reliability score. A high score means the output follows the input steadily. This score is called coherence.

The same information can be shown over time. If one very short click goes in, what comes back is called the impulse response. In practice, a longer test signal is played, and the response to one click is calculated from it. The Fourier transform turns this time view into frequencies and back again. Computers use a fast version. Related methods study a sound in short parts to show how its frequencies change over time. So the impulse response and the transfer function are two views of one system.

These views also show whether an equalizer can fix a problem. In many speakers and simple filters, the delay at each frequency can be calculated from the levels alone. Such a system adds the least delay possible for its levels. It is called a minimum-phase system, and an equalizer can usually correct it. Extra delay that the levels do not predict, often from a reflection, usually cannot be corrected by an ordinary equalizer.

Every step so far uses the same kind of mathematics. A complex number stores size and timing in one value. The way a system changes any signal can be calculated from its impulse response. That calculation is called convolution. These methods usually assume the output changes in proportion to the input, and the system does not change over time. Digital filters are designed with the same mathematics.

The flashcards cover the measurement steps, the two views, and the mathematics behind them. By the end, you will know how a system's response is measured, what its level and timing parts mean, when the data can be trusted, and which mathematics links them.
