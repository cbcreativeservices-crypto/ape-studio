Topic Foundations: Frequency and Spectrum Analysis

Imagine a sound system in a hall that makes a low hum. You can hear the hum, but your ears cannot tell you its exact frequency. A measurement tool can. It splits the sound into separate frequencies and draws them as a chart. That chart is called a spectrum. But how does the tool make it, and when is it accurate?

The tool records a short piece of the sound. A fast calculation turns that piece into many evenly spaced frequency points. Each point measures the energy in a narrow range of frequencies. That point is called a frequency bin. A longer piece gives closer bins, so two close low notes appear separately. But the chart then reacts more slowly. A shorter piece reacts fast but mixes nearby frequencies together. This limit is called the time-frequency tradeoff.

Cutting out a piece also creates sudden edges, and the calculation treats those cuts as extra sound. So the energy of one pure tone spreads into the bins around it. A quiet tone near a loud one can disappear. This is called spectral leakage. To reduce it, the tool fades each piece in and out at its edges. Different fade shapes suit different jobs.

Even with a good fade, one piece gives a chart that changes quickly up and down. So the tool averages many pieces into one steadier chart. Some methods give more weight to the newest pieces, so the chart still follows a changing signal. Averaging makes the display steadier, but most averaging does not remove the noise in the room.

The evenly spaced bins create one more problem. Our hearing works by ratios. A note at double the frequency sounds one octave higher. With even spacing, most bins are in the high frequencies. So many tools group the bins into bands that each cover the same fraction of an octave. This is called fractional-octave banding. Wide bands show the overall balance, and narrow bands show small peaks and dips. Smoothing the line across nearby frequencies makes it easier to read, but too much can hide a narrow problem.

Some displays also add time. One shows frequency against time, with color for level. Another places many charts one behind the other to show how each frequency fades after a sound stops. That is called a waterfall plot. A frequency that continues too long appears as a long raised line, which often shows a resonance. Some sharp peaks in these displays come from the settings, not from the sound.

The flashcards explain each part in detail. They cover the analyzer, the bins, the fade shapes, averaging, the bands, and displays over time. By the end, you will know how an analyzer makes its chart of the frequencies, and how a real problem differs from a result of its settings.
