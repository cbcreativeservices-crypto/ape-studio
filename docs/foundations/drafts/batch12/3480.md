Topic Foundations: Test and Measurement Instruments

Imagine two people at a concert. One says the music is too loud, and the other says it is fine. Ears get tired, and listeners hear differently. An engineer or a health inspector needs a reading that another person could repeat. So this topic asks which instruments turn sound and signals into numbers people can trust.

The first step is to change sound into an electrical signal without changing the sound. A stage microphone often makes some frequencies louder than others. A microphone built to respond almost equally to all frequencies is called a measurement microphone. When it is connected to a circuit that shows loudness as one number in decibels, the instrument is called a sound level meter. Meters are made in two classes, and some laws state which class must be used.

A reading is only useful if it is correct. Every meter's reading changes slowly as the meter gets older. Before and after each job, a small device is fitted over the microphone. It makes a steady tone at a known level, and the meter is set to read that level. This device is called a calibrator. A laboratory checks the calibrator against its own reference, which is checked against a national standard. This unbroken chain of checks is called traceability.

A calibrated meter is enough to measure the noise in a room. Testing a loudspeaker also needs a known input, so you can compare what goes in with what comes out. A device that makes test signals is called a signal generator. It can make one pure tone, a tone that rises from low to high frequencies, or a steady hiss that contains all frequencies at once. Some noise signals are made so their level changes more like music.

Next, an analyzer compares the output with the input. It is often a computer program. It draws the result as lines on a graph, and one line can be saved to compare with a new one. Loudspeakers are often tested in a room whose walls absorb almost all sound, so almost no reflections reach the microphone. Headphones are tested on a model of a human head with microphones in its ears.

Some problems are electrical, not acoustic. A basic electrical meter measures voltage and resistance, and shows whether a wire is broken. An instrument that draws the shape of an electrical signal over time is called an oscilloscope. Small testers check cables, microphone power, and whether a loudspeaker is connected with its two wires in the correct order.
