Topic Foundations: Recording, Mixing and Troubleshooting Concepts

Imagine you record a band. Later, on a phone, the bass is almost gone, the voice is hard to hear, and there are small clicks. The cause could be the equipment, its settings, the mix, or your hearing. So how do you find which one it is?

Start with the level. Sound passes from the microphone through a preamplifier and a converter, and each step has a maximum level. Above it, the tops of the sound wave are cut flat, and this distortion cannot be fully removed. If a level is too low, the hiss each step adds becomes easy to hear. So each step needs a high enough level, with room left below its maximum for sharp sounds such as drum hits.

Even with good levels, a digital system can fail on timing. The computer handles the sound as numbers, in small groups. The delay between sound entering and leaving the system is called audio latency. Larger groups cause fewer clicks but more delay, so a singer hears their voice late. Clicks can also appear when two connected devices both try to control the timing.

The same kind of timing problem happens between microphones. Two microphones at different distances from a drum receive its sound at different moments. Added together, some frequencies cancel. Moving a microphone or adding a small delay reduces this. A cable with two wires swapped turns its signal upside down, and then low frequencies cancel against the other microphone. That is called a polarity mismatch, and inverting the signal again corrects it.

When the microphones work together, each sound needs a place between the two speakers. Its level in each speaker decides where you hear it. When both play it equally, you hear it in the middle, where the voice and bass usually go. Other parts often go to the sides. Many phones combine both sides into one channel, and then sounds that differ in timing between the sides can cancel. Whether a mix still sounds correct in one channel is called mono compatibility.

Once each part has its place, the parts must be balanced. A loud sound can hide a softer one in the same frequency range. That is called level masking. So the main part is often the loudest. Engineers often give each part its own main frequency range.

Even a balanced mix can be judged wrongly, because of how people hear. At low volume, bass seems weaker. A louder version usually seems better, even when it is not. So engineers compare their mix with a finished song they know well, at the same loudness. That song is called a reference track.

The flashcards explain each problem and the check that finds it. By the end, you will know the common causes of a poor recording, and whether each comes from the equipment, the mix, or your hearing.
