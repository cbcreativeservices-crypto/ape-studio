Topic Foundations: Recording, Mixing and Troubleshooting Concepts

Imagine you record a band. Later, on a phone, the bass is almost gone, the voice is hard to hear, and there are small clicks. The cause could be the equipment, its settings, the mix, or your hearing. So how do you find out?

Start with the level. Sound passes from the microphone through a preamplifier, which raises its level, and a converter, which turns it into numbers. Each step has a maximum level. Above it, the tops of the sound wave are cut flat, which cannot be fully undone. If a level is too low, the hiss each step adds becomes audible. So each step needs a high enough level, with room left below its maximum for sudden loud sounds.

Even with good levels, a digital system can fail on timing. The computer handles the numbers in small groups. The delay between sound entering and leaving the system is called audio latency. Larger groups cause fewer clicks but more delay, so a singer hears their voice late.

A different timing problem happens between microphones. Two microphones at different distances from a drum receive its sound at different moments. Added together, some frequencies cancel. Moving a microphone or adding a small delay reduces this. A different fault is not about time. A cable with two wires swapped makes its signal go down when the other microphone's signal goes up. Then much of the sound cancels, most of all the low frequencies. That is called a polarity mismatch, and inverting the signal again corrects it.

Next, each sound needs a place between the two speakers. Its level in each speaker decides where you hear it. Equal levels place it in the middle, where the voice and bass usually go. Many phones combine both sides into one channel, where sounds that differ in timing between sides can cancel. Whether a mix still sounds correct in one channel is called mono compatibility.

Then the parts must be balanced. A loud sound can hide a softer one in the same frequency range. That is called level masking. So engineers decide which part matters most and often make it loudest, and they often give each part its own main frequency range.

Even a balanced mix can be misjudged, because of how people hear. At low volume, bass seems weaker. A louder version usually seems better, even when it is not. Tired ears and the sound of the room can also change what you hear. So engineers compare their mix with a finished song they know well, at the same loudness. That song is called a reference track. They also check the mix on small speakers and in a car.

The flashcards explain each problem and the check that finds it. By the end, you will know the common causes of a poor recording or mix, where each one comes from, and how it is checked.
