Topic Foundations: Recording, Mixing and Troubleshooting Concepts

Imagine you record a band. When you play it later on a phone, the bass is almost gone, the voice lacks some frequencies, and there are small clicks. The cause could be the equipment, its settings, the mix, or your hearing. So how do you find which one it is?

Start with the level. The sound passes from the microphone to a preamplifier, then to a converter that turns it into numbers. Each step has a maximum level. Above it, the tops of the sound wave are cut flat, and that distortion cannot be fully removed later. If a level is too low, the hiss that each step adds becomes easy to hear. So each step needs a high enough level, safely below its maximum.

A digital system must also control timing. The computer handles the numbers in small groups. The delay between sound entering and leaving the system is called audio latency. Larger groups cause fewer clicks, but a singer hears their voice late. Smaller groups reduce the delay, but a computer doing a lot of work may then make clicks. Clicks also appear when two devices both try to set the timing.

Timing also matters between microphones. Two microphones at different distances from a drum receive its sound at different moments. Added together, some of its frequencies cancel. A cable with two wires swapped turns its signal upside down. Added to the other microphone, many low frequencies cancel. That is called a polarity mismatch. Engineers invert the signal again, move a microphone, or add a small delay.

Left and right speakers also work together. When both play the same sound equally, you hear it in the middle. That spot is called the center image. The voice and bass usually go there, other parts go to the sides, and sounds with more room echo seem further back. Many phones combine both sides into one channel, where sounds whose left and right signals are out of step can cancel. Whether a mix still works in one channel is called mono compatibility. One channel also makes another problem easier to hear: a loud sound can hide a softer one in the same frequency range. That is called level masking.

Your ears can also give you wrong information about the mix. At low volume, bass seems weaker. A louder version usually seems better, even when it is not. So engineers compare their mix with a finished song they know well, at the same loudness. That song is called a reference track. They also try to prove their first guess wrong.

The flashcards cover levels and overload, digital timing, timing between microphones, stereo and mono, balance between parts, and listening without being misled. By the end, you will know why a recording goes wrong, and how to tell whether the cause is the equipment, the mix, or your hearing.
