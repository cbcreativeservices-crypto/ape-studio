Topic Foundations: Soldering

Imagine opening the plug of a guitar cable. Inside, each wire is held to a metal tab by a small drop of metal. That metal is called solder. It melts at a fairly low temperature, flows around the wire and the tab, and hardens into one solid connection. So how is such a connection made, checked and removed safely?

Safety comes first. The tip of the soldering iron, the hot tool used for this work, can burn skin instantly and start a fire. So the iron stays in its stand, the work is placed on a heat-proof mat, and the iron is switched off when you leave. The smoke comes from the burning chemical cleaner inside the solder, and breathing it can cause asthma for life. A small fan with a filter pulls it away from your face. Some solder contains poisonous lead, so you never eat at the bench and you wash your hands afterward.

The iron heats a replaceable metal tip. A wide, flat tip moves heat into the connection quickly. Many irons hold the tip at a set temperature, and solder without lead needs a hotter tip. A fine tip suits the tiny parts that sit on the surface of many circuit boards.

Heat alone is not enough, because metal has a thin, invisible layer of tarnish on its surface. A chemical cleaner removes it as the metal heats. That cleaner is called flux, and most solder wire has some inside. On clean, hot metal, melted solder spreads out thin and bonds to the surface. That is called wetting.

Good wetting leaves a visible shape. The solder forms a slope that curves inward from the copper area on the circuit board to the wire. That slope is called a fillet. A rounded ball often means the solder is only resting on the metal. A lumpy, weak joint made on parts that were not hot enough is a cold solder joint. Joints also fail when moved while cooling, or when solder connects two points that must stay apart.

Sometimes a joint must be undone to replace a part. Melting the solder and taking it away is called desoldering. A copper braid absorbs melted solder, and a spring-loaded pump pulls it up by suction. Too much heat or force can tear the copper area off the board. Tiny surface parts are often removed with a hot air tool that melts all their joints at once. To repair a bad joint, you usually remove the old solder, add fresh flux, and solder it again.

The flashcards explain each idea, along with the tools and materials used at the bench. By the end, you will know how a good joint is made and checked, and how joints are undone and repaired.
