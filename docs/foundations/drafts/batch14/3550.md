Topic Foundations: Soldering

Imagine opening the plug of a guitar cable. Inside, each wire is held to a metal tab by a small drop of metal. That metal is called solder. It melts at a fairly low temperature, flows around both, and hardens into one solid connection. So how is such a connection made, checked and removed safely?

Safety comes first. The tip of the soldering iron, the hot tool used for this work, can burn skin instantly and start a fire. So the iron stays in its stand, the work is placed on a heat-proof mat, and the iron is switched off when you leave. The smoke comes from a chemical cleaner inside the solder as it burns, and breathing it can cause asthma for life. A small fan with a filter pulls it away from your face. Some solder contains poisonous lead, so you never eat at the bench and you wash your hands afterward.

A soldering iron heats a replaceable metal tip. A wide, flat tip moves heat into the connection quickly, and a fine tip suits tiny parts that sit on the board's surface. Many irons hold the tip at a set temperature. Solder without lead needs a hotter tip.

Heat alone is not enough, because metal has a thin, dirty layer on its surface that you cannot see. A chemical cleaner removes it as the metal heats. That cleaner is called flux, and most solder wire has some inside. On clean, hot metal, melted solder spreads out thin and bonds to the surface. That is called wetting. Coating a wire or tip with a thin layer of solder before joining is called tinning.

Good wetting leaves a shape that you can see. The solder forms a slope that curves inward from the copper area on the circuit board to the wire. That slope is called a fillet. A rounded ball often means the solder is only resting on the metal without bonding to it. If the parts were not hot enough, the joint is lumpy and weak. That is a cold solder joint. Joints also fail when moved while cooling, or when solder connects two points that must stay apart.

Sometimes a joint must be undone to replace a part. Melting the solder and taking it away is called desoldering. A copper braid absorbs melted solder, and a spring-loaded pump pulls it up by suction. Too much heat or force can tear the copper area off the board. To repair a bad joint, you usually remove the old solder, add fresh flux, and solder it again.

The flashcards cover safety, irons and tips, solder and flux, how solder bonds, joint faults, and tools for removing solder. By the end, you will know how a good joint is made and checked, and how joints are undone and repaired.
