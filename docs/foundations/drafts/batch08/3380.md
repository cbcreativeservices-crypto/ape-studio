Topic Foundations: Delay and Time-Based Effects

Imagine you shout once toward a large stone wall far away. A moment later, you hear your voice again, a little quieter. Many songs use repeats of this kind on purpose, made by equipment instead of a wall. So how do you make repeats that arrive at the right time and in the right position between the speakers, and that help the music?

The basic tool records the sound, waits, and plays it back. The gap between the sound and its repeat is called the delay time. To get more repeats, part of the output is sent back into the input. The control for this amount is called regeneration. Each repeat is usually a little quieter, so the repeats fade away. If each repeat is as loud as the last, they never stop. If each one is louder, the sound keeps growing.

Machines have stored that sound in different ways. Some used a loop of tape, a spinning drum, or a chain of tiny electronic parts. Each repeat from these older designs usually loses some high frequencies. Digital memory gives exact repeats, and it can also play one short section of music again and again.

With the sound stored, the next question is when each repeat arrives. You can set the delay time by listening, or choose a note length so the repeats follow the speed of the song. This link to the song's speed is called tempo sync. Some note lengths place the repeats between the beats, which adds a new rhythm. One delay can also read its stored sound at several points, and each point is called a tap. Each tap has its own time, level and position between left and right.

Repeats can be so short that you do not hear them as repeats. We hear two copies that arrive very close together as one sound. A very short copy in the other speaker makes an instrument seem wider. If the delay time moves slowly up and down, the pitch of the copy rises and falls slightly. Mixed with the original, this effect is called chorus, and one instrument seems to become several. An even shorter moving delay cancels some frequencies, and those frequencies move up and down.

Long repeats cause one more problem. They can make the singer's next words hard to hear. A delay that turns its repeats down while the singer sings, and lets them rise in the pauses, is called a ducked delay. Engineers can also send only one word to the delay, so only that word repeats.

The flashcards cover feedback, the machines that store sound, timing to the beat, taps and stereo, very short moving delays, and ways to keep repeats clear. By the end, you will know how delays are built, timed and placed, and how their repeats can make the music clearer or harder to hear.
