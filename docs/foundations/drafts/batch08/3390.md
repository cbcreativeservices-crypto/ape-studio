Topic Foundations: Creative Effects and Sound Design

Imagine a new song. The singer's voice sounds electronic, a guitar buzzes, and a backwards sound grows louder until the chorus starts. In earlier topics, effects mostly corrected problems, but here they are part of the music. So how can effects turn a recorded sound into a new sound, and when does that help the song?

A common method is to send a signal into a circuit at a higher level than it was designed for. The top of each sound wave is flattened, and new frequencies appear above each note. A small amount of this is called saturation. It often makes a sound easier to hear in a mix. Much more of it creates strong distortion. Other tools remove some frequencies or digital detail on purpose, so a voice seems to come through an old telephone. Others multiply two sounds, creating new tones that were not in either sound.

Saturation does not change its effect over time. Other effects keep changing it. A slow, repeating control signal moves one setting up and down. When it moves the volume, the effect is called tremolo. It can also move the sound between the speakers, or move the pitch slightly. Some older equipment did this with a spinning speaker.

A larger pitch change moves the whole sound higher or lower, without making it longer or shorter. Added notes at other pitches can make one voice seem to be several singers. But a voice raised in pitch often loses its natural character. Every voice has louder frequency ranges, set by the shape of the throat and mouth, and they also move up. Moving those ranges while the notes stay the same is called formant shifting. Another tool uses the changing frequencies of a voice to shape a synthesizer's sound, so the synthesizer seems to speak. That tool is called a vocoder.

Editing can also change the order of a sound. A very short piece can be repeated quickly, in time with the beat. That is called a stutter edit. A sound can also be played backwards, so a cymbal hit grows louder instead of fading. Other edits slow a sound down until it stops, or hold one tiny moment as a long tone.

These edits often appear where one section of a song changes into the next. A backwards sound that grows louder and stops at the new section is called a reverse swell. A noise that rises, or a short silence, also tells the listener a change is coming. Because these effects attract attention, they usually work best in small amounts.

The flashcards cover distortion, tools that remove detail, effects that change over time, pitch and voice tools, edits that repeat or reverse sound, and changes between sections. By the end, you will know how each kind of effect creates something new, and why a small amount is often the better choice.
