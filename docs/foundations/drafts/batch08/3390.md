Topic Foundations: Creative Effects and Sound Design

Imagine a new song. The singer's voice sounds electronic, a guitar buzzes, and a backwards sound grows louder until the chorus starts. Here the effects are part of the music itself. So how can effects turn a recorded sound into a new sound, and when does that help the song?

One method sends a signal into a circuit at a higher level than it was designed for. The top of each sound wave is flattened, and this adds new, higher frequencies related to each note. A small amount of this is called saturation. It often makes a sound easier to hear in a mix. Much more of it creates strong distortion. Other tools lower the sound quality on purpose.

Some effects also change over time. A slow, repeating control signal moves one setting up and down. When it moves the volume, the effect is called tremolo. It can also move the sound between the speakers, or move the pitch slightly. A rotating speaker changes volume, pitch and direction together. A phaser mixes the sound with a copy that is shifted in time by a different amount at each frequency. Some frequencies cancel, and the control signal moves those gaps up and down.

Pitch shifting can move the whole sound higher or lower without changing its length. But a voice raised in pitch often stops sounding like the same person. Every voice has louder frequency ranges, set by the shape of the throat and mouth, and they also move up. Moving those ranges while the notes stay the same is called formant shifting. A vocoder uses the changing frequencies of a voice to shape a synthesizer's sound, so the words can be heard in the synthesizer's notes.

Some digital tools split a sound into many narrow frequency bands and change each band. One tool holds the bands of one tiny moment, so a short note becomes a long, steady tone. That is called a spectral freeze. Another gives the frequency pattern of one sound to a different sound.

Editing can also change the order of a sound. A very short piece can be repeated quickly, in time with the beat. That is called a stutter edit. A sound can also be played backwards, so a cymbal hit grows louder instead of fading.

These edits often appear where one section of a song changes into the next. A backwards sound that grows louder and stops at the new section is called a reverse swell. A rising noise, a short silence, or a sound that slows down until it stops can also mark the change. Because these effects attract attention, engineers often use them only at important moments.

The flashcards explain each of these effects and edits in detail. By the end, you will know how each kind of effect creates something new, and when each one helps the song.
