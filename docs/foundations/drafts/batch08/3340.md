Topic Foundations: Equalization

Imagine a music player with a bass knob for low sounds and a treble knob for high sounds. Turn up the bass, and the low drum notes grow louder, but the voice barely changes. Most sounds mix low, middle and high frequencies. A device that raises or lowers chosen frequencies is called an equalizer. So which frequencies can it change, by how much, and why?

An equalizer divides the frequency range into sections called bands, and each band has a shape. One shape changes a range around one center frequency. Its curve has the shape of a bell, so it is called a bell. Another shape raises or lowers everything below a chosen frequency, and it is called a low shelf. The same change above a chosen frequency is called a high shelf. A filter strongly reduces everything above or below a chosen frequency. A notch strongly reduces one narrow range.

Next, how wide is each change? For a bell, a number called Q sets the width. A high Q changes a narrow range, and a low Q changes a wide range. For a filter, the slope tells how fast the level falls past its set frequency. A unit with frequency, amount and width controls on every band is called a parametric equalizer. Another type has many fixed bands side by side, each with its own slider, and it is called a graphic equalizer.

Equalizers also affect timing. Most equalizers shift some frequencies slightly later than others. This design is called minimum phase. Another design, called linear phase, delays every frequency by the same amount, so the timing between frequencies stays the same. Its disadvantages are extra delay and sometimes a faint sound just before a sharp drum hit.

Each filter design gives up something to gain something else. One design keeps the level of the frequencies it lets through most even. Another keeps the timing of sudden sounds most accurate, but its slope is more gradual. Others are steeper, but the level rises and falls slightly near the set frequency.

Some problems appear only sometimes, such as one loud note. One band can lower its own frequencies only when they get too loud. That is called dynamic equalization.

Which change should you make? Often the goal is correction. Two instruments with shared frequencies can make each other hard to hear, so engineers often lower that range in one. Other changes are creative, such as more high frequencies on a voice. A measurement can show how a room changes the sound of the speakers, and an equalizer can reduce some of those changes. That is called room correction.
