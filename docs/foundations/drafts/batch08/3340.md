Topic Foundations: Equalization

Imagine a music player with a bass knob for low sounds and a treble knob for high sounds. Turn up the bass, and the low drum notes grow louder while the voice stays almost the same. Every sound is a combination of low, middle and high frequencies. A device that raises or lowers chosen frequencies is called an equalizer. So which frequencies can it change, by how much, and why?

An equalizer divides the frequency range into sections called bands, and each band has a shape. One shape changes a range around one center frequency. Its curve has the shape of a bell, so it is called a bell. Another shape raises or lowers everything above or below a chosen frequency. Below, it is called a low shelf, and above, a high shelf. A filter strongly reduces everything above or below a chosen frequency, or one very narrow range.

Next, how wide is each change? For a bell, a number called Q sets the width. A high Q changes a narrow range, and a low Q changes a wide range. For a filter, the slope tells how fast the level falls past the filter's set frequency. A unit with frequency, amount and width controls on every band is called a parametric equalizer. Another type has a row of sliders, one for each fixed band. Nearby bands overlap, so the real result can differ from what the sliders show.

Every equalizer also affects timing. Most designs shift some frequencies slightly in time. Another design delays every frequency equally, so their timing stays aligned. That is called linear phase. The disadvantage is extra delay, and sometimes a faint sound just before a sharp drum hit. Filter designs also differ. Each design balances an even level, a steep slope and even timing in its own way.

Some problems appear only sometimes, such as one note that is too loud. In the topic on compression, limiting and gating, you met the compressor, which lowers loud sound. One band can work in the same way on its own frequencies. That is called dynamic equalization. Other tools copy the frequency balance of a recording you like.

Last, which change should you make? Often the goal is correction: you find a frequency that keeps sounding too long or makes the sound unclear, and lower it. Two instruments that share frequencies can make each other hard to hear, so engineers often lower a range in one. Other changes are creative and give a sound a new character.

The flashcards cover band shapes, width and slope, equalizer designs, timing and filter types, and equalizers that react to sound. They also cover equalizers in a mix and for room correction. By the end, you will know what each kind of equalizer change does to a sound, and why it is chosen.
