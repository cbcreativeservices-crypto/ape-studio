Topic Foundations: Equalization

Imagine a music player with a bass knob for low sounds and a treble knob for high sounds. Turn up the bass, and low drum notes grow louder. Most sounds contain low, middle and high frequencies. A device that raises or lowers chosen frequencies is called an equalizer. So which frequencies can it change, by how much, and why?

An equalizer is split into sections called bands. Each band works on its own part of the frequency range and has a shape. One shape, called a bell, changes a range around one center frequency. Another shape raises or lowers everything below or above a chosen frequency. It is called a low shelf or a high shelf. A filter strongly reduces some frequencies. It can cut those above or below a chosen point, outside a chosen range, or in one narrow range.

How wide is each change? For a bell, a number called Q sets the width. A high Q changes a narrow range. A low Q changes a wide range. Nearby bands overlap, so their changes add together. For a filter, the slope tells how fast the level falls past its set frequency. Controls differ too. A parametric equalizer has frequency, amount and width on every band. A graphic equalizer has many fixed bands, each with its own slider.

Filters also differ in what they do to the sound they keep. Some keep that sound's level even. Some cut more steeply, but that level varies slightly. Some keep all frequencies together in time, but cut more gradually. Most equalizers make some frequencies arrive slightly later than others, which can change sudden sounds. One kind delays every frequency equally instead. The downside is extra delay and sometimes a faint sound just before a sudden loud sound.

These changes are fixed, but some problems appear only sometimes, such as one loud note. A band can act only when its frequencies become too loud or too quiet. That is called dynamic equalization.

Which change should you make? Often the goal is correction, such as a narrow cut that removes a hum. Two instruments with shared frequencies can make each other hard to hear, so engineers often lower that range in one. Other changes are creative, such as more high frequencies on a voice.

Some equalizers help with these choices by measuring the sound first. One kind changes a sound to match the frequency balance of another. Another uses test sounds, sometimes steady noise, to reduce some changes a room makes to its speakers' sound. That is called room correction.
