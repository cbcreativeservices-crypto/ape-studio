Topic Foundations: Equalization

Imagine a music player with a bass knob for low sounds and a treble knob for high sounds. Turn up the bass, and the low drum notes grow louder, but the voice barely changes. Most sounds mix low, middle and high frequencies. A device that raises or lowers chosen frequencies is called an equalizer. So which frequencies can it change, by how much, and why?

An equalizer divides the frequency range into sections called bands, and each band has a shape. One shape, called a bell because of its curve, changes a range around one center frequency. Another shape raises or lowers everything below a chosen frequency, and it is called a low shelf. The same change above a chosen frequency is called a high shelf. A filter strongly reduces everything above or below a chosen frequency.

Next, how wide is each change? For a bell, a number called Q sets the width. A high Q changes a narrow range, and a low Q changes a wide range. For a filter, the slope tells how fast the level falls past its set frequency. A unit with frequency, amount and width controls on every band is called a parametric equalizer. A unit with many fixed bands, each with its own slider, is called a graphic equalizer.

Filters differ in more than slope. Every filter design has an advantage and a disadvantage. One filter design keeps the level of the frequencies it lets through as even as possible. Another keeps the timing of sudden sounds most accurate, but its slope is more gradual. Others are steeper, but the level rises and falls slightly near the set frequency.

Equalizers also affect timing. Most equalizers shift some frequencies slightly later than others. Another design delays every frequency by the same amount, so their timing stays the same. Its disadvantages are extra delay and sometimes a faint sound just before a sharp drum hit.

These changes stay the same for the whole song. But some problems appear only sometimes, such as one loud note. One band can change its own frequencies only when they become too loud or too quiet. That is called dynamic equalization. Other tools change a sound to match the frequency balance of a second sound.

Which change should you make? Often the goal is correction. Two instruments with shared frequencies can make each other hard to hear, so engineers often lower that range in one. Other changes are creative, such as more high frequencies on a voice. An equalizer can also reduce some changes a room makes to the sound of its speakers. That is called room correction.
