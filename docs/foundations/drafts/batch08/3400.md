Topic Foundations: Advanced Parallel & Bus Processing

Earlier, you learned that a compressor makes loud and soft parts more even by lowering the loud parts. But it changes the whole sound. A setting that turns the sound down a lot can also reduce the sharp start of each drum hit. This topic asks the next question: how do you add the effect of a tool and still keep the original sound?

The answer is to use two copies of the same sound. One copy, the treated copy, goes through the tool, often with extreme settings. The other copy stays untouched, and it is called the dry signal. Then you add a small amount of the treated copy to the original. This method is called parallel processing. A control sets how much of each copy you hear.

A compressor is the most common tool on the treated copy. That copy raises the quiet details, while the dry signal keeps the sharp start of each hit. Distortion and other tools are often used in the same way. There is one risk. Some tools delay the sound very slightly. Then the two copies no longer arrive at the same time, and some frequencies cancel when they are mixed.

Another way to add to a sound is to combine several recordings of the same part. The small differences in timing and tuning between them make the sound fuller, and wider when spread. This is called instrument layering. Other layers play the same notes an octave higher or lower, or add a deep tone under a bass.

With many tracks, engineers often send related tracks, such as all the drums, through one shared path. One compressor there reacts to all the drums together, so they rise and fall as one group. This is called bus processing. Grouping also shows where sounds compete. A kick drum and a bass both have many low frequencies, so together they are hard to hear separately. The kick can make only the low frequencies of the bass quieter for a moment on each hit. That is called frequency-selective ducking.

Inside each path, tools are connected one after another, and each tool works on what the previous one gives it. An equalizer before a compressor changes what the compressor reacts to. After it, the equalizer changes only the balance of frequencies you hear. This is called processing order. A common starting point is to fix problems first and add distortion later, but no single order is correct for every sound.

The flashcards cover the blend controls, the tools used on the treated copy and the timing problems between copies. Then they cover layers, groups, ways to keep each sound clear when sounds share frequencies, and the order of tools. By the end, you will know how treated and untreated sound are combined, and why grouping and order change the result.
