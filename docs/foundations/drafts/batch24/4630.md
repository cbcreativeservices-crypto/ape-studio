Topic Foundations: Ultrasonics and Industrial Inspection

You may have seen a hospital scan of an unborn baby. A probe sends sound that is too high for people to hear, and its echoes become a picture. Factories and pipelines have a similar need. A weld or a steel pipe can contain a hidden crack, and cutting it open would destroy it. So how can sound find hidden damage while the part stays whole?

First, the sound must enter the part. A thin layer of air stops almost all of this sound, so a gel or water fills the gap. That material is called a couplant. The probe sends a short pulse. The far side of the part returns an echo, and a crack returns an earlier one, called a flaw echo. Its travel time gives its depth.

Travel times are only useful when the instrument is set up correctly. So the inspector first checks it on a steel piece with holes of known size and depth. That piece is called a calibration block. Its echoes set the distance scale and the sensitivity.

Next, the echoes must be shown. On the simplest graph, the height of each peak shows the level of an echo, and its position shows the distance. That graph is called an A-scan. Other graphs show a side view of the part or a map from above. Many probes contain a row of tiny elements, switched on at slightly different times to aim the beam. That method is called phased array ultrasound.

A peak on the screen is not always damage. The edge of a weld also returns an echo. The inspector decides which signals are real flaws, then estimates their size, often by moving the probe until the echo becomes weaker. No method finds every flaw. Written rules decide which flaws are acceptable, and inspectors are certified at different levels.

Other waves travel far along a pipe and return from places where rust has damaged the metal. Concrete can be tapped with a hammer, and its vibration shows hidden gaps. Sound travel time can also measure the force in a bolt or the speed of a liquid.

Other methods receive sound that the object itself makes. A growing crack releases a small burst of sound when force is applied to it. That burst is called acoustic emission, and sensors can locate its source. Running machines make noise too. A group of microphones can show the loud spots as colors on a photo. It can show where a leak or a worn bearing, the part that lets a shaft turn, makes noise. The frequency of a vibration often shows which part is damaged.

The flashcards explain each of these methods, together with the flaws they find and the rules inspectors follow. By the end, you will know how sound finds and measures damage, and how inspectors decide what each signal means.
