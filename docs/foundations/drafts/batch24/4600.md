Topic Foundations: Aeroacoustics, Vibration and Structural Acoustics

Imagine a windy day. A wire above you makes a steady tone. A fan hums, and a window shakes when a truck drives past. This topic asks how motion becomes sound, and how engineers predict and reduce it.

Moving air comes first. Fast-moving air stops flowing evenly and forms swirls. The pressure in the swirls changes a lot, but most of that change stays with the flow. Only a small part travels away as sound. To find it, engineers rearrange the equations for moving air, so the moving air is treated as a sound source in still air. That method is called an acoustic analogy. It shows that sound becomes much louder when the air moves only a little faster.

The exhaust of a jet aircraft shows this. It mixes with still air and makes a very loud noise, so modern jets usually push a lot of air out more slowly, not a little air very fast. Flow past an object can also make one tone. Air passing a wire forms swirls on one side and then the other. This is called vortex shedding. Air over a cavity or a sharp edge can make a tone in a similar way.

Each blade of a fan or propeller pushes the air as it passes, so the pushes repeat at a steady rate. That rate, called the blade-passing frequency, sets a tone. Swirling air leaving the back edge of each blade adds a hiss. Wings, flaps and wheels add noise when an aircraft lands.

Flow can also shake a structure. If the swirls repeat at about the frequency at which the wire vibrates most easily, the wire vibrates strongly. A structure has several such natural frequencies. At each one, it moves in a fixed pattern, with some points moving a lot and others still. That pattern is called a mode shape. Materials and joints turn some of the motion into heat. This damping controls how quickly vibration fades.

To find these, engineers often tap a structure with a hammer that measures force, while sensors record the motion. How fast a point moves for each unit of force is called mechanical mobility. At high frequencies a structure has too many modes to follow, so another method tracks how vibration energy flows between connected parts.

A vibrating structure sends sound into the air. On a panel, neighboring areas often move in opposite directions, so their sound partly cancels. How well a surface turns motion into sound is called radiation efficiency. To reduce noise, engineers add damping layers, soft mounts, tuned weights on springs, duct silencers and sealed boxes around machines.
