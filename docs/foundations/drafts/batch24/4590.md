Topic Foundations: Data Sonification and Auditory Display

Imagine a car reversing toward a wall. A sensor beeps faster as the car gets closer. The driver never looks away from the mirror but knows the distance. A system that presents information through sound is called an auditory display. This topic asks how sound can present information so a listener understands it quickly and correctly.

The answer starts with how people listen. The ear notices a change even when the eyes are busy. When a door closes, most people hear its size and how hard it was closed, not its pitch. Most people also feel that a high note seems to come from higher up than a low note. Equal steps in sound do not always seem equal to the ear, so designers plan around these ways of hearing.

A designer can then turn numbers into sound. That process is called sonification. The most common method connects each value to one property of a sound, such as pitch, loudness, length or position. Pitch shows small changes clearly, and loudness is less exact. The designer also chooses the direction, usually a higher pitch for a bigger number.

Some data, such as recordings of the electrical activity of the brain, already have the form of a wave. They can be played directly as sound, made faster or slower until the ear can hear them. That is called audification. Other data become sound as they are measured. In this way, a nurse can hear a patient's condition change. Saved data can be played quickly for an overview, then slowly for detail.

Sound also helps people use machines. A short invented sound, such as a set of notes that means "new message," is called an earcon. People learn its meaning. A real-world sound that suggests its own meaning, such as crumpling paper when you delete a file, is called an auditory icon. These sounds also help people who cannot see a screen. A chart line can become a rising and falling pitch, and the button at a street crossing can tick so a blind person can find it.

Alarms are the hardest sounds to design. An alarm must be heard over noise and show which problem it is. The most serious problem should also sound the most serious. How urgent a sound seems to a listener is called perceived urgency. Faster and higher sounds usually seem more urgent. When alarms sound too often, or for nothing, people learn to ignore them and may miss a real alarm.

The flashcards explain how hearing affects design, ways to connect data to sound, methods of listening to data, interface sounds, cues for people who cannot see, alarms, and sound used to send data or messages over a distance. By the end, you will know how information becomes sound, and why some sounds inform listeners clearly while others confuse them.
