Topic Foundations: Physical and Nonlinear Acoustics

Imagine two people talking at the same time. Their sounds pass through each other unchanged, because ordinary sound waves simply add together. But what happens to sound when it becomes extremely loud, or when it meets materials that are not like air?

In a very loud wave, the high-pressure parts travel slightly faster than the low-pressure parts. So the front of the wave becomes steeper, until the pressure rises almost instantly. That sudden rise is called a shock wave. The wave also gains new, higher frequencies. An aircraft faster than sound makes shock waves in the air. People on the ground hear them as a loud bang, called a sonic boom.

In a liquid, very loud sound can also make bubbles. When the pressure is low, the liquid is pulled apart and tiny bubbles grow. Then they can collapse with great force and make intense heat in a tiny spot. This is called acoustic cavitation. It can clean metal parts but also damage living tissue. Loud sound also gives a small, steady push, called the acoustic radiation force. It can hold a tiny object in the air.

Even quiet sound changes when it enters a material, because it loses energy there. In a foam, air moving through narrow, twisted passages rubs against the walls and exchanges heat with them, so sound energy becomes heat. This exchange can also work in reverse. If one end of a passage is much hotter than the other, heat can add energy to a sound wave. Some engines and coolers use this effect.

Materials can also be built to control sound. They are often made of repeating parts smaller than the length of one sound wave. The parts can block a band of frequencies, bend sound the opposite way from normal materials, or guide it around an object. Such a material is called an acoustic metamaterial.

Solid materials carry more kinds of waves than air. In air, particles move only forward and back along the wave's path. In a solid, they can also move from side to side. Other waves travel only along a surface. On a small crystal chip, thin metal strips turn an electrical signal into this kind of wave, called a surface acoustic wave, and back again. Many phones use these chips as radio filters.

Most of these effects are too complex to calculate by hand, so scientists use computer models. A model divides a space into many small pieces and calculates the sound in each one. At high frequencies, other methods follow sound along straight lines. For an open space, the edges of the model must absorb waves.
