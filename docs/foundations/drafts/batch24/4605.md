Topic Foundations: Physical and Nonlinear Acoustics

Imagine two people talking at the same time. Their sounds pass through each other unchanged, because ordinary sound waves simply add together. This topic studies sound where that rule stops working, and materials that change sound in unusual ways. So what changes when sound becomes extremely loud?

In a very loud wave, the high-pressure parts travel slightly faster than the low-pressure parts. So the front of the wave becomes steeper, until the pressure rises almost instantly. That sudden rise is called a shock wave. The wave also gains new, higher frequencies. An aircraft faster than sound makes shock waves in the air. People on the ground hear them as a loud bang, called a sonic boom.

Loud sound in a liquid does more. When the pressure is low, the liquid is pulled apart and tiny bubbles grow. Then they collapse very quickly and with great force, and make intense heat in a tiny spot. This is called acoustic cavitation. It can clean metal parts but also damage living tissue. Loud sound also gives a small, steady push, called the acoustic radiation force. It can hold a tiny object in the air.

Sound of any level also loses energy inside materials. In a foam, air moving through narrow, twisted passages rubs against the walls and exchanges heat with them, so sound energy becomes heat. This exchange can also work in reverse. If one end of a passage is much hotter than the other, heat can add energy to a sound wave. Some engines and coolers use this effect.

Materials can also be built to control sound. They are made of repeating parts smaller than the length of one sound wave. The parts can block a band of frequencies, bend sound the opposite way from normal materials, or guide it around an object. Such a material is called an acoustic metamaterial.

Solids also carry more kinds of waves than air. In air, particles move only forward and back along the wave's path. In a solid, they can also move from side to side. Other waves travel only along a surface. On a small crystal chip, thin metal strips turn an electrical signal into this kind of wave, called a surface acoustic wave, and back again. Many phones use these chips as radio filters.

Scientists use computers to calculate these effects. A model divides a space into many small pieces and calculates the sound in each one. At high frequencies, other methods follow sound along straight lines. For an open space, the model's edges must absorb waves.
