Topic Foundations: Ultrasonics, Underwater and Spatial Audio

Under water in a swimming pool, voices from above almost disappear. But a knock on the pool wall is clear and loud. Light and radio travel only a short distance in the sea, so sound is used to find and measure things there. This topic asks how sound travels and is used in water, at frequencies too high for people to hear, and around a listener.

In the sea, sound travels faster in water with a higher temperature, more salt or more depth. The path of the sound bends toward slower water. Deep in the ocean is a layer where sound travels slowest, so low sounds bend back into it and travel very far. That layer is called the deep sound channel. Seawater also absorbs high frequencies faster, so low frequencies reach farther.

That reach makes sound useful for finding things. One method sends a short pulse and times its echo. Another only listens to the noise a ship makes. Both are called sonar, short for sound navigation and ranging. Each must hear its target over the noise of waves, rain, animals and ships. A calculation called the sonar equation adds the signal, subtracts the losses and the noise, and predicts whether a target can be detected.

But one underwater microphone usually receives sound from all directions, so many are placed in a line. Their signals are added with small delays, and the group receives sound mainly from one direction. A long line can be pulled behind a ship, away from its noise. Other systems map the sea floor, locate underwater vehicles or carry their data.

All these systems need a part that turns electricity into sound and back. Most use a crystal or ceramic that changes shape with a voltage, and makes a voltage when sound presses on it. That part is called a piezoelectric element. Thin elements make ultrasound. Its short waves travel in narrow beams. Many small elements switched on at slightly different times can steer and focus a beam for medical images. A metal tool vibrating at these high frequencies can join plastic parts.

Sound can also be shaped for human ears. Before a sound reaches each eardrum, your head and outer ears change its timing and frequencies, differently for each direction. That set of changes is called the head-related transfer function. Headphones can apply it, so a sound seems to come from around you. Another method stores sound from every direction as one scene. It is called Ambisonics, and it can play on any speaker layout.

The flashcards follow the same path, from the sea and its noise, through sonar and ultrasound, to sound around a listener. By the end, you will know how sound travels in the ocean, how machines use it to find things, and how it can be placed around your ears.
