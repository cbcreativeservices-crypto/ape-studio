Topic Foundations: Ultrasonics, Underwater and Spatial Audio

Under water in a swimming pool, voices from above almost disappear. But a knock on the pool wall is clear and loud. Light and radio travel only a short distance in the sea, so sound is used to find and measure things there. This topic asks how sound travels and is used in water, at frequencies too high for people to hear, and around a listener.

In the sea, sound travels faster in water with a higher temperature, more salt or more depth. The path of the sound bends toward slower water. Deep in the ocean is a layer where sound travels slowest. Low sounds bend back into it and can travel very far. That layer is called the deep sound channel. Seawater also absorbs high frequencies faster, so low frequencies reach farther.

That reach makes sound useful for finding things. One method sends a short pulse and times its echo. Another only receives the noise a ship makes. Both are called sonar, short for sound navigation and ranging. Each must detect its target over the noise of waves, rain, animals and ships. A calculation called the sonar equation adds the signal and subtracts the losses and the noise. It predicts whether a target can be detected.

One underwater microphone usually receives sound from all directions, so many are placed in a line. Their signals are added with small delays, and the group receives sound mainly from one direction. A long line can be pulled behind a ship, away from its noise. Other systems map the sea floor, locate underwater vehicles or send data.

All these systems need a part that turns electricity into sound and back. Most use a crystal or ceramic that changes shape with a voltage. It also makes a voltage when sound presses on it. That part is called a piezoelectric element. Thin elements make ultrasound, with short waves that travel in narrow beams. Many small elements switched on at slightly different times can steer and focus a beam for medical images. A metal tool vibrating at these high frequencies can also join plastic parts.

Small differences in timing also tell a listener where a sound comes from. Your head and outer ears change a sound's timing and frequencies before it reaches each eardrum, differently for each direction. That set of changes is called the head-related transfer function. A processing system can apply it to sound for headphones, so the sound seems to come from around you. Another method stores sound from every direction as one scene. It is called Ambisonics, and it can play on many speaker layouts.

The flashcards follow the same path, from the sea, through sonar and ultrasound, to sound around a listener. By the end, you will know how sound travels in the ocean, how machines use it to find things, and how it can be placed around your ears.
