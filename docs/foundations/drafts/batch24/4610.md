Topic Foundations: Sound Physics and Acoustic Quantities

Imagine two people measuring the noise of a busy road. One stands near the road for a few minutes. The other stands beside a house wall all night. Their meters show very different numbers, and both can be correct. So this topic asks one question: what must you know about a sound measurement before its number is useful?

First, what is measured? Air always has a steady pressure. Sound is a very small rise and fall of pressure on top of it. Small parts of the air move forward and back, then return. How fast sound travels depends on how hard the air or water is to squeeze and how heavy a fixed volume of it is.

Three measures describe this sound. Sound pressure is measured at one point. The energy that flows through an area each second, in one direction, is called intensity. Sound power is the energy a source sends out each second, and it does not change with distance. Each is usually given in decibels, a scale that compares it with a fixed agreed value. That value is called the reference quantity.

The position of the microphone also matters. Close to a source, the level changes a lot over a short distance. Farther away, it falls by a steady amount each time the distance doubles. A space where no surface reflects sound is called a free field. In a room with many echoes, sound arrives from all directions, and the level is almost the same everywhere.

On its way to the listener, sound also loses energy. It spreads over a larger area. The air absorbs high frequencies. Soft ground, trees and buildings absorb or scatter it, and wind can bend it. This drop in level is called attenuation. Inside a room, the time sound takes to fall by a large fixed amount after the source stops is the reverberation time.

The meter also uses settings. It divides the sound into ranges of frequency and averages over a stated time. Real noise rises and falls, so one average is not enough. The level that the noise stays above for a set share of the time is a percentile level. A large share describes the quiet background. Ratings often add extra decibels for night noise, clear tones or sharp bursts. All of this depends on math, such as probability and averages. Random noise is often described with a bell-shaped curve. More averaging usually gives a more stable result.

The flashcards explain each part: the wave in air and water, the main measures and their references, sound fields and test setups, losses outdoors and in buildings, noise over time, and the math behind it. By the end, you will know what a sound number measures, which conditions produced it, and what must be stated before two numbers can be compared.
