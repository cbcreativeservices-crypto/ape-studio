Topic Foundations: Sound Physics and Acoustic Quantities

Imagine two people measuring the noise of a busy road. One stands near the road for a few minutes. The other stands beside a house wall all night. Their meters show different numbers, and both can be correct. So what must you know about a sound measurement before its number is useful?

The first question is what the meter measures. Air always has a steady pressure. Sound is a very small rise and fall of pressure on top of it. Its speed depends on how hard the air or water is to squeeze and how heavy it is for its size.

Three measures describe it. Sound pressure is measured at one point. The energy that flows through an area each second, in one direction, is called intensity. Sound power is the energy a source sends out each second, and it does not change with distance. Each is usually given in decibels, which compare it with a fixed value called the reference quantity.

The position of the microphone also matters. Close to a source, the level changes quickly with distance. A space where no surface reflects sound is called a free field. There, farther from the source, the level falls by a steady amount each time the distance doubles. In a room with many echoes, sound arrives from all directions, and the level is almost the same in most places. When the source stops, the sound in a room dies away. The time it takes to fall by a large fixed amount is the reverberation time.

On its way to a listener, sound spreads over a larger area. The air absorbs high frequencies. Soft ground, trees and buildings absorb or scatter it, and wind can bend it. This drop in level is called attenuation. How well walls and floors reduce sound between rooms is called sound insulation.

The meter can reduce some frequencies to match human hearing, divide the sound into ranges of frequency, and average over a stated time. Real noise rises and falls, so one average is often not enough. The level that the noise stays above for a set percentage of the time is a percentile level. A large percentage describes the quiet background. Ratings often add extra decibels for night noise, single tones that stand out, or sudden bursts.

Because real noise is partly random, these averages depend on probability. Random noise is often described with a bell-shaped curve, and more averaging usually gives a more stable result. Probability is not the only mathematics here. Coordinates give each point in space its position, and equations describe how pressure changes from point to point and moment to moment.
