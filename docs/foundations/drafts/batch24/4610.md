Topic Foundations: Sound Physics and Acoustic Quantities

Imagine two people measuring road noise. One stands near the road for a few minutes. The other stands beside a house wall all night. Their meters show different numbers, and both can be correct. So what must you know about a sound measurement before its number is useful?

The first question is what the meter measures. Air always has a steady pressure. Sound is a very small rise and fall of pressure on top of it. Its speed depends on how hard the air or water is to squeeze and how heavy it is for its size.

Three measures describe it. Sound pressure is measured at one point. The energy that flows through an area each second, in one direction, is called intensity. Sound power is the energy a source sends out each second, and it does not change with distance. Many sources send more sound in some directions than others, which is called source directivity. Each is usually given in decibels. A decibel value compares a measurement with a fixed value, called the reference quantity.

The position of the microphone also matters. A space where no surface reflects sound is called a free field. There, away from the source, the level falls by a steady amount each time the distance doubles. In a room with many reflections, the level far from the source is about the same in most places. When the source stops, the room sound slowly becomes quieter. The time it takes to fall by a large, agreed amount is the reverberation time.

On its way to a listener, sound spreads out. The air absorbs high frequencies more than low ones. Ground, buildings and wind also change it. The total loss along the way is called attenuation. Walls and floors also reduce sound on its way between rooms. How well they do this is called sound insulation.

Before it gives a number, the meter can reduce some frequencies to match human hearing. It can also divide the sound into frequency bands and average over a stated time. Real noise changes, so one average is often not enough. The level that the noise stays above for a set percentage of the time is a percentile level. A large percentage describes the quiet background. Ratings often add decibels for night noise, single steady tones or sudden bursts.

Because noise is partly random, statistics are used to describe these averages. Random noise is often described with a bell-shaped curve, and more averaging usually gives a more stable result. Mathematics also describes where each microphone stands, using coordinates, and how pressure changes from point to point.

The flashcards explain each part, including how rooms are rated for listening and the mathematics behind the measures. By the end, you will know what a sound number measures, which conditions produced it, and what must be stated to compare two numbers.
