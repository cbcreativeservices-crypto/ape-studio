Topic Foundations: Sound Physics and Acoustic Quantities

Imagine two people measuring road noise. One stands near the road for a few minutes. The other stands beside a house wall all night. Their meters show different numbers, and both can be correct. So what must you know about a sound measurement before its number is useful?

The first question is what the number describes. Air always has a steady pressure. Sound is a very small rise and fall of pressure on top of it. Three main measures describe a sound. Sound pressure is this rise and fall at one point. The energy that flows through an area each second, in one direction, is called intensity. Sound power is the energy a source sends out each second, and it does not change with distance. Each measure is usually given as a level in decibels. A level compares the measurement with a fixed value, called the reference quantity.

The position of the microphone also matters. Many sources send more sound in some directions than others, which is called source directivity. A space with no reflections is called a free field. There, far from the source, in the far field, the level falls by a steady amount each time the distance doubles. Close to the source, in the near field, the level is harder to predict. So a report must state the distance.

Indoors, many reflections can change this. Away from the source, the level can be about the same in most places. When the source stops, the sound gradually becomes quieter. The time it takes to fall by a large, agreed amount is the reverberation time. How well walls and floors reduce sound between rooms is called sound insulation.

Outdoors, sound spreads out on its way to a listener. The air absorbs high frequencies more than low ones. Ground, buildings and wind also change it. This drop in level is called attenuation.

Before it gives a number, the meter can reduce some frequencies to match human hearing. It can also divide the sound into frequency bands and average over a stated time. Real noise changes, so one average is often not enough. The level the noise stays above for a set percentage of the time is a percentile level. Ratings over a full day often count night noise as louder, because it disturbs people more.

Because noise is partly random, these averages are described with statistics. Random values often form a bell-shaped curve, with most near the average and few far from it. Measurement also uses other mathematics. Coordinates record where each microphone is placed, equations describe how pressure changes from point to point, and powers of ten shorten very large numbers.

The flashcards explain each part, including how rooms are rated for listening. By the end, you will know what a sound number measures, which conditions produced it, and what must be stated to compare two numbers.
