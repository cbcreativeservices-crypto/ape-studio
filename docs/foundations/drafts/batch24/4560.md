Topic Foundations: Time and Frequency Displays and Spectrograms

Imagine opening a song in an audio editor. You see a long shape that is tall in the loud parts and thin in the quiet parts. That view of the whole file is called a waveform overview. These pictures show when a sound happens and how loud it is. But how can a picture show which frequencies a sound contains?

The answer is to cut the sound into short, overlapping pieces and measure the frequencies in each one. The pieces are then placed side by side. Time runs left to right, and frequency rises from bottom to top. The color at each point shows the level. This picture is called a spectrogram.

The length of each piece decides what the picture shows. A long piece measures frequency exactly but shows the timing less exactly. That view is called a narrowband spectrogram, and it shows the separate frequencies of a note as separate lines. A short piece measures timing exactly but shows the frequencies less exactly. That view is called a wideband spectrogram. No setting gives both, so you choose the view for your question.

So researchers developed other methods. Some use short measuring pieces for high frequencies and long pieces for low frequencies. Others move each measured part of the energy to a more exact time and frequency, so the lines become thinner. The most exact methods compare the signal with itself in a special calculation. This adds false energy halfway between true sounds in the picture. That false energy is called cross-term interference. Separating true sound from these false marks is part of reading any display.

A spectrogram measures all frequencies in the same way, but the ear does not. The ear groups high frequencies into wider bands, so some displays space their bands in that way. Such views are often used to study speech. On them, a line shows how the pitch of a voice rises and falls. Bands of energy at certain frequencies show which vowel is spoken, and a short burst shows a sound such as p or t.

Music has its own views. One adds together every copy of the same note, high and low, under its note name, so chords become visible. Another shows the speed of the beat.

Because a spectrogram shows each sound in its own place, it also helps to repair recordings. Choosing an area by both time and frequency is called spectral selection. The tool then reduces only that area, and other sounds at the same moment stay unchanged.
