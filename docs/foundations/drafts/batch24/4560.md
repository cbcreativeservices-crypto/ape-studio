Topic Foundations: Time and Frequency Displays and Spectrograms

Imagine opening a song in an audio editor. You see a long shape, tall in the loud parts and thin in the quiet parts. That view of the whole file is called a waveform overview. Other views show the wave's highest points or its average level. Some mark places where the sound was too loud and was cut flat. All of these show when a sound happens and how loud it is. But how can a picture show which frequencies a sound contains?

The answer is to cut the sound into short, overlapping pieces and measure each one's frequencies. Time runs left to right, and frequency rises from bottom to top. The color at each point shows the level. This picture is called a spectrogram.

The length of each piece changes what the picture shows. A long piece measures frequency more exactly but shows the timing less exactly. That view is called a narrowband spectrogram, and it shows each frequency of a note as its own line. A short piece measures timing more exactly but shows the frequencies less exactly. That view is called a wideband spectrogram.

Because one piece length cannot show both exactly, researchers developed other methods. Some use short pieces for high frequencies and long pieces for low frequencies. Others recalculate where each part of the energy really belongs in time and frequency, so the lines become thinner. A third group multiplies the signal by a shifted copy of itself. This gives sharp lines, but it also adds false energy halfway between true sounds. That false energy is called cross-term interference.

A standard spectrogram gives every frequency band the same width, but the ear does not. The ear groups high frequencies into wider bands, so some displays space their bands in that way.

Other displays are made for one kind of sound. Speech displays add a line for the rising and falling pitch of a voice. Bands of energy at certain frequencies show which vowel is spoken, and a short burst shows a consonant such as "p" or "t". Music and animal sounds also need their own views. One music display adds together the same note in every octave, so the notes of a chord become visible. Another shows the speed of the beat over time. Animal displays often use other frequency ranges, because some calls are too high or too low to hear.

Because different sounds often appear in different areas of the picture, a spectrogram also helps to repair recordings. Choosing an area by both time and frequency is called spectral selection. The tool then edits only that area, and other sounds at that moment usually stay unchanged.
