Topic Foundations: Archaeological and Heritage Acoustics

Imagine clapping your hands inside an old stone church. The sound continues for several seconds. People in the past heard these places too, but sound disappears as soon as it is made. So how can we know how the places and objects of the past sounded? The study that asks this question is called archaeoacoustics.

Many old places still stand, so researchers measure them. They make a short, loud test sound, and record every echo and how slowly the sound fades. They repeat this at many planned points, because sound changes across a space. This is called an archaeoacoustic survey.

Surveys show that each space changes sound in its own way. Rock walls in a large cave reflect sound many times, so it fades slowly. Closed stone tombs often become much louder at certain low pitches. The rising seats of an open theater help a voice reach many listeners. In a stone church, sung notes overlap, which suits chant, a slow and simple kind of singing. Some researchers suggest that people chose places for these effects. These ideas are debated and not proven.

Many other spaces are ruined or gone. Then researchers build a computer model from plans and remains. The model predicts the echoes, and a recording of a voice made with no echoes is combined with them, so you can hear the lost space. Maps of hills and distance show how far a horn could be heard outdoors. That area is called an audibility zone. Every model includes guesses, so good projects record which parts come from evidence.

The sounds themselves came from objects. Instruments are sorted by the part that vibrates: the air, a string, a stretched skin, or the object's own body. Strings, wood and skin usually decay, so most finds are bone, clay, shell, metal or stone. A large natural rock that people strike to make it ring is called a rock gong. But a hole in a bone does not prove that someone made a flute, because animal teeth make similar holes. So researchers compare the marks on an object with marks on copies used in known ways. This is called use-wear analysis. They also play working copies, so the fragile original stays safe.

Sound is now also treated as heritage. Repairs or a new use can change how a building sounds, so the people who protect it measure the sound and manage those changes. Living traditions, such as bell ringing, are taught so they continue. The sound of places and traditions, treated as worth saving, is called acoustic heritage.
