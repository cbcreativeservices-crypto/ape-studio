Topic Foundations: Astronomical and Cosmological Acoustics

Sound needs matter to travel through, such as a gas, a liquid or a solid. But the space between the stars is almost empty, so no sound reaches us across it. Stars, however, are full of hot gas, and so was the young universe. Can sound waves inside them tell us about places we can never visit?

They can. Near the surface of stars like the Sun, the hot gas is always rising and sinking, and this motion produces sound waves. Deeper inside, the gas is hotter and sound moves faster, so each inward wave bends back toward the surface. There it reflects down again. The trapped waves form steady patterns, each with its own frequency. A trapped sound wave of this kind is called a p-mode, because pressure pushes the gas back.

The Sun is close enough for us to measure them in detail. They make its surface rise and fall, most strongly about every five minutes. Studying the inside of the Sun from these waves is called helioseismology. The exact frequencies show how fast sound travels at each depth, and how fast each layer rotates.

Other stars are too far away for us to see their surfaces move. But the same waves make their brightness rise and fall a little. Learning about the inside of stars this way is called asteroseismology. The spacing between a star's frequencies shows its average density, and so helps us calculate its size and mass. Smaller differences in that spacing come from the core and help show the star's age.

The young universe had sound waves too. Before atoms formed, light and matter moved together as one hot fluid. Gravity pulled matter into denser regions, and the pressure of the light pushed it apart again. When the universe cooled and atoms formed, the sound waves stopped, and their pattern stayed fixed. The oldest light we can see still shows it as tiny hot and cold patches.

Before they stopped, those waves had traveled a certain distance. That distance is called the sound horizon, and scientists can calculate it. Because of it, pairs of galaxies today are found slightly more often at that distance apart. Baryons are the ordinary matter that atoms are made of. This pattern is called baryon acoustic oscillations. How large this known distance looks in the sky, in older and newer galaxies, shows how fast space expands.
