Topic Foundations: Chemical and Molecular Acoustics

Imagine a small tank of water that cleans jewelry. You switch it on, and the dirt is removed from the metal. The tank sends intense sound at a pitch too high to hear. This is called high-power ultrasound. Sound only pushes and pulls on the water. So how can sound clean metal, change chemicals, and also measure what materials are made of?

The answer begins with the pulling. When intense sound pulls on the water, the pressure drops. A tiny pocket of trapped gas opens and grows. That pocket is called a cavitation bubble. Gentle sound makes it grow and shrink again and again. More intense sound makes it grow large and then collapse very fast.

During that collapse, the water squeezes the gas so quickly that its heat cannot escape. A tiny spot inside the bubble becomes extremely hot. This heat splits water molecules into very reactive pieces. Using sound to cause chemical reactions is called sonochemistry. These reactions can break down pollutants or form tiny metal grains.

A bubble that collapses next to a surface also shoots a thin, fast jet of liquid at it. These jets help remove the dirt from the jewelry. Treating a sample with ultrasound can also break open living cells and mix liquids that normally stay separate. Sound too weak to collapse bubbles can instead create a fixed pattern of high and low pressure in the liquid. Small particles then collect at fixed places in that pattern.

Very gentle sound usually leaves a liquid unchanged, so it can measure it instead. The speed of sound shows how tightly the molecules are packed. How much sound energy the liquid absorbs shows how its molecules move. Some processes inside molecules, such as changing shape, take time. When the sound squeezes the liquid too fast, these processes respond late and absorb extra energy. That delay is called acoustic relaxation.

The same idea, measuring with gentle sound, also works on a solid surface. A sensor sends a sound wave through or along a thin crystal. When material sticks to the crystal, the wave slows and its frequency drops. That change shows the added mass. A vibrating quartz disk used this way is called a quartz crystal microbalance. Other sensors send the wave along the crystal's top surface, where a coating holds only the chosen molecules.

So far, the sound came from outside the sample. It can also start inside it, when the sample absorbs light. If the light is quickly switched on and off, the sample warms and cools. So it expands, contracts and sends out a faint sound. This is called the photoacoustic effect.
