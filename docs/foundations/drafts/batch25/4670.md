Topic Foundations: Chemical and Molecular Acoustics

Imagine a small tank of water that cleans jewelry. You switch it on, and the dirt is removed from the metal. The tank sends very intense sound at a pitch too high to hear. This is called high-power ultrasound. Sound only pushes and pulls on the water. So how can it clean and even change chemicals? And how can gentler sound give information about molecules?

The answer begins with the pulling. When very intense sound pulls the water apart, the pressure drops. A tiny pocket of trapped gas opens and grows. That pocket is called a cavitation bubble. Gentle sound makes it grow and shrink again and again for a long time. More intense sound makes it grow large and then collapse very fast.

During that collapse, the water squeezes the gas so quickly that its heat cannot escape. For a moment, a tiny spot inside the bubble becomes extremely hot. This heat splits water molecules into very reactive pieces. Using sound to cause chemical reactions in this way is called sonochemistry. These reactions can break down pollutants or form tiny metal grains.

A bubble that collapses next to a surface also shoots a thin, fast jet of liquid at it. These jets can clean or break things. Treating a sample with ultrasound is called sonication. It can clean parts, break open living cells, break up clumps of powder, and mix liquids that normally stay separate. Weaker sound that does not collapse bubbles can instead hold small particles at fixed places in the sound wave.

Gentle sound can also measure a liquid without changing it. The speed of sound shows how tightly the molecules are packed. How much sound energy the liquid absorbs shows even more. Some processes inside molecules, such as changing shape, take time. When the sound squeezes the liquid too fast, these processes respond late and absorb extra energy. That delay is called acoustic relaxation. The frequency where the absorption is greatest shows how fast each process is.

Sound can also measure tiny amounts of material on a solid crystal. A thin quartz crystal vibrates at a very exact frequency. When material sticks to its surface, the crystal vibrates at a slightly lower frequency, and the change shows the added mass. This device is called a quartz crystal microbalance.

Light can also make sound. A sample that absorbs light that is quickly switched on and off warms and cools, so it expands, contracts and sends out a faint sound. This is called the photoacoustic effect. Light reflected from tiny sound waves inside a material also shows how stiff it is.

By the end, you will know how very intense sound changes materials through collapsing bubbles, and how gentle sound measures what materials are made of.
