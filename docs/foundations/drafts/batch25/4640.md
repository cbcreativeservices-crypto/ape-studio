Topic Foundations: Bioacoustics and Ecoacoustics

Imagine standing near a forest just before sunrise. Many birds sing at the same time. Insects buzz, frogs call from a pond, and wind moves the leaves. The study of how animals make, send and hear sound is called bioacoustics. This topic asks what sound can tell us about animals and the places where they live.

The answer starts with how animals make sound. A bird sings with an organ in its chest. Many insects rub one body part against another, and many fish make a drumming sound with a very fast muscle. Some animals send vibration through a plant stem or the ground instead of the air. Hearing differs too. An insect may hear with a thin patch of its hard outer skin. A fish has ears, and it also feels moving water along its sides.

Each sound has a purpose. A male frog or bird calls again and again to attract a female and to keep other males away. Other calls keep a group together, ask parents for food, or warn others of a predator.

Some animals also use sound to find food. Bats and toothed whales send out short calls and listen to the echoes that return. The echoes show where an insect or a fish is. This is called echolocation. As the animal comes closer to its prey, its calls become faster and shorter.

In one place, many calls can overlap. Often each species uses its own range of pitches or its own time of day. Human noise, such as traffic or ships, can hide calls. When one sound hides another, it is called acoustic masking.

Because so many sounds share one place, researchers also study the sound of the whole place. That study is called ecoacoustics. It sorts that sound into three parts: animals, natural sounds such as wind and water, and people with their machines.

A whole place is too large to hear at once, so researchers record it and then measure the recording. Recording what is already there, without making any sound, is called passive acoustic monitoring. Recorders can stay in place for weeks. A number calculated from such a recording is called an acoustic index. One index measures how many different sounds there are. Another compares animal sounds with machine sounds. A rich mix of animal sounds often means a healthy habitat. Software also marks possible calls, but it can report an animal that is not there, or miss one that is. So people check the results.
