Topic Foundations: Geologic and Seismoacoustics

Imagine a heavy truck passing your house. You hear it, and you also feel the floor shake. Rock, ice and sea water all carry waves from earthquakes, volcanoes and glaciers. Many are too low in pitch for people to hear. So what can scientists learn about the Earth by measuring them?

The answer starts with how waves move through rock. The fastest wave arrives first. It pushes and pulls the rock along its path. It is called the P-wave, P for primary. A slower wave shakes the rock across its path. It is called the S-wave, S for secondary, and it cannot pass through liquid. Other waves travel along the surface. The speed of each wave depends on the rock, so arrival times at known distances show what the rock is like.

Earthquakes are the best-known source of these waves. They happen when rock slips along a fault, a break between two blocks of rock. Rock can also crack on a tiny scale before it breaks. Each new crack sends out a short pulse of sound. A rising number of pulses can warn that rock in a mine, or ice in a glacier, is close to breaking.

Volcanoes add their own signals. Magma (melted rock), gas and hot water move through cracks under the volcano. The steady shaking this causes is called volcanic tremor, and it often grows before an eruption. Gas bursts and explosions also make low sound in the air.

Those low sounds can travel very far. Layers of air bend them back toward the ground, and the sea carries sound across whole oceans. Ocean waves also make a faint, steady shaking of the ground, called a microseism. To find a source, scientists spread sensors over the ground. The wave reaches each one at a different moment, and the delays show its direction.

Scientists also make their own sound to look underground. In a survey, a machine sends a pulse of sound into the ground or the sea. At sea, a ship tows a long cable full of underwater microphones. Each boundary between rock layers sends back an echo. The time of an echo does not show its depth directly. So computers use the speed of sound in the rock to place each echo at its true depth and build an image of the layers. Inside a drilled hole, a tool times sound through the rock beside it. That record is called a sonic log.

The same waves also help engineers judge the ground under a building site. A line of sensors records how fast waves travel through the top layers. Soft ground carries them more slowly, and it often shakes more in an earthquake.
