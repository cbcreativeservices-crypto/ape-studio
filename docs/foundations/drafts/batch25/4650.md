Topic Foundations: Geologic and Seismoacoustics

Imagine a heavy truck passing your house on a quiet street. You hear it, and you also feel the floor shake. Rock, ice and sea water all carry waves from earthquakes, volcanoes and glaciers. Many are too low in pitch for people to hear. So what can scientists learn about the Earth by measuring them?

The answer starts with how waves move through rock. The fastest wave pushes and pulls the rock along its path, so it arrives first. It is called the P-wave, P for primary. A slower wave shakes the rock across its path, from side to side or up and down. It is called the S-wave, S for secondary, and it cannot pass through liquid. Other waves travel along the surface. The speed of each wave depends on how stiff and dense the rock is. So the time each wave takes to arrive gives information about the rock.

Earthquakes are the best-known source of these waves. They happen when rock slips along a fault, a break between two blocks of rock. Their size depends on how large an area of the fault slipped, and how far. Rock also makes tiny sounds before it breaks, a short pulse from each new crack. A rising number of pulses can warn that rock in a mine, or ice in a glacier, is close to breaking. Even with no earthquake, distant ocean waves keep the ground shaking gently. That faint, steady shaking is called a microseism.

Volcanoes add their own signals. Magma (melted rock), gas and hot water move through cracks under the volcano. The steady shaking this causes, often for hours, is called volcanic tremor, and it often grows before an eruption. Gas bursts and explosions also make low sound in the air.

Those low sounds can travel very far. Layers of wind and temperature bend them back toward the ground, and the deep sea carries sound across whole oceans. To find the source, scientists spread sensors over the ground, and the wave reaches each one at a slightly different moment. From those delays, they find the compass direction back to the source, called the backazimuth.

Scientists also make their own sound to look underground. In a survey, a machine sends a short pulse of sound into the ground or the sea. Sensors record echoes from the boundaries between rock layers. Computers combine them into an image of the layers. Inside a deep hole drilled into the ground, a tool times sound through the rock beside it. That record is called a sonic log. Slow travel usually means more open space in the rock.
