Topic Foundations: Surveillance, Forensic and Covert Audio

Imagine a phone recording of an argument, played as evidence in court. Was anything removed? What was said, and who said it? Was a hidden device used? This topic asks how a recording is checked as evidence, and how sound is captured in secret.

Every answer starts with the file. The first file the device made is called the original recording. Examiners keep it unchanged and work on a copy. They look for signs of editing, or of being saved again. In an untouched recording, background noise usually changes only slowly. The frequency of the electricity supply changes slightly all the time. When a recording contains hum from the electricity supply, the hum often keeps that pattern. This changing frequency is called the electric network frequency. It can show when a recording was made, or where it was cut.

Next comes what a genuine recording says. Filters can reduce noise to make a voice clearer, but must not add sound or change words. A trained listener writes down the words and marks unclear parts. A listener who reads a suggested version first tends to hear those words, even when the sound does not support them.

Then, who is speaking? Experts measure features such as pitch and vowel sounds, and ask how common they are in a group of similar speakers, called the reference population. The result says how much more likely these features are if the known person spoke than if someone else did. It does not, on its own, name a person.

A recording can also hold evidence that is not a voice. A gunshot makes a loud bang called the muzzle blast. Microphones record it at slightly different moments, and those differences show where it came from.

The recording in the court may itself have been made in secret. A hidden listening device is called a bug. It usually contains a small microphone and either a recorder or a radio transmitter. Secretly capturing phone calls is called a wiretap. A spike microphone pushed into a wall detects the wall's vibrations from the next room. A laser can detect the vibrations of a window. The sound may be stored, or sent live to a listening post. Laws on secret recording differ by country. Police usually need a court order, and in many places a private person may record only a conversation they are part of.

A search of a room for hidden devices is called a bug sweep. Radio detectors find devices that send signals. A device that only records sends no signal, so searchers also look by eye and by hand.

The flashcards cover each step, from the first file to the room search, and other ways sound can leak secrets. By the end, you will know how a recording is checked as evidence, how sound is captured in secret, and how hidden devices are found.
