Topic Foundations: Surveillance, Forensic and Covert Audio

Imagine a phone recording of an argument played in a court. Was anything removed? What was said, and who said it? Was a hidden device used? This topic asks how a recording is checked and understood as evidence, and how sound is captured in secret.

Every answer starts with the file. The first file the device made is called the original recording. Examiners keep it unchanged and work on an exact copy. Then they look for signs of editing, and for signs that the file was saved again in another format. In an untouched recording, the quiet background sound usually changes only slowly, not suddenly. The frequency of the electricity supply changes slightly all the time. Any hum from the supply in a recording keeps that pattern. This changing frequency is called the electric network frequency. It can show when a recording was made, or where it was cut.

When a recording is accepted as genuine, the next question is what it says. Filters can reduce noise so a voice is easier to hear, but they must not add sound or change words. A trained listener writes down the words and marks unclear parts as unclear. A listener who reads a suggested version first tends to hear those words, even when the sound does not support them.

Even with clear words, one question remains: who is speaking? No person says anything the same way twice. So experts measure features such as pitch and vowel sounds, and ask how common they are in a group of similar speakers, called the reference population. The result compares how likely these features are if one known person spoke, and if someone else spoke. It does not, on its own, name a person.

A recording can also hold evidence that is not a voice. A gunshot usually contains a loud bang from the gun, called the muzzle blast. Several microphones hear it at slightly different moments, and those time differences show where it came from.

Some recordings are made in secret. A hidden listening device is called a bug. It usually contains a small microphone and either a recorder or a radio transmitter. Other methods use a phone line, a sensor on a wall, or a laser aimed at a window. Many countries have strict laws about this.

To protect private meetings, specialists search rooms for hidden devices. This search is called a bug sweep. Radio detectors find devices that send signals. A device that only records sends no signal, so the team also searches the room with their eyes and hands.

The flashcards explain each of these steps, from handling the first file to searching a room. By the end, you will know how a recording is checked as evidence, how sound is captured in secret, and how hidden devices are found.
