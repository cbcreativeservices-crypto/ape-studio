Topic Foundations: Surveillance, Forensic and Covert Audio

Imagine a phone recording of an argument. It is played as evidence in a court. Was anything removed? What was said, and who said it? Was a hidden device used? This topic asks how a recording is checked and understood as evidence, and how sound is captured in secret.

Every answer starts with the file. The first file the device made is called the original recording. Examiners keep it unchanged and work on a copy. They look for signs of editing, or of the file being saved again in another format.

In an untouched recording, the quiet background sound usually changes only slowly. The frequency of the electricity supply changes slightly all the time. Supply hum in a recording keeps that pattern. This changing frequency is called the electric network frequency. It can show when a recording was made, or where it was cut.

Next comes what a genuine recording says. Filters can reduce noise so a voice is easier to hear, but they must not add sound or change words. A trained listener writes down the words and marks unclear parts. A listener who reads a suggested version first tends to hear those words, even when the sound does not support them.

Then, who is speaking? Experts measure features such as pitch and vowel sounds, and ask how common they are in a group of similar speakers, called the reference population. The result compares how likely these features are if one known person spoke, or someone else. It does not, on its own, name a person. A recording can also hold evidence that is not a voice. A gunshot makes a loud bang called the muzzle blast. Microphones hear it at slightly different moments, and those differences show where it came from.

The recording in the court may itself have been made in secret. A hidden listening device is called a bug. It usually contains a small microphone and either a recorder or a radio transmitter. Capturing what is said on a phone line is called a wiretap. A spike microphone pushed into a wall picks up the wall's vibrations from the next room. A laser can pick up the vibrations of a window. The sound may be stored, or sent to a listening post where someone hears it live. Many countries allow secret recording only with a court order, or when at least one person in the conversation agrees.

Specialists search rooms for hidden devices. This search is called a bug sweep. Radio detectors find devices that send signals. A device that only records sends no signal, so the team also searches by eye and by hand.

The flashcards explain each of these steps, from handling the first file to searching a room. By the end, you will know how a recording is checked as evidence, how sound is captured in secret, and how hidden devices are found.
