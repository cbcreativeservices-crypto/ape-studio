Topic Foundations: Medical, Hearing and Clinical Acoustics

Imagine a school hearing check. You wear headphones and raise your hand at each soft beep. In a hospital, a doctor moves a small probe over the skin, and a picture of the inside of the body appears. This topic asks how clinics use sound to measure hearing, and to examine and treat the body.

The hearing check uses an audiometer, a machine that plays exact, quiet tones. The tester finds the softest tone the person hears at each pitch. A second test sends vibration through the bone behind the ear. If the bone result is much better, something blocks sound before it reaches the inner ear. That difference is called the air-bone gap. Speech tests in noise show how well words are understood.

The next question is where sound is blocked. Often it is in the middle ear, behind the eardrum. A soft probe seals the ear canal, plays a low tone, and changes the air pressure. It measures how easily sound enters. This test is called tympanometry. The same probe can detect a tiny muscle reflex to loud sound. This reflex test does not measure hearing itself.

If the middle ear works but hearing is poor, tests check the inner ear and nerve. A healthy inner ear sends faint sounds out through the ear canal, and a tiny microphone records them. Sensors on the head can also record how the nerve and brain respond to a repeated sound. This is called an auditory evoked potential. The inner ear also holds the balance organs. Loud sound can trigger them too, and sensors on the neck or near the eye record a small muscle response.

These results are used to set up a hearing aid or, for some people, an implant in the inner ear. Every ear canal has its own shape, so a thin tube is placed near the eardrum to record what the ear receives. This check is called real-ear measurement.

Sound also helps clinics examine the rest of the body. Doctors listen to the heart and breathing. A scanner sends pulses of ultrasound, sound far too high to hear, into the body, and the echoes build a live picture. Echoes from moving blood change pitch, which shows the flow. Sound can heat tissue, so scanners show safety numbers. Focused on one point and made much stronger, sound can also treat some problems, such as breaking up kidney stones.

All this equipment makes sound of its own. Machines beep all day, and most beeps need no action, so staff can gradually stop reacting quickly. This is called alarm fatigue, and it can put patients in danger.

The flashcards explain each of these tests and tools, from the hearing check to sound in the hospital. By the end, you will know what each test describes and how sound helps clinics see inside the body and treat it.
