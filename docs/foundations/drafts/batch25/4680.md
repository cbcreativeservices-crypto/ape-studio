Topic Foundations: Medical, Hearing and Clinical Acoustics

Imagine a school hearing check. You wear headphones and raise your hand at each soft beep. In a hospital, a doctor moves a small probe over the skin, and a picture of the inside of the body appears. This topic asks how clinics use sound to measure hearing, and to examine and treat the body.

The hearing check uses a machine called an audiometer, which plays exact, quiet tones. The tester finds the softest tone the person hears at each pitch. A second test sends vibration through the bone behind the ear. If the bone result is much better, something blocks sound before it reaches the inner ear. That difference is called the air-bone gap. Speech tests in noise show how well words are understood.

The next question is where sound is blocked. Often it is in the middle ear, behind the eardrum. A soft probe seals the ear canal, plays a low tone, and changes the air pressure. It measures how easily sound enters. This test is called tympanometry. A result with no peak often means fluid behind the eardrum. The same probe can detect a tiny muscle reflex to loud sound. It does not measure hearing itself.

If the middle ear works but hearing is poor, tests check the inner ear and nerve. A healthy inner ear sends faint sounds out through the ear canal, and a tiny microphone records them. Sensors on the head can also record how the nerve and brain respond to a repeated sound. This is called an auditory evoked potential.

These results are used to set up a hearing aid or, for some people, an implant in the inner ear, using a formula to calculate the settings. Every ear canal has its own shape, so a thin tube is placed near the eardrum to record what the ear receives. This check is called real-ear measurement.

Scanners also send pulses of ultrasound, sound far too high to hear, into the body, and the echoes build a live picture. Echoes from moving blood change pitch slightly, which shows the flow. Because sound can heat tissue or form tiny bubbles, scanners show safety numbers. When sound is focused on one point, it can also treat the body, such as breaking kidney stones. Doctors also listen to sounds inside the body, such as the heart and breathing.

Sound in hospitals also affects staff. Machines beep all day, and most beeps need no action, so staff can gradually stop reacting quickly. This is called alarm fatigue, and it can put patients in danger.

The flashcards cover tone and speech tests, middle ear tests, inner ear and nerve responses, hearing aids and implants, ultrasound pictures and treatment, and hospital sound. By the end, you will know what each test describes and how sound helps clinics see inside the body and treat it.
