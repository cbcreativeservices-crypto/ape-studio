Topic Foundations: Machine Learning for Speech and Voice

Imagine you say a short phrase to your phone, and it switches on its speech recognition. You ask a question, and a computer voice reads the answer aloud. These programs learned from many recorded examples, not from fixed rules that people wrote. This topic answers one question. How does a machine turn speech into words, words into speech, and a voice into the name of a person?

First, the machine must know when someone speaks. A small program separates speech from silence and background noise. That is called voice activity detection. A similar program waits for one fixed phrase before it switches on the main system. Other programs remove noise and echo, and judge when you have finished speaking.

Then the speech must become words. Older recognizers use several parts. One part decides which speech sounds are present in each short moment. It is called the acoustic model. A pronunciation dictionary links those sounds to words. A language model checks which word sequences make sense. A search chooses the most likely sentence.

Newer recognizers often use one network that learns the whole path from sound to text. Some first learn from recordings without written text. Others learn from huge amounts of speech in many languages and accents. But these large ones can sometimes write words that nobody said, especially during silence. Other parts then decide what the person wants and which person spoke at each moment.

To compare recognizers, engineers count mistakes. The number of wrong, missing and extra words, compared with the words spoken, is called the word error rate. During training, they often add noise or room echo to recordings, so the system works in real rooms.

Turning text into speech is the opposite job. The software rewrites dates and symbols as spoken words, then turns letters into speech sounds. Older systems joined small pieces of real recordings. Newer systems predict details such as pitch and timing, and a second part builds the sound wave. A related method changes one person's voice into another person's voice.

Because voices can be copied, checking who is speaking matters more. A network turns a recording into a short list of numbers that represents the voice. Comparing this list with the list made when the person first registered is called speaker verification. It can accept the wrong person or reject the right person. A stricter check reduces one error but increases the other. Because a copied voice can still pass, another check, called voice spoofing detection, looks for signs of a recording or a copied voice.

The flashcards explain each part. They cover how devices detect speech, older and newer recognizers, measurement, computer voices, and speaker checks. By the end, you will know how a machine detects speech, writes it as text, speaks and recognizes the human voice, and where each step can make mistakes.
