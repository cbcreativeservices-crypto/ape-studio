Topic Foundations: Machine Learning for Speech and Voice

Imagine you call a help line, and a computer voice answers. You say why you are calling, and the system writes your words as text. It reads an answer aloud, and it may even check that the voice is really yours. These programs learned mostly from many recorded examples, not only from fixed rules that people wrote. So how does a machine turn speech into words and words into speech, and how does it check who is speaking?

First, the machine must know when someone speaks. A small program separates speech from silence and background noise. That is called voice activity detection. A similar program waits for one fixed phrase before it starts the main system. Other programs first remove noise and echo, so the speech is easier to recognize.

Then the speech must become words. Older recognizers use several parts. One part decides which speech sounds are present in each short moment. It is called the acoustic model. Other parts link those sounds to words and choose the most likely word order. Newer recognizers often use one large program, called a neural network, that learns from examples to go directly from sound to text. But large recognizers can sometimes write words that nobody said, especially during silence.

Once the words are written, the system uses them to find what the person wants. That only works if the words are correct, so engineers count the mistakes. The number of wrong, missing and extra words, compared with the words spoken, is called the word error rate. To train and compare these systems, researchers share large collections of recorded and transcribed speech.

Turning text into speech is the opposite job. The software rewrites dates and symbols as spoken words, then turns letters into speech sounds. Many older systems joined small pieces of real recordings. Many newer systems predict details such as pitch and timing, and then a second part builds the sound wave. A related method changes one person's voice into another's.

A related program marks which person spoke at each moment in a recording with several voices. Checking one person's identity is harder once machines can copy voices. A neural network turns a recording into a short list of numbers that represents the voice. Comparing this list with the list made when the person first registered is called speaker verification. It can accept the wrong person or reject the right person. Making the check stricter reduces the first error but increases the second. A copied voice can still pass, so another check, called voice spoofing detection, looks for signs of a recording or a computer-made voice.

The flashcards cover each step, from detecting speech and writing it as text to computer voices, speaker checks and the shared recordings used to test them. By the end, you will know how each step works and where each step can make mistakes.
