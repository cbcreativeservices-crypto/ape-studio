Topic Foundations: AI Music, Voice and Creative Tools

Imagine you are making a video about your grandmother. Her old recording has a fan noise behind her voice. Her favorite song exists only as one finished file. Many programs can now help, using artificial intelligence (AI), which here means software that has learned patterns from a very large number of recordings. So this topic asks what these tools can make, separate, repair and change in sound, and where a person must still decide.

The first job is often repair. A tool that lowers the fan noise and keeps her voice clear is called noise suppression. Similar tools reduce room echo, rebuild sound whose loudest parts were cut off during recording, or restore lost high frequencies. But each tool makes a guess. A wrong guess can add unnatural new sounds, so settings that change the sound less are often safer.

Repair removes sound you do not want. The next step is separating sounds you do want. Her favorite song combines the voice, drums and bass in one file. A tool that separates these parts into their own tracks is called source separation. It keeps the parts of the sound that usually belong to each instrument and turns the rest down. Small parts of other instruments often remain.

The parts must then be balanced. Some tools analyze a mix and suggest settings for tone and loudness. When software prepares the final tone and loudness of a finished mix for you to adjust, that is called assistive mastering. Other tools make a recorded guitar sound as if it was played through a real amplifier. In each case, the software offers a starting point, and a person decides whether it improves the music.

The same learning also works on the human voice. Software can read text aloud in a natural voice. It can also learn how one person sounds from a few recordings and say new words in that voice. That is called voice cloning. Other tools change the voice, the accent or the language, and some turn speech into written text. Because a cloned voice can say things a person never said, using it without permission can harm that person.

So far, every tool has started from a recording. The last step is making sound that was never recorded. You type a short description, and the software creates music that matches it. This is called text-to-music, and similar tools make sound effects from words. Some programs even play music together with a live musician. Whether the result fits the project is still a human decision.

The flashcards explain each kind of tool in more detail, from repair and separation to mixing assistants, voice and speech tools, and new music and sound. By the end, you will know what each tool does, what it can and cannot fix, and why a person's judgment still decides the final sound.
