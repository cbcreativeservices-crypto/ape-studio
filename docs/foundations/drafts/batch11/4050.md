Topic Foundations: Sampling and Virtual Instruments

Imagine you record a friend playing one guitar note on your phone. Now imagine using that recording to play a whole melody. An instrument that plays recordings from keys or pads is called a sampler. So how does a short recording become a playable instrument?

First, silence at the start is removed, and a short fade at each end prevents a click. The sampler plays the recording faster for higher keys and slower for lower keys. The key where it plays at its original pitch is called the root key. For a held note, a steady middle part of the sound repeats. That part is called a loop. Its ends are usually placed where the drawn wave of the sound crosses its center line, so the join makes no click.

But a recording played far from its root key no longer matches the real instrument, because its tone and length change too. So instrument makers record many notes, each with its own group of keys.

A real instrument also changes its tone when played harder, not only its volume. So each note is recorded at several strengths, and how hard you press chooses the recording. Each one is called a velocity layer. A real player rarely plays a quickly repeated note the same way twice. So the note is often recorded several times, and the recordings play in turn. That method is called round robin. For the same reason, makers record each playing style, such as short or connected notes, and the sound a note makes as it ends.

Drums use the same ideas. A drum hit usually plays once, to its end. A closed hi-hat (a pair of cymbals pressed together) can stop an open one that is still ringing. Drum sounds played together as one hit usually need their waves lined up in time. Otherwise they can partly cancel each other, and some frequencies become quieter.

Such a sampler can be a hardware unit or a program, which often runs inside recording software. Each can play only a limited number of notes at once. Finished instruments are often sold as libraries. Their file formats store the recordings with the rules for playing them. A large library can be too big for the computer's memory. So only the start of each recording is loaded, and the rest is read from the drive as it plays.

A library is built from recordings that its maker has the right to use. Using part of someone else's recording usually needs permission from the owner of the recording and the owner of the song. Getting that permission is called sample clearance.

The flashcards explain each step in detail, along with how loops are cut into hits and the history of sampling. By the end, you will know how one recording becomes a playable instrument, and what makes it sound real.
