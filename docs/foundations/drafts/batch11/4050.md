Topic Foundations: Sampling and Virtual Instruments

Imagine you record one note of a friend's guitar on your phone. Now imagine pressing any key on a keyboard and hearing that guitar play the note you chose. An instrument that plays recordings from keys or pads is called a sampler. So how does a short recording become a playable instrument?

First, silence at the start of the recording is removed, and a short fade at each end prevents a click. The sampler plays the recording faster for higher keys and slower for lower keys. The key where it plays at its original pitch is called the root key. For a held note, a steady middle part of the sound repeats. That part is called a loop. Its ends are usually placed where the wave crosses the center line, so the join makes no click.

But one recording sounds unnatural far above or below its root key. So makers record many notes, each with its own group of keys. A real instrument also changes its tone when played harder, not only its volume. So each note is recorded at several strengths, and how hard you press chooses the recording. Each one is called a velocity layer. A note that repeats quickly would sound exactly the same every time, which a real player never does. So it is often recorded several times, and the recordings play in turn. That method is called round robin.

Drums use the same ideas. A drum hit usually plays once, all the way to its end. A closed hi-hat (a pair of cymbals pressed together) can stop an open one that is still ringing, as on a real drum kit. Producers often play several drum sounds together as one hit. Their waves must be lined up in time, or they partly cancel each other and the hit sounds thinner.

The first samplers, before digital ones, played a strip of recorded tape for each key. Today all of this runs inside an instrument, either a physical box or a program in recording software. Each can play only a limited number of notes at once. Large instruments keep only the start of each sound in memory and read the rest from the drive.

Finished instruments are sold as libraries, in file formats that store the recordings with the rules for playing them. Using part of someone else's recording usually needs permission from the owner of the recording and the owner of the song. Getting that permission is called sample clearance.

The flashcards explain each step, from preparing and looping recordings to spreading them across keys and strengths. They also cover drum sounds, playing styles, slicing, memory, the history of sampling, and how libraries are stored, organized and used legally. By the end, you will know how one recording becomes a playable instrument, and what makes it sound real.
