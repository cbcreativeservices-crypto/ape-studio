Topic Foundations: Sampling and Virtual Instruments

Imagine you record a friend playing one guitar note on your phone. Now imagine using that one recording to play a whole melody, with high and low notes. An instrument that plays recordings from keys or pads is called a sampler. So how does a short recording become a playable instrument?

First, silence at the start is removed, and a short fade at each end prevents a click. The sampler plays the recording faster for higher keys and slower for lower keys. The key where it plays at its original pitch is called the root key. For a held note, a steady middle part of the sound repeats. That part is called a loop. Its ends are usually placed where the drawn wave of the sound crosses its center line, so the join makes no click.

But a recording played far from its root key no longer matches the real instrument, because its tone and length change too. So the makers of sampled instruments record many notes, each with its own group of keys.

A real instrument also changes its tone when played harder, not only its volume. So each note is recorded at several strengths, and how hard you press chooses the recording. Each one is called a velocity layer. A real player rarely plays a quickly repeated note the same way twice. So the note is often recorded several times, and the recordings play in turn. That method is called round robin. Makers also record each playing style, such as short or connected notes, and the sound a note makes as it ends.

Drums use the same ideas. A drum hit usually plays once, to its end. A recorded drum loop can also be cut at each hit, so each hit plays from its own key. A closed hi-hat (a pair of cymbals pressed together) can stop an open one that is still ringing. Producers often play several drum sounds together as one hit. Their waves usually need to be lined up in time, or they can partly cancel each other and some frequencies become quieter.

All of this runs inside an instrument, either a hardware unit or a program in recording software. Each can play only a limited number of notes at once. Finished instruments are often sold as libraries, in file formats that store the recordings with the rules for playing them.

A library is built from recordings that its maker has the right to use. Using part of someone else's recording usually needs permission from the owner of the recording and the owner of the song. Getting that permission is called sample clearance.

The flashcards explain each of these steps in detail, along with the history of sampling, and how libraries are stored and used legally. By the end, you will know how one recording becomes a playable instrument, and what makes it sound real.
