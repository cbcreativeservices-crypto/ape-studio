Topic Foundations: Sample Editing and Beat Programming

Imagine tapping a rhythm on a table. Some taps are loud, some are soft, and some come early or late. A machine can repeat a rhythm perfectly, but it plays only what it is told. So this topic asks: how is a drum beat built by hand from short sounds, and how does it keep the small timing changes of a real player?

The work usually starts with one bar of music (a short group of beats) divided into equal slots. Each slot plays a drum sound or stays silent. A tool that works this way is called a step sequencer. Each step can also store how hard the sound is hit, how long it lasts, several fast repeats, or a chance that it plays each time the pattern starts again. The pattern repeats, and several patterns placed in order build a song.

Even so, every hit still plays exactly at the start of its slot. Real drummers rarely play exactly in time, and listeners often notice that perfect timing comes from a machine. One common change delays every second step slightly. That is called swing. Other tools add small random changes to timing and loudness. Some copy the timing of a real performance onto parts built from steps.

This programming is often done on equipment made only for this job. A drum machine has built-in drum sounds and a step sequencer. Other machines play short recordings from rubber pads, and a harder finger hit plays louder. Some older machines store sound with less detail, which adds a faint hiss.

The recordings on those pads often come from old records. A short section of a record where only the drums play, used in a new song, is called a breakbeat. Producers cut it into single hits and play them in a new order. That is called chopping. But speeding up a recording to fit a new tempo also raises its pitch. Changing the length of a sound without changing its pitch is called time stretching. Most methods cut the sound into short overlapping pieces and move them closer together or farther apart.

When those pieces are far too short to hear as separate sounds, each one is called an audio grain. Many grains play together, and each one fades in and out. You control how long each grain lasts, how many play each second, and where in the recording they come from. Holding that point still turns one moment into a long, steady sound.

The flashcards explain each part in detail: steps and patterns, timing and feel, common rhythm patterns, the machines, cutting and stretching recordings, and the controls of grains. By the end, you will know how a beat is built, how it gets the timing of a real player, and how sound is stretched and rebuilt from grains.
