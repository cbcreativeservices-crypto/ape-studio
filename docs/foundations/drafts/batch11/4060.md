Topic Foundations: Sample Editing and Beat Programming

Imagine tapping a rhythm on a table. Some taps are loud, some are soft, and some come early or late. A machine can repeat a rhythm perfectly. So this topic asks: how is a drum beat built by hand from short sounds, and how does it keep the small timing changes of a real player?

The work usually starts with one bar of music (a short group of beats) divided into equal slots. Each slot plays a drum sound or stays silent. A tool that works this way is called a step sequencer. Each step can also store how hard and how long the sound plays. The pattern repeats, and several patterns placed in order build a song. Many styles are known by where their hits fall. A kick drum on every beat is common in dance music. Others place accents away from the strong beats. Patterns of different lengths can also repeat together and line up differently each time.

Even so, every hit plays exactly at the start of its slot. Real drummers rarely play exactly in time, and listeners often notice perfect timing. One common change delays every second step slightly. That is called swing. Other tools add small random changes to timing and loudness. Some copy the timing of a real performance onto parts built from steps.

This work is often done on machines made for it. A drum machine has built-in drum sounds and a step sequencer. Other machines play short recordings from rubber pads, and a harder hit plays louder. Some older machines store each sound as fewer, rougher measurements. This adds faint noise and can remove some high frequencies.

The recordings on those pads often come from old records. A short section of a record where only the drums play, used in a new song, is called a breakbeat. Producers cut it into single hits and play them in a new order. That is called chopping. But speeding up a recording to fit a new tempo also raises its pitch. Changing the length of a sound without changing its pitch is called time stretching. Most methods cut the sound into short overlapping pieces and move them closer together or farther apart.

When those pieces are far too short to hear as separate sounds, each one is called an audio grain. Many grains play together, and each one fades in and out. You control how long each grain lasts, how many play each second, and where in the recording they come from. Holding that point still turns one moment into a long, steady sound. Each grain's pitch can also change on its own, without changing the timing.

The flashcards explain each of these parts in detail. By the end, you will know how a beat is built, how it gets the timing of a real player, and how sound is stretched and rebuilt from grains.
