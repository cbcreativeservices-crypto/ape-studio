Topic Foundations: DAW: Logic

In the topic on digital audio workstation basics, you learned how one program records, edits and mixes a song. This topic studies one such program in depth, called Logic. Besides those jobs, it includes many instruments and parts that can play for you. So this topic asks: how does one program organize all of that work, from the first idea to the finished mix?

The answer starts with the project. The project file holds the whole song and its settings. In the main window, each track is a row. Special rows across the top hold the tempo and sections of the whole song.

With the tracks ready, you record. Often a singer repeats one section many times while the program plays it in a loop. The program keeps every attempt together in one container on the track. That container is called a take folder. You drag across the best part of each attempt to join those parts into one performance.

Even a good performance can have a late note or a note slightly out of tune. The program can stretch recorded sound in small places, so a late drum hit moves onto the beat without cutting the recording. That method is called flex time. A related method shows each sung note as a block that you move up or down to change its pitch. The program can also make the song's tempo follow a player who recorded without a steady guide beat.

Some parts do not need a human player. The program includes virtual musicians that play drums, bass and keyboard parts. You choose a style and give simple directions, and the program writes a part; bass and keyboard parts follow the song's chords. These virtual musicians are called session players.

Every part needs a sound. You can choose a ready-made sound from a library. A saved sound with its instrument and effects is called a Logic patch. Some built-in instruments play recordings of real instruments, and others create sound from electronic waves. Effects add the echo of a room or control loudness. These sounds then go to the mixer. When several tracks need the same room echo, each sends a copy of its sound to one shared channel. A mixer channel that records nothing and only receives sound from other channels is called an auxiliary channel strip. Finally, every channel is combined into one mix, saved as a new sound file.

The flashcards explain the project and its windows, recording and choosing attempts, timing and pitch editing, note editing, and loop grids. They also cover the virtual musicians, the instruments and effects, the mixer, and a related program for the stage. By the end, you will know how Logic organizes a song from the first idea to the finished mix.
