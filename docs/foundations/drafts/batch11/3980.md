Topic Foundations: DAW: Logic

In the topic on digital audio workstation (DAW) basics, you learned how one program records, edits and mixes a song. This topic studies one such program in depth, called Logic Pro. It also includes many instruments, effects and virtual musicians. So this topic asks: how does one program organize all of that work, from the first idea to the finished mix?

The answer starts with the project. The project file holds the whole song and its settings. In the main window, each track is a row. Special rows across the top hold the tempo and sections of the whole song. A second view holds short repeating parts in boxes. You start them in any order to try ideas before placing them on the timeline. That view is called the Live Loops grid.

With the tracks ready, you record. Often a singer repeats one section many times while it plays in a loop. The program keeps every attempt together in one container on the track. That container is called a take folder. You drag across the best part of each attempt to join them into one performance.

Even a good performance can have a late note or a note slightly out of tune. The program can stretch recorded sound in small places, so a late drum hit moves onto the beat. That method is called flex time. A related method shows each sung note as a block that you move up or down to change its pitch.

Some parts do not need a human player. The program includes virtual musicians that play drums, bass and keyboard parts. You choose a style and give simple directions, and the program writes a part. Bass and keyboard parts follow the song's chords. These virtual musicians are called session players.

Every part needs a sound. Some built-in instruments play recordings of real instruments. Others create sound from electronic waves, or change and blend recorded sounds in many ways. Effects can add the echo of a room, control loudness, or copy the sound of a guitar amplifier. A saved sound with its instrument and effects is called a Logic patch. A related program for the stage uses the same instruments and effects, so these sounds can be played live.

These sounds then go to the mixer. When several tracks need the same room echo, each sends a copy of its sound to one shared channel. A mixer channel that does not record its own track, and instead receives sound sent from other channels, is called an auxiliary channel strip. Finally, every channel is combined into one mix and saved as a new sound file.

The flashcards explain each part in detail, including the instruments, the effects and the program for the stage. By the end, you will know how Logic organizes a song from the first idea to the finished mix.
