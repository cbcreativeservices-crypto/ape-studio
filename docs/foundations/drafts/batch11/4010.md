Topic Foundations: Ableton Live

A recording program usually shows a song on a timeline that plays from start to end. But many musicians begin with short repeating parts, such as a drum pattern, and try them in different orders. Some perform with a computer on stage. So how can one program hold a finished song and also be played during a performance?

It gives you two views of the same tracks. One is the timeline, called the Arrangement View. The other is a grid. Each column is a track, and each box can hold one short recording or set of notes, called a session clip. You start clips, or a whole row of them, in any order. Each one usually starts at the beginning of the next bar (a group of beats), so everything stays in time. This grid is called the Session View.

Clips only play in time together at the same speed. A repeating drum pattern recorded faster than your song must slow down without changing pitch. The program finds the beats and stretches the sound between them. Drums, one voice and a full song each usually suit a different method, called a warp mode.

You record into an empty box or onto the timeline. The program also keeps the notes you play even when it is not recording, so they can still become a clip. Notes can be drawn and edited, and a saved timing pattern, such as swing, can be added to any clip.

A clip of notes makes no sound by itself. It needs an instrument, and effects can then change it. On each track, these parts are placed in a row, in the order the sound passes through them. That row is called the device chain. The program includes instruments that play recordings, synthesizers, and many effects. Several devices can be grouped into one box, controlled by a few main knobs. One such group places a different drum sound on each pad. It is called a Drum Rack.

Each track chooses where its sound comes from and where it goes. Most tracks are combined on one final track, and some send part of their sound to a shared effect. Physical devices with knobs and buttons can also move the controls on the screen. Last, the work is saved. A song file is kept in a project folder and lists the sound files it uses, and one command copies every sound into that folder. A side panel called the browser lists the included sounds and your own saved settings.

The flashcards cover the two views, stretching and clip settings, recording and editing notes, devices and their groups, included instruments and effects, routing and control, and files. By the end, you will know how one set of musical ideas can be performed in any order, recorded, and finished as a song.
