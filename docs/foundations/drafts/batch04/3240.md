Topic Foundations: Console Architecture & Bus Structure

Imagine a band in a small club. Every microphone is connected to one large mixing console at the back. Each signal arrives on its own channel, a column of controls. The audience needs one blend of all the sounds. The singer needs a different blend in the speaker at her feet. So how is one console organized to make many mixes from the same inputs at once?

The answer is a set of shared paths inside the console. Each path adds many channels together into one signal. The final path, which carries sound to the main left and right outputs, is called the mix bus. A knob on each channel sends a copy of its signal to another path, at its own level. That knob is called an aux send, short for auxiliary send. One send for each performer gives every player a personal mix. A matrix blends finished mixes into new outputs.

Channels are often grouped. The drum channels can share one path with one fader, a sliding level control. That is called a subgroup, because the drums are added into one signal. Another grouping control only changes the level of its assigned channels, and no sound passes through it. On digital consoles, this control is called a digitally controlled amplifier. One button can also mute a whole set of channels.

Each channel also needs a place between the left and right speakers. A knob sends more of the signal to one side. This is called panning. A sound in the center plays from both speakers and can seem louder. So the console turns a centered sound down a little. That rule is called the pan law. In surround sound, the same idea places a sound among many speakers.

With so many mixes, the engineer needs to hear one channel alone. A button sends that channel to the engineer's headphones. That is called solo. Another, called talkback, lets the engineer speak to the performers. Usually solo changes only what the engineer hears. But some solo modes mute all other channels in the main output. During a show, the audience would hear only one channel, so engineers check the mode first.

Consoles are built in different ways. On an analog console, each knob is part of the signal path. On many digital consoles, the controls only send commands to a separate processing unit. Because every setting is a number, the console can save a snapshot of all settings and load it again. A protected channel keeps its settings when a snapshot loads.

The flashcards explain each part, from the kinds of consoles to the paths, groups, panning, listening controls and saved shows. By the end, you will know how a console turns many inputs into many mixes. You will also know how the engineer controls and checks them, and how a show is saved and loaded again.
