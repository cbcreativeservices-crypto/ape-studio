Topic Foundations: Loudspeaker Measurement & Modeling

Imagine two speakers in a shop. The text on both packages says the speaker plays low notes at high volume. You can compare them by listening, but every room changes the sound. Engineers need a method that gives the same answer anywhere. So how do they measure what a speaker really does, and predict it before it is built?

The first measurement uses no microphone. A test signal goes into the speaker at many pitches, and a meter records how much it resists the current at each. This test is called an impedance sweep. The curve has a tall peak at one low pitch, where the cone moves most easily. A second sweep is made with a small weight added to the cone. The two sweeps reveal the weight of the moving parts and the stiffness of the rings that hold the cone. These numbers are called the Thiele-Small parameters.

Those numbers go into a model. At low frequencies the cone moves as one solid piece. Engineers describe it as a mass, a spring and a part that removes energy, drawn as an electrical circuit. This is called the lumped-parameter model. It is no longer accurate when the cone bends at higher frequencies. At high volume the rings, magnet and coil also change, so more detailed models predict the distortion.

A model is only a prediction, so the sound is measured too. Reflections from walls add false peaks and dips. A microphone close to the cone measures the bass with little room sound. A microphone farther away measures high frequencies before the first reflection arrives. The two results are joined into one curve. Then the speaker is measured at many angles. A three-dimensional picture of the level in every direction is called a directivity balloon.

For a speaker at home, those angles are combined into a few standard curves. One shows the sound that reaches the listener directly. Others estimate the sound that reflects from the walls. This set is called a spinorama.

These curves are measured at a moderate level, so loudness needs its own tests. When a speaker plays loud for a long time, its coil gets hot and resists the current more. The sound becomes quieter than expected. That loss is called power compression. Other tests check how far the cone can move cleanly, and whether the speaker survives short peaks. A number on a data sheet is useful only when you know how it was tested.

In the factory, a machine compares every finished speaker with one that is known to work correctly, and detects the buzzing of a loose part. Sample units are also shaken, dropped and heated. The flashcards explain each of these steps in detail. By the end, you will know how a speaker's behavior is measured and predicted, and what the numbers on a data sheet describe.
