Topic Foundations: Loudspeaker Measurement and Modeling

Imagine choosing between two speakers online. Each comes with a data sheet of numbers and curves. Those numbers must describe the speaker itself, not the room where it was tested. So how do they measure what a speaker really does, and predict it before it is built?

The first measurement uses no microphone. A test signal goes into the speaker at many frequencies, and a meter records how much it resists the current at each. This test is called an impedance sweep. The curve has a tall peak at the resonance, the low frequency where the cone moves most easily. Often a second sweep is made with a small weight on the cone. Together, the sweeps reveal the weight of the moving parts and the stiffness of the rings that hold the cone. They also show how strongly the movement is slowed. These numbers are called the Thiele-Small parameters.

Those numbers go into a model. At low frequencies the cone moves as one solid piece. Engineers describe it as a mass, a spring and a part that removes energy. They can be drawn as an electrical circuit and calculated the same way. This is called the lumped-parameter model. It stops being accurate when the cone bends at higher frequencies. At high volume the push of the motor and the stiffness of the rings change as the cone moves, so more detailed models are used.

A model is only a prediction, so the sound is measured too. Reflections from walls add false peaks and dips. A microphone close to the cone measures the bass with little room sound. A microphone farther away measures high frequencies, using only the sound that arrives before reflections. The two results are joined into one curve. Measurements at many angles form a three-dimensional picture of the level in every direction. It is called a directivity balloon.

For home speakers, the angles are combined into a few standard curves. One shows the sound that reaches the listener directly. Others estimate the sound reflected from the walls. This set is called a spinorama.

These curves use a moderate level, so loudness needs its own tests. When a speaker plays loud for a long time, its hot coil resists the current more, so the sound is quieter than expected. That loss is called power compression. Other tests check how far the cone can move cleanly, and whether short peaks damage the speaker. The same kinds of tests continue in the factory, where a machine compares every finished speaker with one known to work correctly and detects the buzzing of a loose part. Sample units are also shaken, dropped and heated.

The flashcards explain each of these steps, from the first impedance test to the factory check. By the end, you will know how a speaker's behavior is measured and predicted, and what the numbers on a data sheet describe.
