Topic Foundations: Crossover and System Engineering

Imagine opening the front of a loudspeaker box. Often there is a large cone and a small one. Each belongs to a driver, the part that turns an electrical signal into sound. A large driver moves enough air for low frequencies but does not reproduce high frequencies well. A small driver does the opposite. So how is the sound divided between them, and how does it combine again so every frequency reaches the listener at the right level?

The dividing is done by a filter called a crossover. It sends low frequencies to the large driver and high frequencies to the small one. The frequency where one driver stops and the other starts is chosen within the range both drivers handle well. Around that frequency, the filter reduces the level gradually, not at once. How quickly it reduces the level is called the crossover slope.

Inside many boxes, the crossover is a small circuit after the amplifier. In larger systems, the sound is often divided before the amplifiers, so each frequency range has its own amplifier. Using two amplifiers in this way is called biamping. The dividing is then usually done by a digital unit called a loudspeaker processor. It also adjusts tone and delay, stores settings for each speaker model, and limits the level to protect the drivers from sudden peaks and overheating.

Delay matters near the crossover frequency, where both drivers play the same notes. They sit a small distance apart, so their sounds reach a listener at slightly different times. In some directions the sounds add together, and in others they partly cancel. This pattern of louder and quieter directions is called lobing. Careful matching of timing, level and polarity (whether each cone moves out or in first) helps the two drivers combine evenly.

The same adding and cancelling happens between whole boxes. For a large audience, many boxes are often hung close together in a tall column. When aligned, they act as one long source and lose less level over distance. Boxes aimed at near seats can play more quietly than boxes aimed at far seats. Small delays on each box can also aim the sound without tilting the column.

Subwoofers, the boxes that play only the lowest frequencies, are different, because they send those frequencies in almost all directions, including back onto the stage. Several subwoofers can be placed in a pattern and given different delays, so their sound adds together toward the audience. Some subwoofers can also be delayed and have their polarity reversed, so the sound behind mostly cancels. This is called a cardioid subwoofer array.

The flashcards explain each step in detail, from the filters inside one box to the processors and arrays of large systems. By the end, you will know how drivers, amplifiers and boxes are combined so a whole system plays as one source.
