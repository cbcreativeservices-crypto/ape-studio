Topic Foundations: Crossover and System Engineering

Imagine listening to a song on one speaker. The deep bass drum and the high cymbals come from the same box. Inside, there is often a large cone and a small one. Each is part of a driver, the unit that turns an electrical signal into sound. A large driver suits low frequencies, and a small driver suits high frequencies. So how is the sound divided between them, and how does it combine again at the right level?

The dividing is done by a filter called a crossover. It sends low frequencies to the large driver and high frequencies to the small one. The frequency where one driver stops and the other starts is chosen where both drivers work well. Around that frequency, the level falls gradually. How quickly it falls is called the crossover slope.

Inside many boxes, the crossover is a small circuit after the amplifier. In larger systems, the sound is often divided before the amplifiers, so each frequency range has its own amplifier. Using two amplifiers in this way is called biamping. The dividing is then usually done by a digital unit called a loudspeaker processor. It stores each speaker model's settings as a preset. Its limiters turn the level down to protect the drivers from sudden peaks and from overheating. It also adjusts the tone and the delay of each frequency range.

That delay matters near the crossover frequency, where both drivers play the same notes. They sit apart, so their sounds reach a listener at slightly different times. In some directions the sounds add together, and in others they partly cancel. This pattern of louder and quieter directions is called lobing. Careful matching of timing, level and polarity helps them combine evenly. Polarity is whether each cone moves out or in first.

The same adding and cancelling happens between whole boxes. For a large audience, many boxes are often hung together in a tall column. When their timing is aligned, their sounds add into one tall wave. This wave spreads less up and down, so it loses level more slowly toward the back. Boxes aimed at near seats can play more quietly. Small, different delays on each box can also tilt the sound without moving the column.

Subwoofers are the boxes that play only the lowest frequencies. They send those frequencies in almost all directions, including back onto the stage. Several subwoofers placed in a pattern with different delays can add their sound together toward the audience. Some can also be delayed and have their polarity reversed, so the sound behind mostly cancels. This is called a cardioid subwoofer array.

The flashcards explain each step in detail, from the filters inside one box to the processors and arrays of large systems. By the end, you will know how drivers, amplifiers and boxes are combined so a whole system plays as one source.
