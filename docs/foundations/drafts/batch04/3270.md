Topic Foundations: Connectors & Plugs

Imagine pushing your earbuds into a phone, and the music starts. On a stage, cables join microphones, mixers and speakers. Each cable ends in hardware that makes a firm electrical contact that you can also disconnect. That hardware is called a connector. So which connector carries which signal, and how do you join equipment without adding noise?

Every connector has two halves. The half on the cable is called the plug. The fixed socket on the equipment is called the jack. Microphones usually use a round, locking plug with three pins. Guitars use a long plug with two metal sections. A third section can carry stereo sound to headphones, or one signal on two wires. So plugs that look the same can carry different things. The wiring diagram that shows what each pin carries is called the pinout. Both ends of a cable must follow it.

That third section matters because a low electrical buzz, called hum, can enter cables along their length. When two matched wires carry the signal, noise reaches both wires almost equally. The input keeps only the difference between the wires, so the noise cancels. This is called a balanced connection. With only one signal wire, the noise stays, so those cables are kept short. A switch can disconnect the cable's outer shield wire to stop hum between two devices, but the safety ground in the power cable always stays connected.

Sometimes the plug does not fit the jack. A small part that lets one plug type fit a different jack type is called an adapter. It usually changes only the shape, so it cannot make a signal balanced. A small box can change a guitar's weak signal into a balanced one for a distant mixer. Other boxes copy one microphone signal to several mixers without passing hum between them.

Large systems carry many signals at once. One large plug with many pins can carry several channels together. On stage, a metal box can send all the microphone signals as digital data down one thin cable. Home and video equipment often use plugs with one center pin and an outer shield. Digital audio also travels as light in a fiber, or over locking network cable.

Speaker cables carry high current, so speaker plugs lock, and their metal contacts are covered. One common locking power plug must only be connected or disconnected when the power is off. In installed systems, bare wires are often clamped under small screws. Any contact can wear out. Two different metals in damp air can corrode, and a loose or dirty contact often fails first.

The flashcards explain each connector family, the wiring inside it, and the boxes that change or copy a signal. By the end, you will know which connector carries each kind of signal, how balanced and unbalanced wiring behave, and the signs of a worn connection.
