Topic Foundations: Connectors and Plugs

Imagine pushing earbuds into a phone, and music starts. Each cable ends in a connector, a part that makes a firm contact and can be disconnected. So which connector carries which signal, and how do you join equipment without adding noise?

Every connector has two halves. The cable half is called the plug, and the socket on the equipment is called the jack. Microphones usually use a round locking plug with three pins. Home equipment often uses a plug with a center pin. Guitars usually use a long plug with two metal sections. A similar plug with a third section can carry stereo sound to headphones, or one signal on two matched wires. A diagram of what each pin carries is called the pinout.

Two matched wires matter because noise can enter a cable along its length and reach both wires almost equally. The input keeps only the difference between them, so the noise cancels. This is called a balanced connection. Cables with one signal wire do not cancel noise, so they are usually short. Microphone and instrument cables also have a thin wire or foil shield. A steady low tone called hum often comes from the ground, the reference wire that connected devices share. A switch can disconnect the shield to stop it. But the safety ground, the power cable wire that protects people from shock, must always stay connected.

When joining equipment, a plug may not fit the jack. A part that makes it fit is called an adapter. Most adapters change only the shape, not the signal. A direct injection box changes a guitar's signal into a balanced, low-level signal for a microphone input. Other boxes, called splits, copy one microphone signal to several mixers. Some use a transformer, which passes the signal without a direct electrical path, so it can block ground hum.

Many microphones need many connections. A stage box gathers them on stage, often into one plug with many pins. A digital stage box sends them as data through one cable. Digital audio often uses its own connectors. Some use coaxial cable, one wire inside a round shield. Others send light through a thin fiber, or use network and computer data cables.

Speaker cables carry something different: the amplifier's large, high-current signal. Professional speaker plugs usually lock and cover their metal contacts. Power cables have their own plugs, and one common locking type must be connected and disconnected only with the power off.

Every plug and jack in this chain can wear out or corrode. A loose or dirty contact is a common cause of crackle or lost sound.

The flashcards explain each connector family, its wiring, and the boxes that change or copy a signal. By the end, you will know which connector carries each signal, how balanced wiring reduces noise, and the signs of a worn connection.
