Topic Foundations: Connectors and Plugs

Imagine pushing your earbuds into a phone, and the music starts. On stage, cables join microphones, mixers and speakers. Each cable ends in a connector, a part that makes a firm contact and can be disconnected. So which connector carries which signal, and how do you join equipment without adding noise?

Every connector has two halves. The half on the cable is called the plug, and the fixed socket on the equipment is called the jack. Microphones usually use a round locking plug with three pins. Home equipment often uses a plug with a center pin inside a shield. Guitars use a long plug with two metal sections. On that plug, a third section can carry stereo sound to headphones, or one signal on two wires. So plugs that look alike can carry different things. A diagram of what each pin carries is called the pinout. Both ends of a cable must follow it.

That third section matters because a low buzz, called hum, can enter a cable along its length. When two matched wires carry the signal, noise reaches both almost equally. The input keeps only the difference between them, so the noise cancels. This is called a balanced connection. With one signal wire, the noise stays, so such cables are kept short. Most cables also have a shield of thin wires around them. A switch can disconnect the shield to stop hum between two devices. But the safety ground, the power cable wire that protects people from shock, must always stay connected.

Sometimes the plug does not fit the jack. A small part that lets one plug fit a different jack is called an adapter. A simple adapter changes only the shape, not the signal. A small box can change a guitar's weak signal into a balanced one. Other boxes copy one microphone signal to several mixers, some with a transformer that stops hum passing between them.

In larger systems, one plug with many pins can carry several channels. A stage box gathers every microphone signal on stage, and a digital one can send them all as data down one thin cable. Digital audio also travels as light in a thin glass or plastic fiber, or over network cable.

Speaker cables carry high current. Professional speaker plugs usually lock, and their metal contacts are covered. Simpler terminals clamp bare wire under a screw. One common locking power plug must be connected and disconnected only with the power off. Whatever the family, every contact can wear out. Two different metals that touch in damp air can corrode, and a loose or dirty contact often fails first.

The flashcards explain each connector family, the wiring inside it, and the boxes that change or copy a signal. By the end, you will know which connector carries each signal, how balanced wiring stops noise, and the signs of a worn connection.
