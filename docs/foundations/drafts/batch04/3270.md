Topic Foundations: Connectors and Plugs

Imagine pushing earbuds into a phone, and music starts. Each cable ends in a connector, a part that makes a firm contact and can be disconnected. So which connector carries which signal, and how do you join equipment without adding noise?

Every connector has two halves. The cable half is called the plug, and the socket on the equipment is called the jack. Microphones usually use a round locking plug with three pins. Home equipment often uses a plug with a center pin inside a shield. Guitars usually use a long plug with two metal sections. A similar plug with a third section can carry stereo sound to headphones, or one signal on two matched wires. A diagram of what each pin carries is called the pinout, and both cable ends must follow it.

Two matched wires matter because unwanted noise can enter a cable along its length. Noise reaches both wires almost equally. The input keeps only the difference between them, so the noise cancels. This is called a balanced connection. Cables with one signal wire keep the noise, so they stay short. Microphone and instrument cables also have a thin wire or foil shield. A steady low tone called hum often comes from the grounds of two connected devices. A switch can disconnect the shield to stop it. But the safety ground, the power cable wire that protects people from shock, must always stay connected.

A part that lets a plug fit a different jack is called an adapter. Most adapters change only the shape, not the signal. A direct injection box changes a guitar's signal into a balanced, microphone-level one. Its switches can lift the ground or lower the level. Other boxes, called splits, copy one microphone signal to several mixers. Some use a transformer, which passes the signal without a direct electrical path, so hum cannot pass.

One plug with many pins can carry several channels. A stage box gathers the microphone signals on stage, and a digital one sends them as data through one cable. Digital audio also travels on coaxial cable, one wire inside a round shield, as light in a thin fiber, or over network and computer data cables.

Speaker cables carry high current, not a small signal. Professional speaker plugs usually lock and cover their metal contacts. Simpler terminals clamp bare wire under a screw. One common locking power plug must be connected and disconnected only with the power off.

All of these contacts can wear out, and two different metals touching in damp air can corrode. A loose or dirty contact is a common cause of crackle or lost sound.

The flashcards explain each connector family, its wiring, and the boxes that change or copy a signal. By the end, you will know which connector carries each signal, how balanced wiring stops noise, and the signs of a worn connection.
