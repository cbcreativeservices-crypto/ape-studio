Topic Foundations: Console Signal Flow & Gain Staging

Imagine a singer saying a few test words into a microphone before a show, with the cable running to a mixing console. The console repeats the same controls in a row of narrow columns, one for each input. Each column is called a channel strip. Sound enters at the top and passes many controls on its way down. So what happens to the sound at each step, and how is its level set so it stays free of hiss and distortion?

The first step is choosing what comes in. A microphone makes a very weak signal. A keyboard makes a much stronger one. A switch that tells the channel which of the two to expect is called the mic/line switch. A guitar connected directly needs its own input setting. Many microphones also need electric power through the cable, so it is switched on where it is needed.

Next, the channel raises the weak signal to a useful level. This boost is called preamp gain. Too little leaves the sound close to the background hiss of the electronics. Too much makes the loudest moments distort. The aim is a high level, with some level left unused for sudden loud sounds. A drum close to its microphone can be too loud even at the lowest gain. A switch that lowers the signal before the boost is called an input pad. Digital consoles also allow small level changes later.

With the level set, the sound passes controls that correct it. A filter can remove deep rumble. When two microphones pick up one drum, their signals can partly cancel. A switch that reverses one signal, so that each push of the signal becomes a pull, is called polarity invert. Then the two usually add together. The sound can also leave the channel, pass through an external unit, and come back. That route is called an insert.

Near the bottom is the fader, the sliding control that sets the channel's level in the mix. Because the gain was set earlier, the fader position does not show how strong the signal is. Believing it does is called the fader position fallacy. A copy taken before the fader is a pre-fader send. It stays the same when the fader moves, so it is often sent to the speakers that performers listen to. A copy taken after the fader is a post-fader send. It follows the fader, so it is often sent to effects units.

A large show has many strips, so they must stay organized. Each channel can get a name and a color. Two channels can be linked so they change together. Settings can be copied, saved and loaded later.

The flashcards follow this same path, step by step. By the end, you will know how sound travels through one channel strip, and how its level is set at each step.
