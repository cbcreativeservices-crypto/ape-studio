Topic Foundations: Enclosures, Horns and Radiation

Imagine playing music through a speaker's driver, the part that makes the sound, taken out of its box. The bass almost disappears. This topic asks how the box, the horn and the front panel decide how much sound comes out, how low its notes can go, and where it goes.

The bass disappears because a cone makes sound from both sides. When the front pushes air forward, the back pulls air in, so the two sounds are opposite. Low sounds travel around the edge, and the two sides cancel. A flat board around the driver keeps them apart. That board is called the baffle. Most speakers also enclose the back in a box, braced and lined with soft material so its walls and air do not ring.

A box closed on every side is called a sealed enclosure. The trapped air is squeezed each time the cone moves in, and it pushes the cone outward again. A smaller box squeezes the air more, so its lowest useful note is higher. A speaker can be small, play very low notes, or play loudly with little power, but not all three at once.

Other boxes use the back sound. A tube lets air move in and out. The air in the tube has weight, and the box air pushes back on it. Together they move most easily at one low frequency, where the tube adds bass. That tube, or opening, is called the port. A port that is too small makes a rushing noise at high volume. Some boxes instead use a second cone with no magnet or coil, or a long tunnel inside.

Boxes mostly change the bass, but a horn changes how well the driver moves air. Air gives little resistance to a small vibrating surface, so little sound leaves it. A horn is a tube that slowly widens in front of the driver, so the driver moves air more efficiently. The narrow end, where the driver connects, is called the horn throat. The wide end, the mouth, releases the sound. How fast the horn widens sets the lowest frequency it helps.

A horn also aims the sound. How a speaker spreads sound in different directions is called directivity. Low frequencies spread widely, but the same driver sends high frequencies forward in a narrower beam. Special horn shapes keep the width of the beam almost the same over a wide range of frequencies. Even a flat front panel aims sound. Above a frequency set by the panel's width, sound goes mostly forward, so the level in front rises. That change is called the baffle step.
