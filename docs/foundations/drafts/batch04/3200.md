Topic Foundations: Transducer Design and Motor Systems

Imagine a loudspeaker at a party. When the bass plays, you can see its cone move in and out and push the air. The part of a loudspeaker that makes the sound is the driver. This topic asks how an electrical signal moves the cone, and what limits how loud the sound can be, and how closely it follows the signal.

The movement starts with a coil of thin wire attached to the cone. The coil sits in a narrow ring-shaped space inside a strong magnet. That space is called the magnetic gap. When current flows through the coil, the magnet's field pushes the coil, and the coil pushes the cone. When the current reverses, the push reverses. The amount of push for each unit of current is called the force factor.

But the push is not the same at every position. When the cone moves far, part of the coil leaves the gap, so the push becomes weaker. The coil's current also makes its own magnetic field, which changes the magnet's field slightly. Both effects add tones that were not in the signal. A metal ring near the center of the magnet reduces the second effect. It is called a shorting ring.

Flexible rings, the suspension, hold the cone and return it to the center. How easily they let it move is called suspension compliance. How far the cone moves from its resting position is called excursion. Far from the center, the suspension becomes stiffer, so the movement no longer follows the signal exactly. Past a larger limit, parts hit each other and can break.

Even when the cone moves only a little, heat sets another limit. Most of the amplifier's power becomes heat in the coil. Hot wire carries current less easily, so the sound stops getting louder at the same rate as the signal.

The cone itself also has a limit. At low frequencies, the whole cone moves together, as one solid piece. At higher frequencies, parts of it bend separately, some moving in while others move out. This is called a breakup mode, and it adds peaks and dips to the sound. So designers look for cone materials that are stiff but light, and that absorb some of their own vibration.

Other drivers move the air in different ways. Some move a thin metal strip or a small dome instead of a cone. Others use a material that bends when a voltage is applied. Headphones use very small drivers. Their ear seal changes the bass, and some use microphones to help cancel outside noise.

The flashcards explain each part in detail: the magnet and coil, the suspension, heat, cone materials, other drivers, and headphones. By the end, you will know how a driver turns a signal into movement, and why its magnet and coil, suspension, cone and heat each limit its sound.
