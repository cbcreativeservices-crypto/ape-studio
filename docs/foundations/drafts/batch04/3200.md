Topic Foundations: Transducer Design and Motor Systems

Imagine a loudspeaker at a party. When the bass plays, its cone moves in and out. The part that makes the sound is the driver. Headphones contain drivers too. So how does an electrical signal move a driver's cone, and what limits its loudness and accuracy?

The movement starts with a coil of thin wire attached to the cone. The coil sits in a narrow ring-shaped space inside a strong magnet. That space is called the magnetic gap. Current in the coil makes the magnet's field push the coil, and the coil pushes the cone. When the current reverses, so does the push. The amount of push for each unit of current is called the force factor.

But the push is not the same at every position. When the cone moves far, part of the coil leaves the gap, so the push becomes weaker. The cone then no longer follows the signal exactly, which adds tones that were not in it. The coil's current can also change the field a little, adding more of these tones.

Flexible rings, the suspension, hold the cone and return it to the center. How easily they let it move is called suspension compliance. How far the cone moves from its resting position is its excursion. Far from the center, the suspension usually becomes stiffer, adding the same kind of error. Movement is one limit. Heat is another, because most of the amplifier's power becomes heat in the coil. Hot wire carries current less easily, so the speaker plays more quietly than the signal asks.

The cone also has a limit. At low frequencies, the whole cone moves as one solid piece. At higher frequencies, parts of it bend separately. This is called a breakup mode, and it adds peaks and dips to the sound. So designers look for stiff, light cone materials.

Many drivers for high frequencies use no cone. Some move a thin metal strip, and some use a material that bends when a voltage is applied. A compression driver pushes air from a dome through a smaller opening into a horn, which makes it very loud. Inside, a phase plug, a part with shaped channels, makes sound from every point of the dome arrive together.

Headphones use the same ideas, but smaller. Most use a thin diaphragm moved by a coil, and others use a flat sheet or a small moving metal arm. How well the ear cups or ear tips seal changes the bass. Closed cups reduce outside sound, and open cups let air and sound pass through. Some headphones use small microphones to measure outside noise and play an inverted copy that cancels it.

The flashcards explain each of these limits and the other kinds of drivers. By the end, you will know how a driver turns a signal into movement, and what limits its loudness and accuracy.
