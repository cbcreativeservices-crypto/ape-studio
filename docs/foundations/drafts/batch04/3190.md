Topic Foundations: Vacuum Tubes

Imagine a guitar amplifier with its back panel open. Inside, small glass tubes glow. They make the weak signal from the strings strong enough to move a speaker. Each one is a vacuum tube, also called a valve. So how does a glowing glass tube make a signal stronger, and why must people handle it with care?

The glow comes from heat. Inside the glass there is almost no air. A small part is heated until electrons escape from its surface. That part is called the cathode. A metal part around it is held at a high positive voltage, so it pulls the electrons across the empty space. That part is called the plate. This voltage can kill, and it can stay stored after the equipment is unplugged. So technicians safely discharge it and measure it before touching anything.

To amplify, the tube must control that flow of electrons. A mesh of fine wire sits between the cathode and the plate. A small signal voltage on this mesh changes a much larger current to the plate. That mesh is called the control grid. A tube with these three main parts is called a triode. Other designs add more grids for more amplification or power. Small tubes make tiny signals bigger, and large tubes send power to the speaker.

A tube amplifies with little distortion only when it idles at the correct point. Even with no music, it draws a small, steady current. That idle condition is called the bias. With too much current, the tube overheats and can fail. A wrong bias can also distort the sound. The power supply provides the voltages a tube needs: a low voltage to heat the cathodes and a high voltage for the plates.

The large tubes at the end often share the work in pairs. One pushes the speaker and the other pulls it, a design called push-pull. This gives more power and cancels some hum. The two tubes must match, so they are often chosen to draw almost the same current.

Each tube is one of many standard types. Each has a name of letters and numbers and a typical job. Some small, quiet types are made for microphones. Larger types make output power. All tubes wear out. A plate that glows red shows the tube is far too hot. A tube that rings when someone taps it, or adds hiss or hum, often needs replacing. A tube tester checks how well a tube works.
