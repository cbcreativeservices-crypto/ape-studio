Topic Foundations: Vacuum Tubes

Imagine a guitar amplifier with its back panel open. Inside, small glass tubes glow. They make the weak guitar signal strong enough to move a speaker. Each one is a vacuum tube, also called a valve. So how does a glowing glass tube make a signal stronger, and why must people handle it with care?

The glow comes from heat. Inside the glass there is almost no air. A small part is heated until electrons escape from its surface. That part is called the cathode. A metal part around it is held at a high positive voltage, so it pulls the electrons across the empty space. That part is called the plate. This voltage can kill, and it can stay stored after the equipment is unplugged. So technicians safely discharge and measure it before touching anything.

To amplify, the tube must control that flow of electrons. A mesh of fine wire sits between the cathode and the plate. A small signal voltage on this mesh changes a much larger current to the plate. That mesh is called the control grid. A tube with these three main parts is a triode, and other designs add more grids.

A tube amplifies with the least distortion when it works at the correct point. Even with no music, it draws a small, steady current. That resting condition is called the bias. A wrong bias can distort the sound, and too much current makes the tube overheat and fail. That current and the heat both come from the power supply. Many supplies use their own tube, called a rectifier tube, to change wall power into the direct current the plates need.

An amplifier usually has several tubes in a row. Between them, small parts pass the signal from one tube to the next while blocking the high voltage. The last tubes send power to the speaker through an output transformer. It changes their high voltage into the larger current a speaker needs. One tube can amplify the whole signal alone, a design called single-ended. Often two tubes share the signal instead. Each amplifies one copy, and one copy is inverted, so it rises when the other falls. The transformer joins the results. This design is called push-pull. It gives more power and cancels some hum.

There are many standard types, each named with letters and numbers. Small types make tiny signals bigger. Larger power types drive the transformer. Every tube wears out. A plate that glows red shows the tube is far too hot. A tube that rings when someone taps it, or adds hiss or hum, often needs replacing.
