Topic Foundations: Vacuum Tubes

Imagine looking inside a guitar amplifier. Small glass tubes glow and make the weak guitar signal strong enough to move a speaker. Each one is a vacuum tube, also called a valve. Some microphones and preamplifiers use tubes too. So how does a glowing tube make a signal stronger, and why must it be handled with care?

The glow comes from heat. Inside the glass there is almost no air. A small part is heated until electrons escape from it. That part is called the cathode. A metal part around it is held at a high positive voltage, so it pulls the electrons across. That part is called the plate. This voltage can kill, and parts of the power supply can keep it stored after the equipment is unplugged. So technicians safely discharge and measure it before touching anything.

To amplify, the tube must control that flow of electrons. A mesh of fine wire sits between the cathode and the plate. A small signal voltage on this mesh changes a much larger current to the plate. That mesh is called the control grid. A tube with these three main parts is a triode, and other designs add more grids.

Even with no music, a tube draws a steady current. That resting condition is called the bias. A wrong bias can distort the sound, and too much current makes the tube overheat and fail. This current comes from the power supply. Many supplies use a rectifier tube to change the power from the wall outlet into the direct current the plates need.

An amplifier usually has several tubes in a row. Between them, small parts called capacitors pass the signal to the next tube while blocking the high voltage. In most amplifiers, the last tubes send power to the speaker through an output transformer. It changes their high voltage into the larger current a speaker needs.

The last stage can use one tube alone, a design called single-ended. Often two tubes share the signal instead, each amplifying one copy. One copy is inverted, so it rises when the other falls. This design is called push-pull. It gives more power and cancels some hum.

Because tubes do these different jobs, there are many standard types, named with letters and numbers. Small types make tiny signals bigger, and many hold two triodes in one glass. Larger power types drive the transformer. Whatever its type, every tube wears out. A plate glowing red means the tube is far too hot. A tube that rings when someone taps it, or adds hiss or hum in an amplifier or a microphone, often needs replacing.
