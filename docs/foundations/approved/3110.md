Topic Foundations: Specialized and Emerging Microphones

An ordinary microphone responds to the air. Tiny changes in air pressure move a thin part inside, and that movement becomes an electrical signal. Because the air carries every nearby sound, the microphone can also pick up sounds you do not want. So what other ways can sound be sensed, and when is each one the better choice?

On a loud stage, a microphone near a quiet mandolin also picks up the drums. One solution is to sense the instrument's vibration directly. A small part that turns an instrument's vibration into an electrical signal is called a pickup. A magnet and a coil of wire can sense steel strings moving. A thin crystal under the bridge can sense the pressure of the strings. Many instruments, from banjos to kotos, use pickups.

Electric guitars and basses mostly use magnetic pickups. A pickup near the neck usually gives more low frequencies, and one near the bridge gives more high frequencies. A pickup with a single coil also picks up hum from nearby electrical equipment. Two coils connected so the hum cancels make a quieter pickup, called a humbucker.

Pickups have their own problems. A magnetic pickup set too close to the strings can pull on them and affect the tuning. A long cable can reduce a pickup's high frequencies. Two pickups connected with reversed polarity partly cancel each other, so the bass becomes weak. A crystal pickup can add too much middle frequency when the strings are played hard. A pickup's signal is also often weak, so many instruments have a small amplifier built inside to strengthen it.

A trumpet, however, makes its sound in moving air, and the player often walks around. A tiny microphone attached to the trumpet is called a clip-on microphone. For speech in a very loud place, a throat microphone returns to the vibration idea. It rests against the neck and senses the shaking of the voice box through the skin.

Some microphones sense sounds above or below the range of human hearing. Others, built into phones, stay on all day using very little power until a spoken word switches them fully on. In meeting rooms, a ceiling panel can hold many tiny microphones that a chip combines to listen toward the speaker.

Newer designs change how the movement is measured. A microphone that measures the movement with light, which keeps electrical noise out, is called an optical microphone. A laser can even measure a window's vibration from far away. Other designs use electrically charged gas with no moving part, or measure how fast the air moves instead of its pressure. Software can also change the sound of one plain microphone so it resembles other microphones.
