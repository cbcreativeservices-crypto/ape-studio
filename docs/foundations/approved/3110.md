Topic Foundations: Specialized and Emerging Microphones

An ordinary microphone responds to the air. A thin, light part inside moves with tiny changes in air pressure, and that movement becomes an electrical signal. So it picks up all the sound around it.

On a loud stage, a microphone near a quiet mandolin also picks up the drums. One solution is to sense the instrument's vibration directly. A small part that turns an instrument's vibration into an electrical signal is called a pickup. A magnet and a coil of wire can sense steel strings moving. A thin crystal under the bridge can sense the pressure of the strings. A sensor attached to the wooden top can sense its vibration. Banjos, harps, pianos, kotos and many other instruments use pickups.

Electric guitars and basses use magnetic pickups the most. A pickup near the neck gives more low frequencies, and one near the bridge gives more high frequencies. A pickup with a single coil also picks up hum from nearby electrical equipment. Two coils connected so the hum cancels make a quieter pickup, called a humbucker.

For every type, a pickup's signal is often weak and easily reduced by a long cable. So many instruments have a small amplifier built inside to strengthen it. Some also add a tiny microphone inside the instrument, with a control to mix the two, for a more natural sound.

Sensing vibration works well for string and wooden instruments. A trumpet, however, makes its sound in moving air, and the player often walks around. A tiny microphone attached to the trumpet, near its wide opening, is called a clip-on microphone. A sensor worn against the throat works in the same way for a person who is speaking. It picks up the voice even in a very loud room.

All the designs so far cover the range of human hearing. Some go beyond that range, to very high sounds such as the calls of bats, or very low sounds below our hearing. Others are built into phones. They stay on all day using very little power, until a spoken word switches them fully on. All of these designs measure a moving part electrically. A microphone that measures the movement with light instead, which keeps electrical noise out, is called an optical microphone. A laser can measure the vibration of a window from far away. Other designs use electrically charged gas with no moving part, or measure how fast the air moves instead of its pressure.

The flashcards follow the same order: pickups on instruments from many countries, guitar and bass designs and their amplifiers, then microphones attached to players, microphones beyond human hearing, and new methods. By the end, you will know what each type senses, and why it is chosen when an ordinary microphone is not enough.
