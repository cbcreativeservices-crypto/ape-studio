Topic Foundations: Advanced Dynamics

In the core topic on compression, limiting and gating, you learned about the compressor. It turns the sound down when the sound goes above a set level. This topic answers the next question: how do you control exactly when, how and where it turns the sound down? The first step is how it decides. One part of a compressor measures the level of the sound and decides how much to turn it down. Another part does the turning down. The path that carries the sound to the measuring part is called the sidechain.

Because that part makes every decision, you can control the compressor by changing only what this part receives. Reduce the low frequencies in the sidechain, and a loud kick drum lowers the whole mix much less. Increase the "sss" frequencies in the sidechain, and the compressor reacts more strongly to them. This filter changes only what the measuring part receives. It does not change the sound you hear.

After the decision, timing shapes the result. Slow settings make the level change gradually, for example between a quiet verse and a loud chorus. Fast settings act on each separate drum hit. Some compressors return to normal quickly after a short peak, but slowly after a long loud section. Other timing tools change only the start of each hit, and some compressors raise the level of quiet parts instead of lowering loud parts.

The type of circuit also matters. Compressors turn the sound down with a light and a light sensor, a vacuum tube, a transistor, or a small chip. A light-based unit reacts gradually. A transistor unit reacts fast and makes the start of each hit more noticeable. The sound quality that a circuit adds is called character.

One compressor still changes the whole sound at once, so a loud bass note also lowers the cymbals. The solution is to divide the sound into low, middle and high frequency bands, each with its own compressor. That is called split-band compression. A stereo mix can also be divided into the center and the sides in the same way.

When one compressor has to turn the sound down a lot, it can sound unnatural, so the work is shared. Several compressors are connected one after another, and each one turns the sound down a little. That is called serial compression. At the end of this chain, a limiter keeps the loudest peaks below a set maximum level. A limiter cannot react instantly, so some limiters delay the sound slightly to detect peaks before they reach the output.

The flashcards explain each step in detail. When you finish, you will know how a compressor measures and decides, how timing and circuit type shape its sound, and how the work is divided, connected in series and finally kept below a maximum level.
