Topic Foundations: Advanced Dynamics

In the core topic on compression, limiting and gating, you learned that a compressor turns the sound down above a set level. This topic answers the next question: how do you control when, how and where it turns the sound down?

First, a compressor must decide when to act. One part of a compressor measures the sound and decides how much to turn it down. Another part does the turning down. The measuring part can check the sound before or after it is turned down. Checking before is called feedforward topology. Checking after is called feedback topology. The path that carries the sound to the measuring part is called the sidechain. A filter can remove low frequencies from the sidechain. Then a loud kick drum makes the compressor turn down the whole sound less. The filter changes only when and how much the sound is turned down, not the sound you hear. The sidechain can also listen to a different track, so one sound turns another down.

Next, timing changes the result. Slow settings make the level change gradually. Fast settings act on each drum hit. Some compressors change their release speed as the sound changes. Turning the sound down too fast on low notes can add distortion.

Timing also depends on the circuit that turns the sound down. Physical compressors often use a light and a light sensor, a vacuum tube, a transistor or a small chip. A light-based unit reacts gradually. A transistor unit can react fast. The sound quality that a circuit adds is called character.

One compressor still changes the whole sound, so a loud bass note also lowers the cymbals. The sound can be divided into frequency bands, each with its own compressor. That is called split-band compression. A stereo mix can also be divided into a center, the same in both speakers, and sides, the parts that differ. Each gets its own compressor.

One compressor turning the sound down a lot can sound unnatural, so the reduction is divided. Two compressors are connected one after the other, and each turns the sound down a little. That is called serial compression. Peak control can also be divided. A clipper instantly cuts off the tops of the sharpest peaks, so a limiter after it turns the sound down less.

Last, a limiter keeps peaks below a set maximum. A short peak can pass before a limiter reacts, so some limiters delay the sound slightly to detect peaks early. Some also check the level between the digital samples to find hidden peaks.

The flashcards explain each step, plus related tools that lower one sound when another plays, shape the start of each hit, or raise quiet parts instead. By the end, you will know how a compressor decides, how timing and circuit shape its sound, and how the reduction is divided and limited.
