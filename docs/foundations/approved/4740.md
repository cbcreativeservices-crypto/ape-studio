Topic Foundations: Advanced Dynamics

In the core topic on compression, limiting and gating, you learned about the compressor. It turns the sound down when the sound goes above a set level. This topic answers the next question: how do you control exactly when, how and where it turns the sound down?

The first step is how it decides. One part of a compressor measures the level of the sound and decides how much to turn it down. Another part does the turning down. The path that carries the sound to the measuring part is called the sidechain. So you can control the compressor by changing only what the measuring part receives. Reduce the low frequencies in the sidechain, and a loud kick drum lowers the whole mix less. Increase the "sss" frequencies in the sidechain, and the compressor reacts more strongly to them. The filter does not change what you hear.

After the decision, timing shapes the result. Slow settings make the level change gradually, for example between a quiet verse and a loud chorus. Fast settings act on each drum hit. Some compressors return to normal quickly after a short peak, but slowly after a long loud section. Timing also matters for a gate. It opens at one level but closes only at a lower level, so it does not switch on and off rapidly.

The type of circuit also matters. Equipment compressors usually turn the sound down with a light and a light sensor, a vacuum tube, a transistor, or a small chip. A light-based unit reacts gradually. A transistor unit can react very fast. The sound quality that a circuit adds is called character.

One compressor still changes the whole sound at once, so a loud bass note also lowers the cymbals. The solution is to divide the sound into frequency bands, each with its own compressor. That is called split-band compression. A stereo mix can also be divided into its center and its sides, each with its own compressor, and then joined again.

One compressor turning the sound down a lot can sound unnatural, so the reduction is divided. Several compressors are connected one after another, and each turns the sound down a little. That is called serial compression. Peak control can also be divided. A clipper cuts off the tops of the sharpest peaks instantly, so a limiter after it turns the sound down less.

Last, a limiter keeps the loudest peaks below a set maximum. Many limiters cannot react instantly, so a short peak can pass above that level. Some limiters delay the sound slightly to detect peaks before they reach the output. Some also work at a higher sample rate inside, to detect quick peaks better.

The flashcards explain each step. By the end, you will know how a compressor decides, how timing and circuit shape its sound, and how the reduction is divided and limited.
