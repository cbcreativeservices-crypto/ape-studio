Topic Foundations: Advanced Dynamics

In the core topic on compression, limiting and gating, you learned about the compressor. It turns the sound down when the sound goes above a set level. This topic answers the next question: how do you control exactly when, how and where it turns the sound down?

The first step is how it decides. One part of a compressor measures the sound and decides how much to turn it down. Another part does the turning down. The measuring part can read the sound before it is turned down, or the result after it. Reading the result often makes the compressor react more gently. The path that carries the sound to the measuring part is called the sidechain. So changing what it receives changes how the compressor acts. Reduce the low frequencies in the sidechain, and a loud kick drum makes the compressor turn down the whole mix less. The filter is not heard itself; it only changes when and how much the sound is turned down.

Timing then shapes the result. Slow settings make the level change gradually, such as between a quiet verse and a loud chorus. Fast settings act on each drum hit. A gate, the tool that turns down sound below a set level, also needs timing. It opens at one level but closes at a lower one, so it does not switch on and off rapidly.

Timing also depends on the type of circuit that does the turning down. Physical compressors usually use a light and a light sensor, a vacuum tube or a transistor. A light-based unit reacts gradually. A transistor unit can react very fast. The sound quality that a circuit adds is called character.

One compressor still changes the whole sound at once, so a loud bass note also lowers the cymbals. The solution is to divide the sound into frequency bands, each with its own compressor. That is called split-band compression. In the same way, a stereo mix can be divided into its center and its sides, each with its own compressor.

One compressor turning the sound down a lot can sound unnatural, so the reduction is divided. Several compressors are connected one after another, and each turns the sound down a little. That is called serial compression. Peak control can also be divided. A clipper cuts off the tops of the sharpest peaks instantly, so a limiter after it turns the sound down less.

Last, a limiter keeps the loudest peaks below a set maximum. A short peak can pass before a limiter reacts, so some limiters delay the sound slightly to detect peaks early. Some also measure the sound more often inside, to find peaks between the normal measurements.

The flashcards explain each step. By the end, you will know how a compressor decides, how timing and circuit shape its sound, and how the reduction is divided and limited.
