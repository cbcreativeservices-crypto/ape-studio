2. How this was done

  1. Every existing lab (01_LABS), every lab requirement (02), every credential (03) and every topic (04) was read. Because the data links labs to credentials but not to topics, each lab was mapped by hand to the topics it teaches directly or touches partly (appendix A).
  2. Each topic was then marked covered, partial or none. A credential's gaps are its topics with no direct lab.
  3. Gaps were grouped into 27 subject areas and ranked (section 1). Each area was researched against standards, manufacturer documentation and textbooks; sources are listed in each proposal.
  4. Proposals favour a few excellent labs over many thin ones. Where an existing lab is the natural home, an extension is proposed instead of a new lab.
  5. Every proposal was checked against the brief's rules: accuracy before beauty; labs never block navigation; every lab ends on a "what's left" screen; credit is never taken away; display pinned at the top, controls at the bottom, display text at least 9 pt; popups, never pulldowns; the same on iPhone, iPad and Android; miking and mixing guidance given as starting points; no "coming soon" labs; the visual charter (06).
  6. Every gs number and every credential slug in this report was checked by script against the catalogue export: none are invented.

How to read a proposal. "Required by" means the proposal should become a graduation requirement for that credential, with the completion rule shown. "Relevant to" means the lab supports the credential but should not be required. Learning outcomes are written as knowledge ("Knows that…"), as the brief asks.

The gap it fills

Networked audio is now how most venues, churches, conference centres and broadcast plants move audio, and three topics cover it (gs3320, gs3330, gs4720), yet no lab touches it. Two certificates and one program have no lab at all, and seven large programs include all three topics.

Purpose (in the learner's words)

"Every venue I walk into runs audio over a network. When the stage box disappears, a channel goes silent or the clock turns red, I want to understand what the system is telling me, not just reboot things and hope."

Learning outcomes

  • Knows that a networked-audio system is two layers, the network and the audio on top of it, and can explain which kind of fault belongs to which layer.
  • Knows what a subscription is, how unicast and multicast flows differ, and why a device can run out of flows before it runs out of channels.
  • Knows how one device becomes the clock leader, what "preferred leader" and "sync to external" change, and why two leaders at once mean trouble.
  • Understands latency as a safety window set by the number of switch hops, and why the receiver's setting is the one that wins (the higher of the two).
  • Knows the switch settings that audio depends on (quality of service with clock packets first, energy-saving Ethernet switched off, multicast filtering) and why office defaults can break a show.
  • Understands how primary and secondary networks give redundancy, and why joining them together is a serious mistake.
  • Knows what AES67 is for: letting different manufacturers' devices exchange audio. Knows what it needs: PTP version 2, multicast streams and session announcements.

Structure

The rack layout throughout: the network map display is pinned at the top (devices as illustrated boxes, cables, switches, with a clock icon on the leader and flow lines that light up), and the controls are docked at the bottom (popups for every choice, never pull-down menus).

  1. The network under the audio. Cables, switch ports, IP addresses and subnets drawn as a street map. Display: the map with address labels. Controls: add device, plug into a port, an "automatic or fixed address" popup.
  2. Devices, channels and subscriptions. A routing grid (transmit channels across, receive channels down) that is the same idea as a patchbay. Display: map plus a flow counter for each device. Controls: tap a cell to subscribe; a popup to make a multicast flow.
  3. Clocks. Leader election run live. Display: a clock-status panel (leader, follower, offset histogram) beside the map. Controls: Preferred Leader on or off, Sync to External, unplug the leader.
  4. Latency and the hop budget. Display: a packet-arrival histogram against the latency window (green, then amber near the edge, then red dropouts). Controls: latency popup (0.25 / 0.5 / 1 / 2 / 5 ms), add switches between devices, set a port to 100 Mb.
  5. Switch settings that matter. Display: switch queues with packets coloured by type (clock, audio, other traffic). Controls: quality of service on or off, strict priority, energy-saving Ethernet, multicast filtering (IGMP snooping) with or without a querier, a VLAN popup.
  6. Redundancy and talking to other systems. Display: two colour-coded networks. Controls: Redundant or Switched mode popup, cable pulls, AES67 mode, a PTP version 2 clock source.
  7. Fault bench. Twelve realistic faults, each found by reading the displays first: a device on the wrong subnet; a flow limit reached; two clock leaders after a multicast filter misconfiguration; an energy-saving switch causing clicks; latency too low for the hop count; secondary joined to primary; a sample-rate mismatch blocking a subscription; a 100 Mb port forcing 1 ms; an AES67 stream not visible because announcements are off; a channel subscribed twice by mistake; an unmanaged switch flooding multicast; an external word clock not set as leader.

What the learner does

  • Builds a 32-input stage box, front-of-house console, monitor console, recorder and amplifier system, then routes it, and is told what each route costs in flows and bandwidth.
  • Predicts which device will become clock leader, then sees it happen, then forces a different one and explains why.
  • Chooses a latency for a network of a given size and checks the histogram. A choice that is too tight shows amber, then dropouts, never a silent failure.
  • Sets up a switch from a "factory office settings" state, and sees and hears (clicks and dropouts on a test signal) what each wrong setting does.
  • Works the fault bench, where every option has an explanation and any reasonable fix is accepted.
  • Listening: a music loop through the simulated network, so learners hear dropouts, clicks from clock problems, and the silence of a failed subscription.

Real-world accuracy notes

  • Clock packets take top priority: on Dante hardware, time-critical PTP is DSCP 56 (CS7), audio and PTP are 46 (EF), and 8 (CS1) is reserved. Dante software applies no DSCP marks. Switches need DiffServ quality of service with strict priority and four queues, and energy-saving Ethernet must be off.
  • Typical latency by hop count, which the lab offers as suggested starting points: up to 3 hops 0.25 ms, up to 5 hops 0.5 ms, up to 10 hops 1 ms, up to 20 hops 2 ms, and beyond that (or when there are problems) 5 ms. The higher of the transmitter's and receiver's settings is the one used. Multicast flows use at least 1 ms, and so does a 100 Mb port.
  • Unicast flows usually carry up to 4 channels and multicast flows up to 8, but flow limits depend on the device's chipset (for example, 16 flows on small devices and 32 or more on large ones). The lab shows the limit from the device's own profile rather than one universal number.
  • Leader election, in order: Preferred Leader, then devices set to sync to an external clock, then gigabit over 100 Mb, then the lowest MAC address. Several leaders at once often point to a multicast-filter (IGMP) problem.
  • Redundancy needs physically separate primary and secondary networks, with each device set to Redundant mode before the secondary is connected.
  • AES67: PTPv2, multicast only, discovery by SAP when AES67 mode is on, and Dante devices bridge PTPv1 and PTPv2.
  • Must not teach: that any network switch will do; that more latency means worse sound (it only adds delay); that multicast is always better; that a red clock means a bad cable.

Purpose (in the learner's words)

"Twenty wireless mics and eight in-ear packs have to work all night in a room full of phones, LED walls and TV transmitters. I want to know how a frequency plan is built and why a pack drops out, instead of swapping batteries and praying."

Learning outcomes

  • Knows that legal wireless-microphone spectrum depends on the country and changes over time, and can explain the difference between licensed and licence-free use.
  • Understands intermodulation: two transmitters produce new frequencies (for example 2f1 − f2), and these limit how many systems fit in a band.
  • Knows what a spectrum scan shows (TV channels, the noise floor, interference) and how a frequency plan is built around it.
  • Understands why antenna placement, line of sight, the human body and cable loss matter more than transmitter power.
  • Knows the difference between audio gain and RF level, and what squelch and diversity do.
  • Knows the signs of a battery, interference, intermodulation or dropout fault from receiver readings.

Structure

Display (pinned): a spectrum analyser across the top (frequency across, level up), with markers for each wireless channel, shaded TV channels, and intermodulation products drawn as thin markers in a warning colour with a dashed outline (not colour alone). Controls (docked): frequency entry with a stepper, a region popup and system-type popups.

  1. The spectrum you are allowed to use. Region popup: United States (FCC), United Kingdom (Ofcom), or "other — check your national regulator". The display shades licensed, licence-free and forbidden ranges with their power limits.
  2. Scanning a venue. Load a venue (an arena, a theatre, a church, a hotel ballroom) and see its scan. Learners name what they see: TV carriers, a noisy LED wall, a raised noise floor.
  3. Frequency coordination. Place channels one by one; the lab draws the 3rd- and 5th-order intermodulation products live and flags any within 250 kHz of a carrier. The task is to fit the cast list.
  4. Antennas and placement. Plan view of the venue with receive antennas, paddles or omnis, and cable runs. A coverage heat map changes with antenna height, line of sight, the body blocking the signal, coax length (with a loss readout) and the distance between in-ear transmit antennas and mic receive antennas.
  5. Gain, squelch, power and batteries. A receiver front panel showing RF level (RSSI), audio meter, diversity indicator and squelch. Learners set audio gain for a shouting singer and a quiet presenter, and see why raising RF power does not fix an audio-gain problem.
  6. Show day. A 20-minute show compressed to a few minutes. Faults appear: a battery fading, an actor walking behind a steel wall, a new TV transmitter, an intermodulation hit when the in-ears come on. The learner diagnoses each from the readings.

What the learner does

  • Builds a legal plan for a stated show (for example 16 mics and 6 stereo in-ears) in the chosen region, and gets feedback on every intermodulation collision.
  • Chooses antenna positions and justifies them. Any position that achieves coverage is accepted.
  • Diagnoses six show-day faults, each answer explained.
  • Listening: short clips of a clean wireless signal, a squelch tail, an RF dropout, intermodulation interference (a chirp), and a low-battery fade.

Real-world accuracy notes

  • United States: wireless mics may use TV channels 2–36 (470–608 MHz in UHF) and 614–616 MHz (licence-free, 20 mW EIRP). The 600 MHz duplex gap is 653–657 MHz for licensed users (20 mW) and 657–663 MHz licence-free (20 mW). Licence-free TV-band use is limited to 50 mW EIRP and licensed Part 74 UHF to 250 mW. Other licence-free bands are 902–928 MHz, 1920–1930 MHz and 2.4 GHz. Use of the 600 MHz service band ended in July 2020.
  • United Kingdom: licence-exempt bands are 173.8–175 MHz, 863–865 MHz and 2400–2483.5 MHz; Channel 38 (606.5–613.5 MHz) needs a shared licence; interleaved UHF needs a site licence.
  • Third-order intermodulation products of f1 and f2 fall at 2f1 − f2 and 2f2 − f1 (for example, 590 and 595 MHz produce 585 and 600 MHz). Intermodulation power rises with the square of carrier power and falls with the square of the distance between transmitters. Three transmitters can make six in-band 3rd-order and three 5th-order products. A common margin is 250 kHz between any 3rd-order product and any carrier.
  • A quarter-wave antenna at 600 MHz is about 12.5 cm long (λ = c/f ≈ 0.5 m), and the lab computes it rather than stating it.
  • Must not teach: that more transmitter power is the fix; that spectrum rules are the same everywhere; that a frequency list from last year's tour is legal this year.

Purpose (in the learner's words)

Learning outcomes

Purpose (in the learner's words)

"I want to measure a room and a PA the way consultants and system techs do: get an impulse response, read the reverb time, align the subs, and know when the screen is lying to me."

Learning outcomes

  • Knows the parts of a measurement chain (calibrated microphone, interface, reference loopback) and why the reference signal matters.
  • Understands how a sine sweep turns into an impulse response, and why distortion separates out from it.
  • Understands how reverberation time is read from the decay (T20, T30, EDT), why background noise spoils the tail, and why each seat is kept separate.
  • Understands magnitude, phase and coherence in a transfer function, and why low coherence means "do not EQ this".
  • Knows how a delay finder works and why alignment is then checked on the phase trace.
  • Knows what EQ can fix (a stable, coherent response problem) and what it cannot (reflections, comb filtering, coverage gaps).

Structure

Display: a stacked analyser panel (impulse response with time axis; magnitude with log frequency; phase; coherence), each read from one value through one scale, with a "freeze trace" button to compare. Controls: popups for averaging, smoothing, window and FFT size.

  1. The measurement chain. Connect microphone, interface, loopback and speaker; a calibrator check with a pass/fail readout.
  2. Impulse response. Fire a sweep, see the deconvolution produce the impulse response, and see harmonic distortion appear before the main peak. Choose sweep length and level for good signal-to-noise.
  3. Room decay. The energy decay curve from backward integration; T20 and T30 lines fitted live; a noise floor slider shows the curve bending; EDT, C50 and C80 shown with labels; several seats kept separate.
  4. Transfer function. Live music as the source; magnitude, phase and coherence update. Toggle between power averaging and vector averaging and watch the late reflections behave differently.
  5. Delay and alignment. Delay finder on a main and a subwoofer; insert delay; verify on phase and on the summed response.
  6. Tuning a system. A venue model; measure several positions; decide where EQ helps and where moving or aiming a speaker is the better fix. The lab refuses to apply EQ in a band with very low coherence and explains why.

What the learner does

  • Measures three rooms (a studio, a church, a gym) and reports T30 per octave band from several seats.
  • Spots three bad measurements (clipped sweep, missing reference, noise-dominated tail) and says why each cannot be trusted.
  • Aligns a main and a sub so that they add at the crossover, then confirms it.
  • Proposes an EQ for a PA and has it scored on reasoning (fixing coherent, broad problems), not on matching a hidden answer.
  • Listening: each room's impulse response convolved with speech and with a drum loop (auralisation), so the reverb-time numbers can be heard.

Real-world accuracy notes

  • ISO 3382: T20 uses the decay from −5 to −25 dB and T30 from −5 to −35 dB, each extrapolated to 60 dB. EDT uses 0 to −10 dB. Just-noticeable differences are about 5% for T20, T30 and EDT and 1 dB for C50 and C80.
  • Backward integration (the Schroeder method) of a noisy impulse response overestimates decay unless the noise is compensated or the response truncated. The lab shows this effect instead of hiding it.
  • An exponential sweep places harmonic distortion before the linear impulse response after deconvolution (Farina's method).
  • Power (RMS) averaging is blind to arrival time, while vector averaging suppresses late and uncorrelated energy. Coherence near zero means the measurement has no causal relationship to the source at that frequency.
  • Every number is labelled measured, calculated or estimated, as in the Room Design lab, and calculations call the app's calculator functions (the source of truth).
  • Must not teach: that a flat analyser trace means it sounds right; that EQ can remove a reflection; that one microphone position describes a room.

Purpose (in the learner's words)

"I've been asked to check whether a venue, a factory or a festival breaks the noise limit. I need to set the meter up the way the rules say, add the numbers correctly, and write a conclusion that stands up."

Learning outcomes

  • Knows what A, C and Z frequency weighting and Fast, Slow and Impulse time weighting do, and which ones the common rules ask for.
  • Understands why decibels are added on an energy basis (two equal sources give +3 dB, not double) and how background noise is corrected for.
  • Understands Leq, Lmax, Lpeak, L10 and L90, and the evening and night penalties in Lden.
  • Knows why a meter is calibrated before and after every measurement, and what class 1 and class 2 mean.
  • Understands how a noise survey is planned (positions, heights, weather, duration) and why a conclusion must be no bigger than the evidence.

Structure

Display: an illustrated sound level meter with a real readout and a time-history strip chart below it (level over a day, with period bands shaded). Controls: weighting, time-weighting and metric popups; a calibrator button.

  2. Decibel arithmetic. Add and subtract levels, average a day, correct for background noise, and compare point and line sources over distance. Every result is computed by the app's calculator; impossible inputs (background louder than total) are refused with an explanation.
  3. Metrics. One recorded day of road, festival and plant noise; compute LAeq, LAFmax, LCpeak, L10, L90, Lden and Ldn, and see what each rewards or hides.
  4. Survey and assessment. Plan positions and heights, note the weather, and write a short conclusion from a choice of statements, each graded for whether it overstates the evidence. A simple noise map shows contours from a source.
  5. Noise at work: measuring dose. Dosimeter set-up, exchange rate (3 dB or 5 dB), criterion level; calculate a worker's dose from a shift log.
  6. Reducing noise. Try source changes (quieter machine, schedule), path changes (barrier height, distance) and receiver changes; see the predicted level change.

What the learner does

  • Calibrates, measures and reports a festival boundary reading against a stated limit.
  • Spots three bad readings: no calibration check, wind noise, a reflection off a wall behind the meter.
  • Computes a worker's dose under two rule sets and explains why they differ.
  • Listening: the same recording played, with the weighted levels shown, to hear why A-weighting under-reads bass-heavy music.

Real-world accuracy notes

  • IEC 61672-1:2013 covers class 1 and class 2 meters, with A weighting mandatory; F is 125 ms, S is 1 s and I is 35 ms. Class 1 tolerance is about ±1.1 dB at 1 kHz, class 2 about ±1.4 dB. Calibrators (IEC 60942) give 94 dB (1 Pa) or 114 dB at 1 kHz.
  • Lden (EU Directive 2002/49/EC): day 07–19 with no penalty, evening 19–23 +5 dB, night 23–07 +10 dB; member states may shift the hours. Reporting thresholds are Lden 55 and Lnight 50.
  • Workplace limits: OSHA PEL is 90 dBA over 8 hours with a 5 dB exchange rate (action level 85). The NIOSH REL is 85 dBA with a 3 dB exchange rate (88 dBA for 4 hours, 94 dBA for 1 hour, 100 dBA for 15 minutes). EU Directive 2003/10/EC sets action values of 80 and 85 dB(A) with peaks of 135 and 137 dB(C), and a limit of 87 dB(A) and 140 dB(C), where only the limit takes hearing protection into account.
  • Point source: −6 dB per doubling of distance; line source: −3 dB, both in free field. The lab labels these "ideal".
  • Must not teach: that dB(A) is "how loud it sounds"; that a phone app replaces a class 1 meter for compliance; that a 6 dB louder reading means "twice as loud".

  • Must not teach: that a loudness target is a quality goal; that −14 LUFS is "the standard for everything"; that LUFS is the same as dBFS RMS.

  5. Mics on actors and line-by-line mixing. Place a mic at the hairline, ear or cheek (the sound changes with placement); then mix a musical number line by line from a script, using scenes and VCA groups.
  6. Keeping the voice on the actor. A plan view with the actor, the main speakers and front fills; adjust delay and level; a localisation map shows where most of the audience hears the voice coming from.
  7. The orchestra pit. Lay out a pit band; conductor video, click and monitoring; balance pit and stage.

What the learner does

  • Calls and takes cues in a compressed scene, and gets feedback on timing (early, on time, late) with reasons.
  • Builds a 20-cue sound plot for a scene from choices of effects and fades.
  • Mixes a two-actor duet line by line, with points for muting unused mics, not for matching a hidden fader move.
  • Sets delay so that the voice stays on stage for the stalls, then explains the trade-off for the balcony.
  • Listening: a voice localised to the speaker versus to the actor, comb filtering when two open actor mics are close, and an actor's mic at three placements.

Real-world accuracy notes

  • SMPTE ST 12 frame rates: 23.976 (24/1.001), 24, 25, 29.97 (30/1.001; drop or non-drop) and 30. Drop frame skips frame numbers 00 and 01 every minute except every tenth minute (18 of every 18,000 numbers); an hour of 29.97 non-drop timecode runs 3.6 s behind the wall clock. MTC sends quarter-frame messages, four per frame.
  • Precedence effect: a delayed copy arriving within about 5–30 ms can be up to about 10 dB louder without moving the image; the effect breaks down if it is about 15 dB louder, and the echo threshold depends on the signal. A theatre delay of about 10–20 ms with level offset is a common starting point. The lab presents this as a starting point with no single right answer.
  • Line-by-line mixing keeps one actor mic open at a time where possible, to avoid comb filtering and to protect gain before feedback (each doubling of open mics costs 3 dB).
  • Must not teach: a single correct mic position; that more delay is always better; that timecode is "the same everywhere".

Purpose (in the learner's words)

"Scientists, forensic examiners and sound designers all stare at spectrograms. I want to set one up on purpose and know when the picture is lying."

Learning outcomes

  • Understands how an FFT turns a block of samples into frequency bins, and why bin width is the sample rate divided by the FFT size.
  • Understands the time-versus-frequency trade-off: short windows show when, long windows show which frequency.
  • Knows what spectral leakage is, and why window shape trades a narrow main lobe for low side lobes.
  • Knows that zero-padding makes a smoother-looking spectrum but does not separate two close frequencies — only more signal does.
  • Understands how overlap, colour scale and dynamic range change the look of a spectrogram without changing the sound.
  • Knows the common features (harmonics, formants, transients, hum lines, clicks, reverberation tails) and common display artefacts.
  • Knows how very slow signals (seismic, infrasound) and very high ones (bats, ultrasound) are brought into view.

Structure

Display: a spectrogram (time across, frequency up, level by the house colour scale with a level legend), and below it the single-frame spectrum at the cursor and the window shape. Axis scales can be linear or logarithmic.

  1. From waveform to spectrum. One block, one FFT; change FFT size and watch the bins; two tones close together merge or separate.
  2. Windows and leakage. Rectangular, Hann, Blackman and Kaiser (with β); a weak tone hides under the side lobes of a strong one until the window changes; the zero-padding test.
  3. The spectrogram. Window length, hop/overlap, colour range and floor, linear or log frequency axis; the same chirp and drum loop at four settings.
  4. Reading real recordings. Speech, a guitar, a car pass-by, a room tail, hum and a click; tag each feature; a "trap" set where the feature is a display artefact.
  5. Slow and fast signals. Seismic and infrasound records (long windows, decimation, time compression) and a bat or ultrasonic call (high sample rate).
  6. Wildlife and soundscapes. Bird, whale and frog choruses; frequency bands of species; why soundscape indices need care.

What the learner does

  • Chooses settings to separate two whistles 20 Hz apart, then to time-stamp a drum hit to within 5 ms, and explains why one setting cannot do both.
  • Identifies ten features on real spectrograms, and three artefacts.
  • Measures a formant, a hum frequency, a call's frequency sweep and a reverb tail from the display.
  • Listening: every display has its audio; slowed-down and sped-up versions in module 5.

Real-world accuracy notes

  • Bin width = fs/N, and the window lasts N/fs. For example, at 44.1 kHz an error under 5 Hz needs N of at least 4,410, which is about 0.1 s. The app calculates these values; it does not store them.
  • Window shape trades main-lobe width against side-lobe level; the Kaiser β parameter sets the trade-off. Zero-padding interpolates but does not improve resolution (MIT 6.300 notes). Some popular tutorials get this wrong, and the lab must not repeat the error.
  • Colour maps must be readable without relying on colour (contours or level readout on tap), per the charter.
  • Seismic audification: speeding up 500 times maps 0.5–40 Hz to 250 Hz–20 kHz (the IRIS SeisSound method).
  • Must not teach: that a spectrogram is "the sound"; that more FFT points is always better; that colour intensity is loudness as heard.

  • Knows the common components and how their values are marked (resistor colour code, capacitor marking, E-series, tolerance), and how each behaves in an audio circuit.
  • Knows what each bench instrument shows (multimeter functions, oscilloscope time and voltage settings, signal generator, audio analyser) and how to use it safely.
  • Knows what diodes, transistors and op-amps do in audio circuits: gain, bias, headroom and clipping.
  • Knows the standard circuit blocks: gain stage, filter, balanced input and output, power-supply regulation, and the mic preamp with its noise floor.
  • Understands a systematic fault-finding method (signal tracing, half-splitting, substitution) and the typical component faults it finds.
  • Knows what acceptable and defective solder joints look like, and the classes of the inspection standard.
  • Knows how a design becomes a product: PCB assembly, through-hole and surface mount, wiring harnesses, test and quality control.

Structure

Display: an illustrated circuit board with a schematic overlay (junctions drawn only where the model connects), and an instrument docked beside it: a multimeter readout or an oscilloscope trace driven by the circuit model.

  1. Parts on the bench. Identify and read values; build a resistor divider and an RC filter, then measure them.
  2. Meters and scope. Multimeter on V, I, Ω, continuity and diode; scope timebase, volts per division and trigger; generator; THD+N on an audio analyser.
  3. Active devices. Diode, BJT and FET stages, op-amp gain; push the input and watch clipping appear on the scope.
  4. Circuit blocks. Gain stage, tone control, balanced I/O, regulated supply, and the mic preamp (gain, input impedance, equivalent input noise).
  5. Fault-finding. A mixer channel with a hidden fault; follow the signal stage by stage with probes; faults include a leaky coupling capacitor, a dead op-amp, a cracked joint, a scratchy potentiometer and an open supply rail. Safety gate: discharge capacitors, mains isolation, and valve high voltage (the lab refuses unsafe probing orders).
  6. Soldering and joint inspection. Photo-realistic joint gallery to classify (good, cold, insufficient, excess, bridged, disturbed, lifted pad); temperature, lead-free versus leaded; desoldering.
  7. From prototype to production. PCB assembly flow, surface-mount versus through-hole, ESD, harnesses, end-of-line test, a quality-control sampling exercise.

What the learner does

  • Finds five faults in three devices (a mixer channel, a DI box, a pedal) using the fewest measurements, and is scored on method, not speed.
  • Classifies 30 solder joints, each with an explanation.
  • Calculates and checks a mic preamp's noise against the thermal-noise limit.
  • Listening: each fault's symptom (crackle, distortion, hum, silence) before diagnosis.

Real-world accuracy notes

  • The circuit model must be correct electronics (for example, op-amp rails limit swing; coupling-capacitor leakage shifts DC bias), and any equation shared with the calculators calls them.
  • Thermal noise, V² = 4kTRB: a 150 Ω source over 20 kHz is about −131 dBu (200 Ω about −129.6 dBu). Equivalent input noise is quoted with source resistance, bandwidth and weighting. An open input is noisier than one with a microphone connected.
  • IPC-A-610 (revision J current in 2026) defines acceptability classes 1–3 and conditions (target, acceptable, process indicator, defect); J-STD-001 covers the soldering process. The gallery uses original photographs.
  • Safety: mains and valve high-voltage hazards; the lab teaches recognition, not live work.
  • Must not teach: that a component "looks fine" means it is; that unplugged gear is safe (capacitors hold charge); that more solder is a better joint.

Purpose (in the learner's words)

"I want to know what's inside a loudspeaker, why one box goes deeper than another, what a horn really does, and how the numbers on a spec sheet were measured."

Learning outcomes

  • Understands the motor (magnet, voice coil, Bl), the suspension and the main Thiele–Small parameters (fs, Qts, Vas), and what each predicts.
  • Understands how sealed, vented and horn-loaded designs trade size, efficiency and bass extension, and what a port and baffle step do.
  • Understands how a horn or waveguide controls directivity, and why mouth size limits control at low frequencies.
  • Understands crossover slopes and alignments (Butterworth versus Linkwitz–Riley), driver polarity and timing, and lobing.
  • Knows how a loudspeaker is measured (impedance curve, sensitivity, gated or ground-plane response, polar plots, distortion) and how to read a datasheet critically.

Structure

Display: a cut-away loudspeaker illustration with the cone moving in time with the graph (the house rule), and a response and impedance graph below it; polar balloons in module 6.

  1. Driver types and the motor. Cut-aways of each type; Bl, voice coil and suspension; T/S parameters introduced one at a time with sliders.
  2. Measuring a driver. Free-air impedance curve; find fs and Qts from it; the added-mass idea for Vas; sensitivity at 1 W/1 m versus 2.83 V/1 m.
  3. Boxes. Sealed (choose Qtc), vented (tuning, port length, port air speed warning), bandpass; baffle step from cabinet width; excursion limit shown against Xmax.
  4. Horns and waveguides. Throat, flare and mouth; beamwidth versus frequency; a compression driver on a horn; mouth size versus pattern control.
  5. Crossovers. Two-way and three-way; passive versus active; Butterworth versus Linkwitz–Riley at several orders; polarity and delay; the vertical lobe drawn as drivers are moved apart.
  6. The finished system on the bench. Gated quasi-anechoic and ground-plane methods, polar measurement, distortion and maximum SPL; read three real-style datasheets and spot the optimistic one.

What the learner does

  • Designs a two-way bookshelf speaker from a driver list to a stated target (box size, low-frequency extension), and the model reports where it falls short.
  • Chooses a crossover that sums flat on axis, and explains what happens off axis.
  • Spots three common datasheet tricks (sensitivity at 2.83 V into 4 Ω, response with ±10 dB tolerance, peak power).
  • Listening: a modelled sealed versus vented response on music, crossover phase errors (a dip at crossover), port chuffing at high level (pre-rendered).

Real-world accuracy notes

  • Qts = Qes·Qms/(Qes + Qms). Sealed: fc = fs·Qtc/Qts and Vb = Vas/((Qtc/Qts)² − 1); Qtc 0.707 is a Butterworth (maximally flat) alignment. Efficiency bandwidth product EBP = fs/Qes. The rule of thumb (Qts below about 0.4 suits a vented box, 0.4–0.7 a sealed one) has known exceptions, and the lab says so.
  • Linkwitz–Riley: two cascaded Butterworth sections; each output is −6 dB at the crossover point and the outputs sum flat with no lobing error. Butterworth sums with a +3 dB peak and a tilted lobe. LR2 needs one driver's polarity inverted.
  • Baffle step is about a 6 dB rise from low to high frequencies around the frequency where the wavelength is comparable to the baffle width (an ideal model; real cabinets show diffraction ripple).
  • Calculations call the app's calculators; impossible designs (for example, a negative box volume when Qts > Qtc) are refused with an explanation.
  • Must not teach: that bigger always means deeper bass; that crossover slope alone sets "phase coherence"; that a sensitivity figure is comparable without its drive condition.

  • Speeds: air about 343 m/s, water about 1,480 m/s, seawater about 1,500 m/s, soft tissue about 1,540 m/s (the scanner assumption); longitudinal waves in steel about 5,900 m/s. Air at about 400 rayl versus tissue at about 1.6 MRayl gives near-total reflection.

  • FDA Track 3 limits: derated ISPTA of 720 mW/cm² or less and MI of 1.9 or less (lower for the eye); the AIUM's ALARA principle applies.
  • Sonar: active SNR = SL − 2TL + TS − (NL − AG); spherical spreading TL = 20 log R (60 dB at 1 km, 80 dB at 10 km). Water decibels use a 1 µPa reference versus 20 µPa in air (a 26 dB offset); equal intensities differ by about 61.5 dB, and even that "conversion" misleads for biological effects.
  • Must not teach: that the app or lab is diagnostic; that air and water decibels compare directly; that higher frequency is always better.
