# Drum Overhead Miking Technique
Pro Audio Training Academy   Drum Kit Cross Reference   Research checkpoint 5
Drum overheads are a microphone perspective on the whole kit, not a separate instrument. They capture membranophones such as snare, kick, and toms alongside idiophones such as hi-hat, ride, and crash cymbals. This lesson teaches how to choose one overhead or a stereo array for studio and live sound, balance the kit by placement and aim, and combine the result with close microphones. It distinguishes documented geometry from practical starting choices and calls for checks with the actual player, room, sound system, and equipment. No audio examples are included. [1‑5]
# Learning goals
A learner should identify the overheads' role as either a principal kit image or a cymbal-focused contribution; choose mono or stereo according to the intended audience and output; explain coincident X/Y, near-coincident ORTF, spaced A/B, and the Glyn Johns overhead-and-side technique, with Mid-Side or Recorderman as optional advanced branches; place capsules and mounts safely; adjust height, front-to-back position, aim, and width deliberately; use snare distance as a helpful reference without treating it as a complete phase solution; check stereo imaging and mono summation; and decide what overhead coverage is useful in a live rig.
# Hear the source before choosing geometry
Ask the drummer to play representative grooves and fills at performance intensity, including every cymbal, hi-hat foot pattern, snare accent, and tom passage. Listen to the balance in the room and from the intended mic location. Change tuning, cymbals, or playing only with the player's involvement. A pair cannot independently rebalance instruments it already combined into each microphone. RØDE describes changing placement to favor kit components when one or two more distant microphones are used. DPA distinguishes a few microphones representing a natural ensemble from close mics intended for individual control. [2, 3]
Decide whether the overheads are a principal picture of drums and cymbals, a cymbal-focused supplement to close mics, or something in between. Shure's engineer Mike Major explicitly treats overheads as more than cymbal microphones and starts with the kit balance, while Shure's live guide also documents a cymbal-oriented overhead with low-frequency roll-off. Neither intent is universal. A cymbal-heavy drummer, low ceiling, reflective wall, loud stage, or nearby vocal mic can change the best placement. [1, 4]
# Safe mounting and exposure
Have the drummer stop before moving stands or microphones. Set booms and mounts so the complete stick path, raised cymbal edge, player posture, and full kit motion remain clear. Use stands, clamps, stereo bars, counterweights or ballast approved for the actual load, with boom and legs secured. Do not balance a heavy boom precariously above a player. An overhead position is not permission to suspend microphones from building structures; any flown hardware or rigging must follow venue rules and qualified personnel. Route cables with strain relief away from sticks, pedals, walkways, and stand adjustment points. Confirm that an adjusted stand remains stable through the performance. DPA cautions that closely stacked X/Y microphones should not touch because mechanical noise can result. [5]
Repeated drum soundchecks can create hazardous human exposure. NIOSH recommends an 85 dBA eight-hour time-weighted limit and halves recommended exposure duration per 3 dBA increase. This is human exposure guidance, not a close microphone's maximum-SPL rating. Limit unnecessary loud repetitions and follow applicable hearing and venue procedures. Use the selected microphone and console manuals for power, pad, and gain: mute or lower relevant outputs before cable or phantom changes, check that monitor and aux feeds are also controlled, and set gain on strongest intended playing. A mic pad does not repair distortion that already occurs before the pad. [10, 11]
# Mono and stereo options
Technique
Geometry and useful starting role
Main tradeoff and verification
One overhead
One stable mic above or slightly behind the kit aimed for the intended drum/cymbal balance. Shure's live guide illustrates a center pickup about 1 ft above the drummer's head; this is an example, not a clearance rule. [4]
No overhead stereo width. Adjust height, aim, and front/back position until the whole kit and any close mics work together.
X/Y coincident
Two directional capsules as close to coincident as physically possible, angled apart without touching. A 90° cardioid arrangement is one common example; angle and aim are adjustable. [5, 6]
Typically a stable, relatively compact stereo image and a comparatively reliable mono sum within the pair. This does not guarantee good interaction with close mics.
ORTF near-coincident
Two cardioid capsules about 17 cm apart and 110° apart, mounted on a suitable stereo bar. [6]
More width and time difference than X/Y, with more potential mono coloration. Test the actual kit and room rather than assuming its textbook geometry fits every placement.
A/B spaced pair
Two separately positioned capsules across the kit; directional or omni mics can be used. [2, 5, 6]
Can create a wide image and more adjustable side coverage; differences in arrival time can color the mono sum and individual drums. Check capsule-to-snare distances and audition the whole kit in mono.
Glyn Johns overhead and side
One mic above the snare/central kit, one beside or just beyond the floor tom aimed across toward the snare; kick and snare spot mics commonly complete a four-mic setup. Match the two main mics' acoustic-center distances to the same snare reference as a starting check. [12–14]
An intentionally asymmetric whole-kit image with few mics. The side mic is not an ordinary second high overhead. Evaluate floor tom, hi-hat, cymbal distribution, room, mono sum, and spot-mic interactions. See the full procedure below.
Mid-Side optional extension
A forward-facing Mid mic with a side-facing figure-eight capsule at nearly the same point; decode to left/right through a proper console or software matrix. [6, 7]
Width can be adjusted, but side pickup can emphasize room sound. Raw Mid and Side feeds are not already conventional left/right channels. Confirm matrix and mono sum.
Recorderman optional extension
Shure's example uses one cardioid above the snare and one by the drummer's right shoulder, equidistant from snare and kick by its specific method. [6]
Not simply two symmetric overheads and not a guarantee for tom/cymbal mono compatibility. Its cited 32-inch dimension is a described example, never a reason to violate player clearance.

Recorderman and Glyn Johns have conceptual similarities but different second-mic positions and distinct measurement conventions in the cited descriptions: the former puts the second mic by the drummer's shoulder and explicitly references both snare and kick; the latter puts it beside the floor tom and conventionally measures equal distance to the snare. Do not interchange their names or claim either geometry guarantees a coherent entire kit. If an app presents Mid-Side, make the encoding and decoding explicit: with matched gains, the decoded channels are proportional to Mid plus Side and Mid minus Side; when summed to mono, the Side contributions cancel. Check the actual matrix and wiring instead of instructing novices to modify microphone cables. [6, 7, 12–14]
# Glyn Johns overhead-and-side technique
The name is **Glyn Johns**. This section teaches the whole-kit technique here because its two main microphones establish the image; the kick and snare spots provide optional or customary additional control. Sources variously describe three mics (overhead, side, kick) or four (add snare), and disagree on precise height and fixed panning. A documented historical account describes two condensers plus kick and snare, one above the snare and one over the floor-tom side; MusicTech's practitioner guide describes the three-or-four-mic variant. These descriptions are practical families of setups, not an authenticated single immutable recipe from Johns. [12–14]
Role
Initial position and purpose
Check before proceeding
Main overhead
Secure one mic above the snare region, aimed down toward the snare or desired center of the kit. Roughly 40 in (about 1 m) from the snare is an example in one account, not a required height or safe clearance. [12]
Check the player's highest sticks, cymbal travel, mount security, ceiling, and whether this mic alone gives a useful kit balance.
Floor-tom-side mic
Put a second mic outside the drummer's reach near or just beyond the floor tom, aimed across the kit toward the snare/hi-hat region. A guide describes about 6 in (15 cm) above the floor-tom rim, while another account gives a side perspective with the snare as the main aim; actual clearance and stroke path take priority. [12–14]
Measure from each capsule's acoustic center to the **same snare strike reference** while the drummer is stopped. Move/aim the side mic to approximate the overhead's snare distance without putting it in a stick, hand, cymbal, or stand path. Then check floor tom and cymbal balance.
Kick and optional snare
Add close spots only when the part or reinforcement needs their independent weight and articulation. Four total mics is a common documented form; a three-mic variant omits the close snare. [12, 13]
Bring these up one by one after the two main mics, compare mono tone and polarity options at intended levels, and do not assume equal overhead-to-snare distance also aligns the spots.

Set and verify. With the player stopped, choose a sturdy position for the main overhead, secure it, and identify a repeatable snare reference without fastening tape or string to the drum without permission. Position the side mic across the floor tom and measure both acoustic-center-to-snare distances with a non-contact tape or string reference. Adjust within the player's safe performance envelope, then have the drummer play the whole passage at intended dynamics. Listen to both microphones individually, together in mono, and finally at modest, deliberate stereo positions; check whether kick, snare, toms, hi-hat, ride, and crashes retain the desired balance. Some accounts pan the overhead partway to one side and the side mic wider; there is no mandatory left/right or hard-pan setting, especially if stage or audience layout differs. Add kick and snare as needed, one at a time. Repeat the mono and clearance checks after every material move. [12–14]
Why measure, and what it cannot promise. Equal snare distance tends to align the direct snare arrival at the two main capsules, which can help a centered, solid snare when combined. Their different locations still receive kick, toms, cymbals, reflections, and spill at different times and levels. Directional microphones can also color off-axis sources differently. Listen rather than declare the setup “in phase”; a polarity reversal or an arbitrary delay is not a substitute for checking the complete arrangement. [5, 6, 8, 12]
Studio and live use. This whole-kit image rewards a player-controlled balance and a room whose contribution is useful. On a quiet stage it may be tried when stands, sightlines, and the PA layout permit; treat the side mic as another open mic with spill and feedback potential. On a loud stage with wedges, an overhead-plus-side pair may give insufficient isolation or usable gain. A single overhead, closer directional positions, or individual spots may work better. Do not promise that a successful studio geometry transfers unchanged to a live PA, or place a low side mic where a moving floor tom, cymbal, or performer can strike it. [4, 9, 12–14]

# The four placement controls
Height. Move within a safe physical range to compare direct kit sound, cymbal emphasis, and room pickup. More distance often increases the room's relative contribution, but a higher position can also change which instruments fall on the mic's axis. Shure notes that a very high X/Y angle can aim past the kit; RØDE describes changes in cymbal-to-drum balance with height. Neither source gives a universal optimum number. Keep microphone-to-performer and ceiling clearance. [1, 3, 6]
Front to back. Shure describes moving a pair toward the front of its example kit to emphasize cymbals and rack tom, and toward the rear to favor snare, floor tom, and hi-hat; actual geometry can vary. Move and re-aim the array together when comparing, then assess the whole kit. [1]
Aim and polar pattern. Aim on-axis toward cymbals for a more cymbal-centered perspective or toward the drums for more drum presence; changing aim also changes off-axis coloration and what the mic rejects. DPA notes that a wide cardioid can cover a larger area close to a kit while tighter patterns focus more narrowly. Omni and directional responses are different at low frequencies and in a reverberant room. Do not equate small diaphragm with one fixed sound or claim a dynamic mic cannot serve as an overhead. Select actual model, capsule, mount, and pattern by their specifications and tested result. [1, 2, 5]
Pair width and center. For an X/Y pair, angle determines coverage while the capsules remain nearly coincident; moving their physical center shifts the image. For a spaced pair, spacing and unequal distances to sources affect width, arrival time, and level. Shure recommends considering an audio center through kick and snare instead of blindly centering above the kit's overall hardware footprint. Check whether snare and kick appear where intended in the stereo image and whether nearby hi-hat or crash cymbals dominate one side. [1, 5, 6]
# Distance, polarity, and mono
For two separated overheads, compare distance from each capsule's acoustic center to the snare strike region, using a safe non-contact measuring method while the player is stopped. DPA recommends equal snare distances for its stereo pair; Shure describes the same as a way to mitigate one source's time offset. Equal distance to the snare does not mean equal distance to kick, toms, or cymbals, and is not a proof of perfect phase coherence in the full kit. The Glyn Johns setup uses this snare reference for its overhead and floor-tom-side mic; Recorderman explicitly tries to reference both snare and kick. Avoid attaching tape or string to a player's drums without permission. [2, 6, 12–14]
Bring the two overhead channels into mono at intended relative levels, comparing snare body, kick, toms, cymbal tone, and apparent image stability. X/Y's near-coincident geometry tends to avoid time-offset comb filtering between the overheads themselves, but close kick and snare mics are still at different distances. DPA says conventional spaced A/B may produce comb filtering when summed and advises particular caution. A satisfactory stereo sound should not excuse a weak mono output when that output matters for the system. [5, 6]
Then add close channels one at a time in mono and finally in the intended stereo or PA layout. Reassess overhead or close-mic positions if a drum becomes thin, changes attack, or shifts sideways. Compare the console polarity option if appropriate, but do not label it a time-alignment switch: it inverts electrical polarity and cannot remove acoustic delay. Check any delay processing in the context of the entire kit, not by a universal sample offset or visual waveform alignment alone. High-pass filtering overheads can reduce unwanted low-frequency spill when the close kick/toms carry the low end, yet a full-range overhead may be exactly what an overhead-led kit needs. [1, 8]

Overheads may be the main coherent kit image, with close mics adding focus; room tone can be desirable. [1‑3]

Mechanics

Stable stands, secured cables, changeover repeatability, audience sightlines, and stage motion are central.
Glyn Johns arrangement
A useful option for an integrated, asymmetric whole-kit image when the player and room support it; add kick and/or snare control as needed. [12–14]
Assess whether the side mic's spill, stand position, and needed gain are workable. Retain the measured snare-distance concept only when its physical placement is safe; choose another approach if it is not. [4, 9]

The live guide's example of an overhead about one foot above the drummer's head should not be reproduced automatically: performers vary in height and movement, and that region may be occupied by sticks or cymbals. If the front-of-house system needs no overhead signal, a mic may still be useful for broadcast or in-ear monitoring, subject to their separate requirements. Do not intentionally cause feedback to teach a lesson. [4]
# Practical setup procedure
1.Hear the whole kit and intended performance. Decide whether the overheads will represent the kit or contribute primarily cymbals, and identify mono, stereo, monitors, and other feeds.
2.Choose one microphone or a stereo geometry suited to the role. For a basic stereo comparison, start with safe X/Y and then compare a separated pair if more width or coverage is useful. If choosing Glyn Johns, follow its dedicated overhead-and-floor-tom-side procedure above; do not treat it as a conventional symmetric pair.
3.Ask the player to stop. Mount microphones and cable safely, inspect ceiling and light clearance, and check the entire stick/cymbal motion and stand stability. No stand should be precarious above the performer.
4.Set equipment power and gain according to manuals with appropriate headroom. Have the drummer play at expected maximum intensity at a sensible monitoring level.
5.Compare height, front/back position, aim, and stereo angle or spacing one variable at a time. Listen for balance of snare, kick, toms, hats, crashes, ride, and room.
6.For separated capsules, compare distances to the snare as a starting reference, then audition both overheads in mono for all kit components. Adjust rather than assuming equality solves every source.
7.Add close mics at intended levels, check mono and the actual stereo or PA coverage, then re-check secured mounts and clearance with the full player motion.
# Troubleshooting and assessment
Observation
First checks
Hat or crash overwhelms one side
Move or re-aim the array and inspect capsule distance to that cymbal; review the stereo center and drummer's balance. [1]
Snare pulls to one side when close snare is added
Inspect overhead audio center, level and aim; compare left/right capsule distance to snare and intended panning. [1, 2]
Spaced pair sounds thin or uneven in mono
Compare arrival distances for snare and other prominent sources, change spacing or position, and consider X/Y if useful. Equal snare distance is not a whole-kit guarantee. [5, 6]
Overhead pair too roomy or diffuse
Evaluate room and reflective surfaces; move safely closer or re-aim toward the kit, or use a more focused pattern if suitable. [2, 6]
Feedback or stage spill when overhead added live
Lower or omit unnecessary overhead gain, test microphone pattern and position relative to monitors and other sources, and assess what the PA needs. Do not deliberately provoke feedback. [4, 9]
Boom or cables enter player path
Stop; secure and reposition the assembly, route cable safely, and repeat the player's full-motion clearance check.

Assessment scenario: give learners a kit with an off-center snare, left-side hi-hat, asymmetric crashes, close kick and snare channels, a moderately reflective room, and a PA that may sum to mono. Ask for a mono or stereo choice; X/Y, ORTF, A/B, or Glyn Johns geometry; safe mount; the role of overheads; and a sequence of distance, balance, and mono checks. For Glyn Johns, require learners to distinguish the overhead from the floor-tom-side mic, demonstrate the common snare-distance reference, explain optional kick/snare spots and adjustable panning, and listen for whole-kit and mono results. A second scenario changes to a loud stage with limited gain before feedback. Require learners to explain which decisions change, what remains unmeasured, and why one source's equal distance cannot validate the entire kit. More than one placement can pass if its reasoning and safety checks are sound.
# Critical source audit
Agreement: DPA, Shure, and RØDE describe overheads as a kit or cymbal perspective shaped by placement, not just a microphone model. Shure and DPA recognize that coincident X/Y generally provides a reliable mono sum between the two overheads, whereas spaced pairs need mono checks. Shure and DPA recommend attention to snare distance or center when using separated overheads. [1‑6]
Different emphases: Shure's Mike Major essay starts with overheads as an integrated kit image and aligns stereo around snare and kick; the Shure live guide also offers a cymbal-focused role with low-frequency filtering. DPA describes A/B's ambient and wide properties while its kit guide emphasizes controlled snare arrival; Shure's recent five-technique article highlights X/Y, Mid-Side, ORTF, A/B, and Recorderman. These are different intended outcomes, not a ranking of universally best methods. [1‑6]
Glyn Johns evidence: Recording Magazine's historical discussion and practitioner tutorials agree on an overhead above the snare region, a second mic beside the floor tom looking across the kit, and a snare-distance check; kick and sometimes snare spots complete the arrangement. They differ on fixed heights, three versus four total mics, and panning. Shure only mentions the conceptual similarity to Recorderman in the guide cited here. None of these secondary accounts proves a unique original dimension or obliges a manufacturer-specific microphone. Teach the consistent geometry and check outcomes in the actual room. [6, 12–14]
Overreach or tension: Shure's new article describes X/Y and Mid-Side mono compatibility as guaranteed. The narrow claim is sound for a correctly arranged overhead pair or properly decoded Mid-Side, but it does not guarantee a good result when combined with close mics, a miswired matrix, off-axis response, or other acoustic sources. DPA's stereo guide says A/B generally is not suitable for mono and suggests using a single channel, while Shure allows spaced pairs after actual mono checks and mitigation. Treat this as different tolerance for mono coloration and application, not a physical contradiction that forbids every spaced-pair mono sum. [5‑7]
Gaps: The cited guides do not establish one safe stand or boom configuration for all hardware, a mandatory height above any drummer, a universal amount of cymbal isolation or gain before feedback, a guaranteed numerical tonal curve for app controls, or automatic optimal delay and polarity settings for a multi-miked kit. The sources do not test Glyn Johns across different live monitor and PA arrangements. Human noise exposure and microphone maximum-SPL ratings are separate matters. Qualified stage personnel and the player should review any final live placement or overhead rigging depiction. [1–6, 9–14]
# Proposed app presentation
Provide labeled top and side views of the kit, player envelope, stands, and room. Start with mono versus stereo and overhead role. Let the learner choose X/Y, ORTF, A/B, or the complete Glyn Johns setup; optionally open Mid-Side and Recorderman explanations. In the Glyn Johns branch, let learners position the main overhead and floor-tom-side mic, compare both distances to a single snare reference, choose optional kick and snare spots, set modest adjustable panning, and check mono. Explicitly mark unsafe side-mic and boom locations, and avoid showing an arbitrary fixed 40-inch or 6-inch location as universally safe. The Membranophones drum-kit navigation links here, and Ensembles and Voice links back when discussing band rhythm sections. Controls move capsule center, height, aim, and separation; a snare-distance aid reports geometry without promising perfect phase. A mono button prompts a written check without fabricated response curves. Live mode adds PA coverage, monitors, and feedback considerations. Use diagrams and text, without audio examples.
