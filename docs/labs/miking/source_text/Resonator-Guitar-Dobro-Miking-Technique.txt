# Resonator Guitar and Dobro Miking Technique
Pro Audio Training Academy   Chordophones Lab   Studio and Live Sound   October 3, 2026
# Scope and learning goals
This lesson treats the acoustic sound of resonator guitars, including square-neck lap-style Dobro and round-neck instruments. The learner will identify the resonator system, find a balanced one-mic position, deliberately emphasize cone versus strings, adapt to a moving player and loud stage, and evaluate a pickup alongside a microphone. It covers miking, not instrument repair or pickup installation. No audio examples are included.
# What makes this source different
The strings excite a bridge and one or more metal resonator cones, which radiate through a coverplate and openings. A spider-bridge single-cone guitar, a biscuit-bridge single-cone guitar and a tricone do not present identical sound fields [1, 2]. The body material, sound ports, coverplate, player position, slide and picking hand also affect what a microphone hears. “Dobro” often refers to a spider-bridge lap-style resonator, but inspect the particular instrument instead of assuming every resonator is that design. It remains a chordophone: strings start the vibration.
The microphone hears airborne sound from the complete instrument. A bridge or cone pickup supplies a different electrical path; it can be useful for separation and gain before feedback, but it is not itself a microphone. Shure’s account of a bluegrass touring setup describes Jerry Douglas using a direct Fishman pedal for Dobro and, when a mic is used, Shure KSM32 or KSM44A [3]. That is a real-world option, not a universal prescription.
# One-microphone placement map
Begin with a steady performance at the musician’s real playing posture. For a quiet studio, a practical experiment is a mic roughly 20–45 cm (8–18 in) from the instrument, facing the broad coverplate/upper-body area from slightly off the player's picking path. This range is a teaching trial, not a manufacturer-specified optimum. Locate the sound by listening around the instrument before committing to a spot. A cardioid condenser can reveal detail; a dynamic can provide a robust close option; choose the actual mic for sensitivity, pattern, stage level and desired tone.

Shure’s recording guide groups Dobro with other acoustic string instruments and provides broad placement ideas [4]; its ordinary flat-top guitar sound-hole illustration should not be transferred literally to an instrument with a resonator coverplate. Move the microphone a few centimeters at a time, then change distance, and have the musician play low/high strings, open and barred notes, quiet/loud passages and the actual musical part. Aiming straight into the visual center of the coverplate is one experiment, not an established universal sweet spot.
# Playing posture and tone goals
Square-neck lap style: The strings and coverplate face upward. Bring a boom from the side so the capsule has a clear view of the instrument without crossing the bar path or the picking hand. Confirm the player’s seated/standing support and full reach before tightening the stand. Do not position the mic where a steel bar, arm or instrument movement can strike it. For more resonator identity, compare the off-center cone/coverplate region; for a less pointed tone, include more of the body and string area by moving back or changing angle.
Round-neck upright style: The instrument may face the room or be turned toward another performer. Aim at the actual projected sound, beginning between the resonator region and neck/body side, and watch how strumming or slide posture changes its orientation. A fixed close mic can sound quite different when the player turns; mark a comfortable playing zone and compare the full performance, not one held note.
Bright, cutting part: A closer view of the resonator region may help a busy ensemble, but assess metallic edge and pick noise in the mix. Rounded, integrated part: Move back enough to hear several radiating regions, if the room and spill allow. Do not prescribe a frequency boost, cut or microphone solely from a genre label; match a specific tonal goal against the actual player and arrangement.
# Studio approach: one mic, then an optional second view
Record or monitor one reliable mic first. Check the room, reflective floor and adjacent instruments; moving an instrument or mic can alter both direct sound and reflections. If more flexibility is needed, add a second mic for a distinct purpose, such as a closer cone view plus a farther overall view, or strings/neck region plus coverplate. Keep each channel separate, listen alone and combined at useful matched levels, and check the blend in mono. Different path lengths can produce cancellations; reposition and rebalance before assuming a polarity switch is the cure. A distant second view may be mainly room and cannot be expected to supply isolation.
If the instrument has a pickup, capture it separately when available. Compare the pickup alone, microphone alone and both together in mono; shift the mic or balance if the blend becomes hollow. Acoustic propagation and pickup electronics can give different timing and phase responses; no fixed delay or polarity setting suits every instrument. Label paths so a pickup or processed imaging pedal is not described as an acoustic microphone. Fishman offers different resophonic pickup products for spider and biscuit designs and recommends professional installation for a cone-mounted model [5]. Do not modify the resonator for a lesson.
# Live sound: isolation, feedback and player movement
In an acoustic ensemble on a quiet stage, a stand mic can preserve a broad instrument sound, provided the player maintains a workable distance. Shure describes both choreographed shared-mic bluegrass performance and individual close miking; the former depends heavily on room, monitors and performer movement, while closer positions can improve gain before feedback [3]. Soundcheck the full ensemble, including voices, banjo, cymbals and wedges. Choose a directional pattern and place its rejection toward the strongest competing source using that microphone’s actual polar pattern; supercardioid models have a rear pickup lobe, so a wedge directly behind may be a poor location.
Where stand placement restricts movement, an instrument-mounted mic can move with the guitar. DPA explicitly lists its 4099G for guitar and Dobro and gives a body-depth fit of 35–122 mm for its clip [6]. Its general guitar suggestion is near the neck/body junction and toward a sound hole for output; a resonator lacks the same flat-top sound hole, so aim and mount only after checking the actual instrument and the clip instructions. Never attach to the delicate cone, obstruct the coverplate/sound ports, clamp an unsuitable edge or interfere with bar, hands and case handling. Obtain the owner’s consent before a clip contacts a finish. Secure a strain-relieved cable and keep stand bases outside foot traffic.
On a loud stage, an existing, correctly installed pickup through the appropriate preamp/DI can carry the main PA level, with a microphone blended only as gain before feedback permits. A Fishman imaging pedal is one documented direct strategy for resophonic guitar [3, 7]; its modeled/imaged output is an electrical sound source, not a mic capsule hearing air. If feedback appears, reduce the channel/monitor level, identify the offending path, change instrument/monitor/mic geometry and open-mic count, and retest. A high-pass filter may remove unnecessary low rumble, but its setting is context dependent; avoid automatic steep filtering that strips body. With phantom power, follow the microphone and connected device manuals. Avoid placing the player or engineer in a loud monitor field during extended troubleshooting.

Do not open a coverplate or adjust a cone, bridge, spider screw or pickup to solve a microphone problem. National Reso-Phonic warns that the cone is delicate and instrument setup is model-specific [2]. Keep booms and clamps off the resonator hardware; check cable paths and stand stability. For loud stages, use measured levels and suitable hearing protection as needed; OSHA identifies 85 dBA eight-hour time-weighted average as its hearing-conservation action level, which is not a guarantee of safety at other exposures [8].
# Teaching exercise and pass criterion
1.Identify the player’s posture and visible resonator type without opening the instrument. Ask the musician to perform the intended part and define a safe mic/stand zone.
2.Using one mic, compare an off-center resonator view, a neck/body view and a broader view; keep playing level and gain comparisons as consistent as practical. Describe actual observed changes rather than assumed ones.
3.Choose one studio and one live position, documenting capsule, distance, angle, player movement, room/monitor placement and spill. Explain why they differ.
4.If a pickup exists, compare microphone, pickup and mono blend, and identify the electrical path accurately. Demonstrate a safe response to feedback or a loose resonator component.
Pass criterion: The learner can set a stable and playable mic position, justify a tonal choice by listening, check a multi-source blend in mono, and avoid unsafe contact with resonator hardware or hazardous stage levels.
# Evidence audit and limits
Direct support: Beard and National document resonator components and designs [1, 2]. Shure documents a working Dobro microphone/direct choice and the tradeoffs of shared versus close live miking [3]. DPA explicitly supports a model-specific Dobro clip [6]. Fishman documents specialized pickups and professional installation [5].
Inferences to verify: The 20–45 cm trial and the regional tone descriptions are a structured listening method based on acoustic geometry, not a published universal Dobro standard. Shure’s general acoustic-guitar chart [4] and DPA’s flat-top guitar aim [6] do not establish a particular coverplate point. Instrument design, posture, room and player can reverse an expected tonal adjective. Confidence is high in the mechanical and signal-path distinctions, moderate in any predicted tonal change before listening to the actual guitar.
