# Concert Bass Drum — Miking Technique Research
Lab: 1. Membranophones — Drums and Drumheads
Sequence: After timpani; before concert snare. One instrument in this installment.
Scope: Suspended orchestral/concert bass drum (gran cassa), for studio, concert recording, and live reinforcement. This is distinct from the drum-kit kick lesson. No audio examples.
# Learning goals
Identify the sound of a large, usually two-headed concert bass drum in the ensemble; choose whether the main array needs a spot; safely position a spot on the playing side without entering the mallet or damping path; compare attack and sustained low-frequency body; preserve headroom for powerful strikes; check multiple mics in mono; and adapt placement for amplified stages. [1–5]
# Begin with instrument and performance
The concert bass drum may be mounted on a wheeled, tilting stand, with a head on each side. A player can strike different head regions, use various mallets, play a roll, let the sound bloom, or damp one or both heads to suit the score. James Campbell's Yamaha teaching guide describes the middle region as a normal starting area, with outer and center regions producing different sustain and color; these are *player decisions*, not microphone positioning commands. The drum may face sideways into the ensemble, and its orientation influences the perceived directness. Learn the particular instrument, stage plan, playing side, damping hand, and score before positioning stands. [2, 3]
A large hit can produce substantial low-frequency energy and a fast transient, even if the part has few notes. In orchestral recording, an ensemble main array can already carry much of the low end; a spot may primarily add definition. In live reinforcement, the balance is different because PA, monitors, neighboring instruments, and room modes affect how much of a spot can be used. Listen to the acoustic sound and main microphones before choosing a channel count. [1, 4]

# Tone, location, and microphone selection
A spot is a supplement in a natural orchestra. With omni main microphones, the guide specifically expects low-frequency content from the main array and uses the closer bass-drum spot to give a distinct transient. Start with the spot low in the mix and listen for whether the drum stays in the same apparent space as the orchestra. An overly loud or narrow spot can shift its position unnaturally. [1, 4]
Aim and distance change the balance. A safe above/diagonal playing-side angle can give attack without putting the capsule in the strike path. Moving closer generally increases direct drum pickup relative to the room and nearby sources, but makes the exact head region and mallet sound more prominent. Moving back may integrate the two heads and room better but increases bleed and reduces usable gain on a loud stage. The particular result is a hypothesis to test with rolls, large strokes, and damping in the intended space, not an invariant frequency curve. [1–4]
Other head and surrounding space. A resonant-side mic, a side position, or an approved room mic can be tested for a featured solo in a quiet environment. These sound fields overlap with the main pickup and playing-side spot; adding channels does not necessarily improve the result. No reliable source in this research established a universal second-head location or a rule that one side always supplies bass while the other supplies attack. Treat such claims cautiously and keep the arrangement as simple as the performance allows. [1, 4; application inference]
Pattern and hardware. The guide notes that a cardioid spot may be adequate when the main omni array already supplies low frequencies; it also allows an omni but cautions that adding it can change the ensemble image more. Choose the actual mic for response, pattern, peak SPL, powering, size, and stable mounting. A bass-drum-branded mic may have a shaped response designed for a modern kick; audition it against a neutral mic if orchestral realism matters. A condenser is not automatically too fragile, and a dynamic is not automatically the only safe option. [1, 5]
Ribbon caution. The cited classical guide strongly warns against a ribbon microphone close to a bass drum because moving air can damage a delicate ribbon. A cautious lab avoids close or direct air-blast placement; if a ribbon is proposed at any distance, follow its own maker's guidance and evaluate air movement and rig stability. The book's absolute warning is a conservative practitioner position, not proof that all distant ribbon use is physically impossible. [1]

The source's possible suggestion of rotating the drum to point more toward the conductor is *not* an engineering shortcut. Yamaha percussion pedagogy explains why sideways head orientation often blends better, and the actual stand's manufacturer prescribes securing and angle-adjustment steps. Only the authorized player/crew should consider a musically appropriate orientation under the specific stand manual. Some stands require caster brakes, multiple people for mounting, and correctly tightened pivot holders; turning a clamped drum can damage the holder. [1–3]
In live sound, locate the PA and monitors relative to the microphone's *actual* polar pattern, and check for low-frequency feedback and excessive bleed at planned level with the system operator. More mics or an added resonant-side mic can reduce usable gain. Wind protection may be needed outdoors, but any cover must be appropriate to the chosen microphone and position. Never intentionally drive the system into feedback to prove a placement. [5, 6]
# Headroom, overlap, and mono
Set the microphone, preamp, and any pad for the strongest realistic hit, not the average passage; leave headroom in the recorded or reinforced signal. A pad in the wrong stage of the chain cannot repair overload earlier in the path. The guide specifically notes that a powerful bass-drum hit may require a mic pad at its example distance. Also check the main-array input path, since it hears the same low-frequency energy. Do not equate microphone maximum-SPL ratings with safe hearing exposure. [1, 7]
Bring up the main array alone, then the spot at intended level. Compare powerful single hits, quiet strokes, rolls, and the decay in mono and stereo. If adding a spot weakens body or gives a hollow transient, try position, level, and polarity comparisons; inversion is a diagnostic and cannot cancel acoustic travel time at all frequencies. If a second-head mic is used, check the two spots together, then against the main array. A fixed automatic polarity reversal for the far head is unjustified. Time alignment may help a particular blend, but can alter the main image and spill: decide by listening in the full ensemble. [4, 8]
# Physical and hearing safety
Before placing a mic, ask the performer to show the complete mallet arc and both-hand damping actions, including rolls, alternative mallets, and the back head. Stop playing and mute channels for stand adjustments. Never place a mic or boom where a mallet can hit it or where a hand must reach to damp the head. Route cables away from the player's feet, stand casters, and percussion traffic. Use a stable, appropriately rated stand and approved rigging; avoid clipping hardware to the drum without a compatible mount and the owner's permission. [2, 3; specific mic-clearance checks are instructional inference]
Do not unlock, tilt, rotate, or lift the instrument to obtain a preferred microphone angle. The Yamaha CB-9000 stand manual, for example, says to lock four caster brakes and use at least two people when mounting, and to loosen the two wing bolts before angle adjustment, then secure the holders. Other models may differ: the equipment's own instructions and the percussion crew control this operation. A falling concert bass drum can cause severe injury. [3]
Avoid excessive sound levels in soundcheck, rehearsal, and crew proximity. NIOSH exposure guidance concerns *people*; it is separate from a mic's SPL limit. Plan monitoring and hearing protection appropriate to duration and level. [7]
# Guided practical lab
1.Inspect the real setup. Confirm drum type, stand model, playing side, mallet/roll path, damping of both heads, likely peak dynamics, player access, and nearby percussion. Do not move the drum. [2, 3]
2.Evaluate ensemble pickup. Listen to the main microphones with the full passage, including loud hits and decay. Describe what is missing before adding a spot. [1, 4]
3.Place one safe spot. Try the above/diagonal playing-side example around 45 cm from the head only if the performer confirms complete clearance and the stand is secure. Check signal and peak headroom. [1, 3]
4.Compare tone. With the same performance passage, adjust aim or distance one variable at a time. Identify changes in articulation, body, room, other instruments, and the perceived placement in the ensemble. [1, 4]
5.Test an optional second view. If musically useful and physically feasible, compare a more distant or far-head position; first solo, then sum in mono at useful levels. Discard it if it adds bleed, phase problems, or risk. [4, 8]
6.Check live conditions. With a qualified operator, use the actual PA and monitors to evaluate intended level, low-frequency feedback, bleed, cable routing, and full playing/damping movement. [5–7]
7.Document the choice. Note position, measured distance/aim, pattern, stand clearances, gain/pad, main/spot balance, and why any optional mic was kept. No audio examples.

# Critical source audit
Agreement. The classical-recording guide and orchestral engineer account support an ensemble main array with optional spots; the former specifically locates a single concert bass-drum spot above and diagonally toward the playing head. Yamaha's pedagogy establishes that strike area, damping, and drum orientation affect the musical result, while Yamaha's stand manual establishes real handling hazards. [1–4]
Different emphases. The Decca-tradition guide emphasizes transient definition on top of main-array low end and warns that a raised spot can disturb the ensemble image. Yamaha's educational article emphasizes how sideways placement can warm/blend the instrument. These address recording balance and player placement respectively; neither authorizes an engineer to rotate a clamped stand. [1–3]
Direct tension. The guide's strong “no ribbon” advice is a conservative close-drum protection rule. It should not be expanded to claim that a properly positioned *distant* ribbon could never capture the ensemble. Follow the actual ribbon maker's limits and avoid direct air pressure; the evidence here does not specify a universally safe ribbon distance. [1]
Gaps. These sources provide no universal far-head microphone distance, invariant attack-to-bass split between heads, guaranteed polarity, stand clearance, numeric safe peak for a human ear, or fixed live feedback margin. The lesson treats such choices as site-specific tests, prioritizing musician and crew safety. [1–8]
