# Headed Tambourine — Miking Technique Research
Lab: 1. Membranophones — Drums and Drumheads
Sequence: After concert snare; last named instrument in the Membranophones list. Cross-link jingles and headless tambourine in Lab 2, Idiophones.
Scope: Handheld and securely mounted headed tambourines in studio and live sound. No audio examples.
# Learning goals
Identify the membrane and jingle contributions; select one position that covers the actual playing motions; compare head-forward, jingle-forward, and integrated tones; manage rapid jingle peaks and changing distance; decide whether one mic or a room/ensemble perspective suffices; apply safe live placement and gain checks. [1–6]
# Classification and playing reality
A concert headed tambourine combines a vibrating drumhead (membranophone) with metal jingles (idiophones). This lesson belongs in Membranophones because it emphasizes the *headed* instrument, but learners should follow the Idiophones cross-link for the jingles and for headless tambourine. Some headed models are struck by hand or stick; others are shaken, rolled by thumb/finger, or played with a knee/fist motion. A Percussive Arts Society instructor describes a roughly 45-degree playing angle that blends head and jingles; that is a performer's technique example, not a prescribed mic angle. [1–3]
Ask for the entire intended phrase before placing a mic: isolated fingertip strikes, loud accents, shake and thumb/finger rolls, mounting changes, movement between other percussion, and any deliberate muffling. Head material, jingle material, number of rows, and player's technique can change the balance considerably. A manufacturer describes a headed goat-skin model as suitable for hand or stick, while PAS notes that a concert tambourine has a head and is distinct from a typically headless rock tambourine. Do not require an altered grip or roll to make a mic diagram work. [1–3]

Shure's table does not say its 6–12-inch rule is specifically for a headed tambourine or for every playing style. Yamaha explicitly notes that a headed version has extra low/mid content and that shaking toward/away from a stationary mic changes level. These differences justify a motion test, not a single fixed distance. [4, 5]
# Movement, aim, and tone
Forward and backward movement. At a fixed mic, a handheld tambourine that moves toward the capsule on one stroke and away on the next can produce large level differences. Yamaha suggests that sideways movement relative to the mic can yield a steadier capture, but that is a *possible performance choice* to discuss with the player. The engineer should first seek a mic location with adequate coverage of the player's intended technique; do not force the musician into a new motion. If the playing station shifts materially, place for the total travel or choose a wider coverage option at the cost of greater spill. [5, 6]
Head versus jingles. Moving the mic's aim toward the head can emphasize the struck membrane, while turning toward the rim/jingles can emphasize bright metallic attack; the sound radiates from multiple vibrating parts, so the balance may change with head angle and stroke. Treat these as listening hypotheses. Keep the mic off the player's hand/stick and shake trajectories. Do not confuse a mic placed *near* the head with one physically contacting or muting it. [1–5]
Distance and room. Closer pickup usually increases direct sound relative to neighboring musicians but can make a single jingle or impact dominate. A little more distance can blend head, rim, and room; excessive distance on a loud stage admits bleed and may reduce gain before feedback. If it sounds too bright, Shure explicitly recommends experimenting with distance and angle. A microphone whose high end is naturally gentle can be considered, but the actual response and physical coverage matter more than a transducer stereotype. [4, 5]
Microphone choice. A suitably specified condenser can capture fast transients and subtle rolls; a suitable dynamic or ribbon may produce a different balance. Yamaha mentions all three as possibilities, with the caveat that an aggressive peak can overload the input even when a meter that averages level appears modest. Use an appropriate peak meter and verify the mic and preamp's actual maximum levels. A fragile ribbon must never be put in the sweep of a struck or shaken instrument or exposed to an unsafe air blast; follow its manufacturer's placement limits. A cardioid can help reduce some spill, while a wider pattern may cover movement better but hear more of the stage. [5, 6]

A spot may pull the tambourine too close in the image; avoid unnecessary bright spill.
Live reinforced percussion station
Use an appropriately directional, stable mic aligned to the *actual* station and monitors. A documented tour used a direct cardioid shared with shakers and tambourine and broader percussion overheads. [6]
Verify gain before feedback, other percussion bleed, full performer movement, and cable/stand clearance.
Player walking or dancing
A stationary mic may provide inconsistent coverage; work with the performer and system operator on station choreography or suitable approved coverage.
Do not attach an unsuitable clip to the shell or require the musician to stop moving to satisfy one spot.

Do not use a universal high-pass frequency, limiter threshold, or EQ notch for all headed tambourines: the head may contribute musically important low/mid information, while metal jingles can be sharp. A live filter should be judged by the full sound and feedback behavior at the intended level. Stage monitoring and PA geometry determine what is workable. [5–7]
# Gain, multi-mic summation, and safety
Set gain and any pad from the loudest intended shake/accent as well as the quietest head/roll phrase. Yamaha warns that brief hand-percussion transients may exceed what a slow VU meter appears to show; use peak metering and check both mic and input-stage overload. Do not push a player to hit harder to “test” hardware beyond the musical passage. If the best integrated mic already conveys the part, a second close mic is generally unnecessary. [4, 5]
Where a percussion overhead, ensemble array, or optional second angle also hears the tambourine, compare all microphones at intended levels in mono. If body or attack changes unnaturally, first move/aim or adjust level; try electrical polarity as a diagnostic, not an automatic switch. Polarity inversion does not fix every frequency-dependent arrival-time difference. Extra open mics live can also increase spill and feedback risk. [6–8]
Ask the player to demonstrate the complete arm, wrist, hand, stick, and instrument swing before placing stands. Keep capsules, clips, and cables clear of both the moving frame and any head contact. Stop playing, mute, and move hardware only with communication. Use stable mounts with owner permission and do not attach to an instrument or interfere with jingles/head unless the hardware is expressly compatible. If an unstable tambourine is already damaged or jingles/pins appear loose, refer it to the owner or percussionist; do not modify it. [1–3; detailed clearance checks are instructional inference]
Use hearing protection and level management appropriate to actual exposure in rehearsal and performance. Human hearing safety is separate from the mic's peak-SPL capability. If feedback arises in a live PA, work with the operator on mic/monitor location, open channels, gain, routing, and appropriate filtering rather than promising that one polar pattern alone will solve it. [7, 9]
# Guided practical lab
1.Observe. Have the player show fingertip/hand/stick strikes as relevant, shake and finger/thumb rolls, loudest accents, transitions to other stations, and the entire motion path. Identify whether the instrument is headed. [1–3]
2.Establish one mic. Choose a stable position outside movement, initially in the source-backed 6–12-in neighborhood only when physically safe. Log actual capsule distance, angle, pattern, and player posture. [4, 5]
3.Compare head and jingle balance. Change aim/distance one variable at a time, checking the same complete passage. Note how body, sparkle, room, and other instrument spill change. [4, 5]
4.Check peaks. Watch peak metering during real accents and make sure the quiet roll is still intelligible without clipping on transients. [5]
5.Evaluate ensemble/live use. Audition existing percussion mics or the main array, add the spot only if helpful, check any combination in mono, and use real monitors/PA with an operator for feedback tests. [6–9]
6.Document. Explain the chosen instrument/head/jingle balance, movement, clearance, peak headroom, and reason for the live or studio geometry. No audio examples.

# Critical source audit
Agreement. PAS describes the head/jingle blend and multiple performance motions. Yamaha confirms extra low/mid contribution of a headed tambourine and the effect of movement and short peaks. Shure proposes a single mic 6–12 in away and recommends angle/distance experiments when too bright. A documented live percussion rig uses direct and wider area microphones to cover changing station needs. [1, 4–6]
Different emphases. Shure's placement is an instrument-wide generalization; Yamaha's eight-inch starting point is general hand percussion but explicitly mentions headed tambourine. They do not establish a specific strike-angle or safe distance for all players. PAS's roughly 45-degree angle describes the *way to hold* a concert tambourine; it must not be reinterpreted as a mic angle. [1, 4, 5]
Direct contradictions. None of the cited primary sources reports opposing results under the same instrument, technique, room, and microphone conditions. The apparent “one spot” versus “direct plus overheads” difference is a choice of solo versus multi-instrument amplified coverage. [4–6]
Gaps. There is no verified universal head-to-jingle frequency ratio, instrument-motion envelope, minimum safe clearance, guaranteed feedback margin, prescribed polarity, or gain setting. The lesson requires an actual performance and site test rather than inventing them. [1–9]
# Curriculum and app presentation
This lesson closes the named Membranophones instruments. Headed tambourine is a combined membrane/jingle source: link its jingles to the headless tambourine and tambourine-jingles material in Lab 2, Idiophones. Later percussion-ensemble and band lessons revisit it in multi-source context. The drum-kit overview points to Drum Overheads for Glyn Johns; that method is unrelated to one handheld tambourine.
Illustrate headed frame, membrane, jingle pairs, hand/stick and shake paths, a stand at a safe location, and a broad pickup region. Let learners move the *mic*, selecting studio/live/ensemble and qualitative head/jingle balance. Include a motion-path clearance and peak-meter check; no audio examples.
