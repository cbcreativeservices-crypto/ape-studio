# Miking Labs Master Plan
Complete curriculum scope • Seven labs • October 4, 2026
## Purpose
Teach how microphone choice, position and arrangement shape instrument, voice, event and environmental pickup. Compare studio, location and live approaches. Scientific and acoustical lessons prioritize valid, repeatable measurements. Direct electrical signals and contact sensors are supporting comparisons where relevant.
## Seven lab structure
1. Membranophones — Drums and Drumheads
2. Idiophones — Cymbals, Solid-Body Percussion and Mallet Instruments
3. Aerophones — Brass, Woodwinds and Free-Reed Instruments
4. Chordophones — Strings, Guitars, Basses and Acoustic Pianos
5. Ensembles and Voice — Individual Singers and Combined Sources
6. Foley Field Recording and Scientific and Acoustical Recording
7. Sports and Broadcasting
## How to use the plan
This plan consolidates the approved sources and later additions. Scope IDs identify topics, not finished lessons. Develop and deliver one source or application at a time; retain functional pairs and setups when they need to be taught together.
Each lesson must be researched, audited and saved as a downloadable document before moving to the next. No audio examples are included. The plan sets teaching objectives; it does not substitute for the sourced placement instructions in individual lessons.
## Decisions carried forward
Cover the Glyn Johns technique within drum overheads in Lab 1. Ensemble lessons cross-reference that lesson.
Include wooden flute alongside the common metal flute, and retain piccolo.
Keep djembe in its original place; review existing coverage rather than create a duplicate.
Add tonbak, tabla, gong, oud, sitar, veena, full orchestra, jazz combos and jazz big band.

# Lab 6 Scientific and Acoustical Recording
Second part of Lab 6. Define the measurement question, then choose positions that answer it. Field type, calibration, orientation and procedure affect the interpretation of measured data. [1, 2]
ID
Application
Miking techniques to develop

Select appropriate response and field correction; verify capsule orientation, calibration data and compatible powering; perform and log acoustic checks before and after a session when required.
F12

Plan representative locations, heights, orientations and durations; document obstacles, weather and background; distinguish calibrated SPL results from uncalibrated waveform levels.
F13

Select source and receiver positions for the intended room-acoustic method; compare multiple locations; capture usable impulse responses and decay data; identify noise-floor limits.
F14

On-axis and off-axis positions, listening-area sampling, near-field and boundary approaches where applicable; identify reference paths and timing; separate room effects from source response.
F15

Repeatable coordinates around safe demonstration sources; separate airborne and structure-borne paths; compare single points and spatial maps; avoid contact with moving or energized equipment.
F16
Scientific arrays and specialized sensors
Introductory microphone-array localization, sound-intensity probes and acoustic imaging; use method-specific spacing, orientation and calibration. Optional bioacoustic, underwater and ultrasonic extensions require suitable transducers and bandwidth.
## Scientific teaching requirements
Log microphone and calibrator identity, calibration information, sensitivity and gain, position and orientation, source condition, environment and capture settings. Preserve relevant raw data; document analysis, processing and uncertainty. A single-frequency field check does not establish full system frequency response.
Separate classroom comparisons from measurements to a named standard. Verify current method and equipment requirements before teaching compliance, exposure, sound-power or intensity conclusions. Relative comparisons need not support an absolute calibrated level claim.
## Safe and repeatable exercises
Use modest-level sources and logged positions. Change one variable, then repeat the original setup. Control excitation level and hearing exposure. No audio examples are included.
## Cross links
Link field speech to Lab 7 interviews, spatial pickup to Lab 5 ensembles, and measurement positions to the Sound Systems and Acoustics labs.

## Production context that affects miking
Teach program, PA and monitor-feed distinctions; talkback, IFB and mix-minus as supporting routing concepts; microphone position remains the core task. Check what the performer hears and whether a microphone feeds the PA. Coordinate usable pickup with camera shots, lighting, operator access and wireless-system personnel.
For every application, compare live continuity with controlled studio alternatives. Document a workable fallback if movement, wind, wardrobe, crowd noise or channel availability prevents the preferred approach.

## Acceptance criteria
A successful setup provides understandable speech, useful action detail and an intentional venue perspective at the required output. It remains workable through movement and changing conditions. Document coverage, peak headroom, mic overlap, camera constraints, weather protection and approved mounting. Keep equipment out of playing areas unless expressly approved and safely installed.

# Research Framework for Labs 6 and 7
The application lists are curriculum proposals. These sources establish useful foundations; every detailed lesson still requires application-specific research, verified geometry and a practical observation sheet. Manufacturer demonstrations describe particular equipment and conditions.
## Points of agreement
The field and sports sources emphasize microphone placement and protection against unwanted noise. The measurement sources tie microphone selection and geometry to the intended question. Teach a defined target, an appropriate microphone and a documented position before adding channels. [1–5]
## Different approaches
Foley practitioners select different microphones for different sounds. Broadcast pickup uses handheld, body-worn, boom and ambience approaches according to the task. Measurement pickup depends on the sound field and method. These choices support different objectives rather than one preferred universal technique. [1, 4–6]
## Contradictions requiring scrutiny
Klover’s FAQ suggests acoustic wavelength is less relevant than electromagnetic wavelength to parabolic behavior. Wildtronics’ technical discussion explicitly relates gain to wavelength and dish diameter. Teach that dependence; distinguish low-frequency sound reaching a capsule from useful directional gain supplied by the dish. Verify claims against model-specific data. Use Klover’s assembly guidance for its equipment, without promising a universal range. [7, 8]
## Important gaps
These overview sources do not establish universal distances, safe sport-specific mounting locations, a complete measurement-compliance procedure or reliable isolation for every venue. Detailed lessons must obtain those requirements from the applicable equipment, measurement method and venue. Confidence is high in the category structure and broad distinctions; specific setups remain to be researched.

# Lesson Development and Delivery
## Required structure for every lesson
1. Source and task: design, sound radiation, action or movement, and desired perspective. For measurement lessons, define the quantity and method.
2. Microphone selection: useful types and polar patterns, relevant response, output and headroom; verify model-specific requirements.
3. Core positions: clearly describe target, distance, angle and clearance. Present numbers as sourced starting points or explicitly labeled trials.
4. Alternative approaches: compare closer and more distant perspectives, on-axis and off-axis pickup, and mono or stereo arrangements when useful.
5. Studio and live comparison: room contribution, isolation, spill, monitor rejection, feedback margin, movement and practical channel priorities.
6. Multiple microphones: explain overlap, polarity and timing checks where applicable; test the combined sound, not just isolated channels.
7. Troubleshooting and safe setup: tonal problems, noise, clipping, secure stands, performer access, cable routing and appropriate listening levels.
8. Teaching exercise: a repeatable placement comparison, student observation sheet and clear criteria for evaluating the result.
9. Evidence audit: source links, supported practices, differing approaches, limitations and items requiring further verification.
## Research and accuracy standard
Use authoritative technical documentation, credible instrument specialists and documented practitioner methods. Check that a source applies to the actual instrument, microphone and situation. Distinguish a documented method from a proposed classroom experiment. Explain disagreements in terms of musical goal, room, player, microphone and reinforcement context. Do not invent quotes, source attribution, universal distances or unsupported safety claims.
Audit instructions before delivery, particularly mounting, electrical interfaces, hearing exposure and feedback demonstrations. Verify specifications against the equipment in use; obtain qualified venue support for overhead or suspended mounting. Keep hands, bows, sticks and moving instrument parts clear of microphone hardware.
