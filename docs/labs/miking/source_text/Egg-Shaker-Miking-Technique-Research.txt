# Egg Shaker Miking Technique
Pro Audio Training Academy   Idiophones Lab   Egg shaker   October 3, 2026
# Scope and learning goals
This lesson addresses the small, handleless egg shaker, singly or as a pair. Its enclosed particles collide with the shell as the performer moves it: an idiophone, with no drumhead or speaker required. The preceding handheld-shaker lesson establishes general moving-source principles; this lesson concentrates on the egg's compact shell, the player's grip, widely varying product output, and the possibility of two independent hands. Maracas, including handled egg-shaped models, are next in the list.
Students should be able to choose a suitable egg shaker before using gain to compensate for a quiet source; find a microphone position that covers a full phrase without hand or stand collision; decide whether one mic can serve two hands; and adapt that setup to quiet studio work, ensemble spill, and live sound.
# What changes the acoustic sound
The character and level depend on shell, fill and playing action. The performer may use short wrist strokes, broad arm motion, one egg, or an egg in each hand. The sounds of forward and return motion need not be equally accented. Do not prescribe a particular “bright end” or frequency balance from the egg's shape: listen to the actual object and whole rhythmic phrase. A palm can partly shield the shell, and changes in grip can affect the heard level or texture. Ask the player to use the intended grip and dynamics during microphone placement, rather than forcing a cramped hand position.
The instrument choice matters. Meinl's four-piece egg-shaker set explicitly offers soft, medium, loud and extra-loud versions for different ensemble levels [1]. Those labels are relative product categories; they are not calibrated sound-pressure levels or a promise that an extra-loud model will cut through every stage. An egg that works as a delicate overdub may not give a live microphone enough useful direct sound above a loud backline. Audition alternatives in context before adding channel gain.
# A single egg: starting method
1.Listen and watch. Ask for the complete part, including its largest gesture and the player's normal body movement. Observe the egg's working area and nearby cymbals, vocals and monitors. The shell must remain safely clear of the stand, capsule and cable throughout.
2.Try one stand-mounted mic. In a quiet studio, audition a suitable condenser with its capsule around 30–60 cm (1–2 ft) from the middle of the usual playing area, aimed into that area. Shure offers at least 30 cm as a broad beginner example for percussion [2]. The upper end and center-of-motion target here are reasoned trials for an egg that moves; neither is an egg-specific standard. Read whether the microphone is side-address or end-address. A cardioid can favor the source over room noise; in a quiet room an omni can be considered if broad pickup is more helpful than isolation [3].
3.Use the full pattern to refine. If one stroke leaps in level because it passes very close, move back or recenter the microphone. If the egg is buried by room or other instruments, try closer placement *outside the movement path*, another position with better rejection, or a louder suitable egg. A slightly different height or angle can change the balance of the egg, hand noise and spill, but do not assert the same tonal effect for all models.
4.Set gain on the loudest phrase. Leave input headroom; listen for preamp or capsule overload separately from acoustic rattles. A pad is for electronic overload, not a solution to poor source-to-spill ratio. Apply high-pass filtering only if a low thump, handling sound or stage rumble is unwanted and the part remains useful. Do not automatically boost the high end to fix a weak live signal.
The stated distance is to the center of the motion, not to the nearest excursion. At 30 cm a hand may still approach the capsule in a large stroke: verify the real clearance. A quieter source can tempt unusually close miking, which makes the changes in distance, handling sound and off-axis tone more prominent. Shure's general placement guidance recommends listening for a balanced position rather than choosing by sight alone [4].
# One egg versus two hands
For one egg, a single mono microphone often provides all the control needed. For two eggs, have the player perform the actual interlocking pattern. If both hands occupy a reasonably compact area, start with one mic aimed between their usual positions, far enough back to encompass both; compare levels and timbres hand to hand. If the hands separate widely or intentionally play different parts, consider a separate spot for each hand or a well-positioned area mic. Two spots offer independent control, but add spill, stands, and a need to check the combined result for timing, comb filtering and mono compatibility. Do not assign left and right microphones merely because there are two hands: decide from the arrangement and movement. If one hand obscures its egg with the body or palm, reposition the player or microphone before adding another channel.
The manufacturer's egg-shaker pairs are evidence that two eggs may be played or supplied together [5]; the exact two-hand miking arrangements above are practical proposals to test, not product instructions. Stereo can represent deliberate spatial gestures in a quiet room; a simple mono spot often keeps a supporting rhythm stable within the mix.

# Studio and live application
Quiet studio overdub. Choose an egg whose level and grain suit the part [1]. Work away from noisy fans and reflective surfaces if the extra room sound is unwanted. Set the mic for the normal grip and entire phrase. A slightly wider working distance can make movement-related changes less abrupt, while a closer position can improve direct-to-room ratio. Solo briefly for defects, then judge in the arrangement. Avoid making the player strike harder than the music requires only to generate input level.
Simultaneous ensemble. Check whether a main stereo pair or percussion overhead already captures the egg sufficiently. Where a spot is needed, put the egg player and microphone so that the desired source has a useful level over cymbals and instruments. Listen to main and spot together and in mono. A weak egg in a loud acoustic setting cannot be rescued simply by turning up the spot: it also turns up spill. Move the source, choose a louder egg, or change the ensemble layout.
Live stage. Give the performer an accessible, repeatable playing location. A dedicated directional mic can help separate the egg from the rest of a percussion rig; one documented DPA tour used a direct microphone for shakers and tambourine, though it did not specify egg-shaker placement [6]. Soundcheck with the complete band and monitors. Cardioid microphones reject most at their rear; supercardioid and hypercardioid designs have rear lobes and nulls to the sides of the rear, so point the actual mic's low-sensitivity direction toward the relevant wedge or backline [3]. Keep the egg in the main pickup area while leaving comfortable arm movement. Mute an idle open spot where appropriate. In a very loud setting, physical spill and feedback margin may make close acoustic reinforcement impractical; state that limit rather than promise an EQ fix.
Wind and movement. Outdoor wind can cause microphone rumble; use a suitable windscreen and a sheltered position if possible. A windscreen does not make a quiet egg louder relative to amplified stage sources. Tape or clamp a loose cable to the stand with strain relief and keep loops away from the moving hands and feet. Avoid attaching a mic or cable to a small egg unless a purpose-built system and the performer's technique make it safe and useful.
# Microphone and gain considerations
Small diaphragm condensers are a reasonable audition choice for detail; a suitably placed dynamic can be useful amid stage spill if there is adequate gain and the result suits the arrangement. The polar pattern and position matter more than a blanket microphone ranking. Directional mics can change tone as the egg leaves their pickup axis; proximity effect from very close directional pickup can exaggerate low-frequency handling sound [3]. An omni generally allows broader direction coverage but picks up more room and loudspeaker sound, so it is generally unsuitable for a loud monitored stage [3]. Verify the particular microphone's specifications and any phantom-power requirements through its proper interface.
For quiet eggs, distinguish low source level from insufficient preamp gain: increase gain if the direct mic signal is clean and the acoustic separation is already acceptable. If cymbals or wedge sound dominates, source placement, microphone position and instrument output are the first decisions. Monitor at a safe level; don't increase playback or wedge volume to evaluate a subtle shaker detail.

# Teaching exercise and assessment
1.Compare soft and loud egg shakers with the same player and phrase. Identify which one fits a quiet overdub and which has a better chance amid a band; do not assume product labels alone establish the result.
2.Mark one hand's movement path. Start a cardioid spot around 30 cm from its center, then compare around 60 cm. Keep clear of the actual closest approach. Match monitoring levels and note pulse definition, noise, level variation and spill.
3.Repeat with one egg in each hand. Compare one central mic with two spots only if the hands and arrangement justify them; assess balance and mono compatibility.
4.Simulate a live stage: identify mic nulls, position a wedge accordingly, play the loudest full-band section, and demonstrate one fix that does not consist solely of more channel gain.
Pass criterion: The student can select a source appropriate to the acoustic context, keep the entire hand motion safely within usable pickup, explain a one- versus two-mic choice for two eggs, and distinguish spill or feedback from a simple gain deficiency.
# Evidence audit and limits
Agreement: Meinl explicitly supplies egg shakers at several output levels [1]. Shure's general percussion starting distance, listening-first placement advice, and polar-pattern guidance support the method of auditioning position and treating room or stage spill as an acoustic issue [2–4, 7]. DPA provides a documented live example of a direct mic for the broader shaker/tambourine category [6].
Differences of emphasis: Manufacturer product information emphasizes selecting an appropriate shaker output; microphone guidance emphasizes geometry, rejection and the room. These are complementary, not contradictory. No source located establishes a universal egg-shaker microphone distance, a guaranteed tonal change from one side of the egg, or a specific two-hand stereo layout. The 30–60 cm trial, centered coverage, and two-hand choices are reasoned teaching proposals to verify with the actual player and environment. Confidence is high for the source/output and polar-pattern principles, moderate for the proposed starting workflow, and low for predicting an exact tonal outcome before audition.
Safety and scope: Inspect a damaged shell and retire it if fill can spill; keep small loose parts away from children. Keep stands and cables outside the player's path, and control stage monitor exposure. This lesson covers acoustic handleless eggs; maracas follow separately. No audio examples are included.
