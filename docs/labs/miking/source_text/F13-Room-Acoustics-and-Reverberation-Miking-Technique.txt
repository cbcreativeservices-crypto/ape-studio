# Room Acoustics and Reverberation Miking Technique
Pro Audio Training Academy  •  Foley, Field and Acoustical Lab F13  •  Studio, venue and measurement applications
Measure how sound develops and dies away at listener positions. This lesson emphasizes microphone placement, repeatable impulse-response capture, frequency-dependent decay, and the limits imposed by background noise. A room measurement and a loudspeaker-system measurement answer different questions. No audio examples are included.
# Define the question and the operating state
A room impulse response (IR) describes the direct arrival and later reflections for one source–receiver pair. A change in either position changes the result. First state the purpose: estimate room reverberation for acoustic use, characterize the installed reinforcement system at a listener location, compare treatment changes, or document a particular recording position. Record whether doors, curtains, movable panels, furniture, audience, HVAC and other machinery are in their intended operating states. Empty and occupied conditions may differ materially. [1–4]
For a room-oriented test, use a suitable broadly radiating, well-characterized test source where the applicable method calls for one. A sweep through the installed PA characterizes its response plus the room along that path: speaker directivity, processing and coverage shape the result. This is useful for a listener-position system check but cannot by itself isolate an intrinsic room response. Label the source and signal chain accurately. Follow ISO 3382-2 for ordinary rooms or ISO 3382-1 for performance spaces when a formal result is required; the standard, purpose and room type determine positions, apparatus and reporting. [1–3]

An omnidirectional measurement microphone is a common receiver for room-acoustic work, while a directional mic can intentionally characterize a chosen perspective but cannot silently substitute for a standard receiver. Disable automatic gain control, noise reduction, gating, reverb and changing dynamics; note any unavoidable processing. Confirm sample rate, channel mapping, polarity and clocking. [1–3, 5]
# Place the source and receiver deliberately
Select source positions representing the real use: performer positions for a room study, or the actual installed speaker and processing for a system study. Select receiver positions across relevant listeners or working areas, including locations likely to differ in distance, boundaries and coverage. Measure multiple source–receiver combinations as required by the chosen standard or project brief. Do not replace spatial sampling with a single “center of room” reading. Avoid a position dominated only by direct sound or an unrepresentative wall/corner unless that position itself is the target. The governing method, room geometry and purpose set precise spacing and counts; there is no universal microphone height or source distance for every room. [1–3]
Mount the microphone firmly, orient it according to its calibration and method, and keep people and stands out of the direct path where practicable. Mark positions and measure distances so a second pass after treatment can reproduce them. Monitor from a safe location; a person near the mic or a moved chair changes reflection conditions. For open-air or unusual spaces, check whether the selected indoor-room method actually applies. [1–3]
# Capture enough decay without unsafe level
Before the test, measure or log the room noise by band with the intended HVAC and occupancy. Record the full excitation and a tail long enough to include the useful decay plus the background floor. Watch the input for clipping and the output for limiting. Repeat each configuration to spot transient noise, movement and failed playback. A sweep captured through a noisy room can yield an IR, but a plotted decay that merges with the floor does not support a reliable late slope. Close an optional noise source only if the intended operating condition permits, or reschedule a quieter measurement. [2, 4–6]
For energy-decay analysis, the T20 slope is fitted from −5 to −25 dB and extrapolated by three; T30 uses −5 to −35 dB and extrapolates by two. Early decay time (EDT) uses the first 10 dB and extrapolates by six. These are estimates of the time for a 60 dB decay, not direct observations of 20 or 30 seconds. Keep the end of the fitted region sufficiently above the noise floor: Rational Acoustics describes a 10 dB margin and roughly 35 dB usable range for T20 or 45 dB for T30 under ideal conditions. If T30 lacks margin, consider a valid T20; if neither is supported, report insufficient range. Do not drive the source louder solely to force a result. [4–6]
Inspect the IR and energy-decay curve in frequency bands, not only a single broadband “RT60” figure. Reverse integration of squared IR energy is a common decay-curve method. Check early reflection spikes, a changing slope, low-frequency modes, tail truncation and sudden disturbances; small rooms and non-diffuse spaces may not have one clean exponential decay. Report T20/T30/EDT separately and identify invalid bands, spatial spread and the method used. Averaging positions is useful only when the individual observations and outliers remain visible. [1, 4–6]

# Guided teaching exercise
1. Write the question and room state: room-only comparison or installed-system listener response. List the planned source, its path and two or more representative receiver positions. Identify the standard if claiming a formal result.
2. Log steady background noise and disturbances. Set the mic at position A with documented height, orientation and distance. Check calibration or verification status, software reference, processing bypass and input headroom.
3. Capture a controlled sweep at a safe, appropriate level and enough post-sweep time for the decay. Repeat once. Reject clipped, disturbed, truncated or mismatched takes before fitting a slope.
4. Move only the receiver to position B, document the difference and repeat. If comparing a treatment change, restore the same positions, source, settings and operating state for the second pass.
5. Inspect band-by-band decay and noise floor. Mark whether T30, T20 or neither has enough usable range; compare EDT only as a distinct early-decay measure. Record early reflection or multi-slope behavior.
6. For a live-system variant, write a separate result label naming the PA source and processing. Explain why its IR cannot be attributed solely to the room. Keep the measurement mic out of the loudspeaker return.
Pass criterion: the student identifies the measurement target, reproduces geometry, captures an unclipped adequate tail, rejects unsupported fits, distinguishes T20/T30/EDT and reports spatial and operating-state limits. No audio examples.

# Critical evidence audit
## What the sources agree on
ISO 3382 frames reverberation measurement around defined procedures and multiple positions. Rational Acoustics and NTi agree that T20 and T30 are fitted, extrapolated decays whose usefulness depends on sufficient dynamic range and an uncontaminated floor. Their guides support inspecting frequency bands and documenting measurement conditions. [1–6]
## Different purposes and methods
An impulse response from an installed PA is valid for system-plus-room behavior at that position; a room-focused result calls for a suitable test source and method. Interrupted noise can yield decay estimates, while controlled sweep deconvolution yields a time-resolved IR. EDT and late-decay T values quantify different portions of the response and need not agree. These distinctions should appear in the result label. [1–6]
## Limits
The cited public guides do not supply a universal source level, mic distance, position count or acceptable reverberation time for every room. Small-room modal decay, variable occupancy, uncalibrated equipment and low signal-to-noise ratio can defeat a single summary number. The published ISO 3382-1:2009 performance-space edition is the cited current standard; a 2026 revision draft is under development, so the draft must not be represented as a published replacement. [1, 7]
