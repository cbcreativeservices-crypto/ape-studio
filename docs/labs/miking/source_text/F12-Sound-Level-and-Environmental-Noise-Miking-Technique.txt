# Sound Level and Environmental Noise Miking Technique
Pro Audio Training Academy  •  Scientific and Acoustical Lab F12  •  Representative positions and time
This lesson teaches where and when to place a measurement microphone for a defined sound-level question. Learners distinguish environmental noise from worker exposure, specify the reported descriptor and interval, manage weather and background sound, and preserve calibration and site records. The purpose is to design and critically inspect a measurement plan, not to declare compliance from one spot reading. No audio examples are included.
# Name the question, receiver and time
Define the source or whole environment, the receiver or area of concern, the relevant operating condition and the decision the result must support. ISO 1996-2 describes determination of sound pressure levels for environmental assessment and spatial scenario comparisons; ISO 1996-1 defines basic community-noise quantities and assessment procedures. Those standards do not tell a classroom exercise which local limit applies. Use the current local permit, project specification or relevant method before making a formal claim. [1, 2]
A neighborhood baseline, highway traffic study, venue boundary check and worker hearing exposure differ. An area microphone represents sound at its position and time; it does not by itself represent every nearby listener or a mobile worker’s daily dose. OSHA distinguishes area sampling from personal dosimetry and favors representative personal sampling where mobility, variation or impulse noise make area monitoring inappropriate. [3]
# Choose a descriptor that answers the question
Descriptor
What it describes
What must accompany it
LAeq,T
A-weighted equivalent continuous level: the sound energy of a varying signal averaged over the stated interval T. [4]
Give start/stop, duration, weighting, site and source conditions. It is not the arithmetic average of dB readouts.
LAFmax or LASmax
Largest A-weighted fast- or slow-time-weighted level in an interval, respectively. [4]
State F or S, event and period. Neither is automatically the unweighted instantaneous peak.
L10, L50, L90
Levels exceeded for 10%, 50% or 90% of the chosen period. L90 may characterize a quieter underlying level in some settings. [4]
State weighting and window; a percentile is not a source-free background measurement when other sound never stops.
LCpeak or another specified peak
Peak sound-pressure descriptor under the chosen instrument and method, useful where impulses matter.
State the specified frequency weighting and instrument capability; never relabel a fast maximum as a true peak.

Select the instrument’s frequency weighting and any time weighting before capture. Record actual labels from the meter rather than casually writing “dB”: dBA, dBC, LAeq,T, LAFmax and LCpeak are different. An ordinary recorder reading in dBFS is not calibrated SPL. If combining interval levels later, use the method’s energy and time weighting, not a simple arithmetic mean. FHWA defines LAeq,T as an energy-equivalent time average and distinguishes fast/slow maximum from statistical exceedance levels. [4]
# Map representative microphone positions
Use a site sketch or coordinates. Mark source paths, receiver areas, elevations, ground, buildings, walls, vegetation, reflective facades, obstructions, PA loudspeakers, prevailing wind and safe access. Place the microphone at the position, height, orientation and boundary relationship specified by the method. A small shift toward a road, a reflecting facade or the direct axis of a loudspeaker can materially change the result. FHWA’s highway field guide uses a 1.5 m height for a defined procedure unless its plan states otherwise; that is a method-specific example, not the universal height for all environmental or live measurements. [5]
If the task is a broad site survey, sample more than one representative location and explain why those positions stand for the area. If it is a particular receiver, document the exact intended point. For a before/after comparison, return to the same coordinates, height, orientation and source state; also log changed weather or other sources. FHWA distinguishes exterior facade positions from more open positions because reflections are part of what the microphone receives. A reading at a wall cannot be silently called a free-field reading. [5, 6]
Mount a response-appropriate measurement microphone or complete sound level meter in its specified orientation; document grid and windscreen. Check the model’s field correction and outdoor specifications as taught in F11. Keep the operator and tripod shadow/reflection away as required by the method, and secure the cable and stand without creating a public hazard. If using more than one meter, synchronize time and record each channel’s calibration and position. [5, 6]
# Plan duration and source conditions
Choose a window that includes the variation relevant to the question: cycles of traffic, HVAC, production shifts, event program changes or quiet periods. Log events, counts or equipment state while the instrument runs. A few minutes may be useful for scouting, but cannot be assumed to describe an hour, day or season. FHWA plans highway measurements around the applicable study hour and keeps traffic counts and meteorological notes; NPS uses much longer sampling to characterize park soundscapes across conditions. These are different purposes and methods. [6, 7]
Specify which events are part of the question before collection. A truck pass-by may be the target for a traffic assessment and an interference for a measurement of a fixed machine. Keep original time histories and a written reason for any excluded interval; do not delete inconvenient loud events after seeing the result. For controlled equipment demonstrations, hold the source operating mode steady and repeat a baseline. For an actual community environment, document natural changes rather than pretending it is stationary.
# Weather, wind and background sound
Fit the specified windscreen and monitor for gust-driven low-frequency turbulence. Note wind speed/direction, precipitation, temperature and humidity as the method requires. A windscreen reduces buffeting but does not make an invalid high-wind interval valid or waterproof the microphone. FHWA says wind can create false readings even through a proper windscreen; NPS excludes intervals over 5 m/s in its own park monitoring protocol because of artifacts. That threshold belongs to that protocol, not to every study. [6, 7]
For a source-specific level, measure background under comparable conditions with the target absent when possible. The combined level includes source and background energy; ordinary dB subtraction is invalid. FHWA provides a method-specific energy subtraction when source and background are sufficiently separated, and rejects source-only estimation when they are too close for its procedure. If the target cannot be stopped, report the limitation and use the approved method; L90 is only a statistical descriptor and does not magically remove continuous traffic or a running PA. [4, 6]
Do not apply an arbitrary high-pass filter, noise reduction or a “wind correction” to obtain a cleaner-looking number. If the adopted method permits a correction, use its specified hardware and analysis and retain raw records. Record unusual reflections, rain, foliage motion, aircraft, speech and construction because they can change the meaning of a level even when the instrument functions correctly. [6, 7]
# Calibrate, log and report
Use an appropriate complete meter or chain for the method and verify its current calibration status. Perform and record the compatible acoustic field check before and after, preserving the unadjusted post-check indication. Follow the model and method if drift exceeds its tolerance; do not overwrite a failed check and present the run as valid. FHWA’s field guide logs meter and microphone identifiers, height, weather, times, level descriptors, a site sketch, file IDs, and pre/post checks. [5]
Report the exact descriptor, units, weighting, averaging interval, coordinates or site description, height, orientation, distance and boundary condition, source activity, weather, instrument/calibrator identity and check results. Give temporal coverage and exclusions. A map or table should show the actual sampled positions, not imply continuous coverage between them. State uncertainty and limits appropriate to the study; a single measurement does not automatically establish a health, nuisance or regulatory conclusion. [1, 5]
For live sound, distinguish a real-time spot check at audience positions from an occupational exposure assessment of crew or performers. Moving listeners, duration and changing program level matter. Keep microphones clear of audience traffic, loudspeaker rigging and sightlines; never feed a measurement microphone to the PA merely to see its reading. A venue’s sound limit may name a location, descriptor and averaging period; obtain its actual terms instead of substituting a different metric.
# Safety and decision limits
Choose accessible, authorized positions away from roads, tracks, water edges, moving equipment and electrical hazards. Use remote placement or qualified site coordination if a receiver is unsafe to reach. Do not increase sound level to make a meter “respond”; use ordinary operation and appropriate hearing protection for staff. Stop outdoor work for lightning or other unsafe conditions. NWS instructs people to seek safe shelter when thunder is heard and remain there until 30 minutes after the last thunder. [8]
This lesson teaches measurement geometry and data quality. It does not specify a universal legal noise limit or substitute a sound level meter for dosimetry. If formal exposure, environmental compliance or health decisions are at stake, have a qualified practitioner apply the applicable jurisdictional method and calibrated instrument. [1–3]
# Guided teaching exercise
1. Choose an accessible low-level location with a repeatable ordinary sound source, such as a permitted HVAC unit or a road viewed from a safe property position. Define two questions: the sound at one receiver and variation across two safe positions. Do not enter traffic or change the source level for the exercise.
2. Write the descriptor and interval before measuring. Sketch two positions, height, orientation, ground, reflectors and likely background sources. Explain which result will and will not represent a larger area.
3. Confirm microphone field response, windscreen, instrument setting and compatible pre-run acoustic check. If calibrated equipment is unavailable, use an uncalibrated relative comparison and label it explicitly; do not fabricate SPL.
4. Record two same-length windows at the first position, noting events and weather; repeat at the second. Return to the first to check reproducibility. Keep weighting and interval identical and do not delete anomalous minutes without a documented rule.
5. If target and background can be separated safely, collect a representative background window; otherwise record that they cannot be isolated. Do not subtract decibel readings numerically.
6. Perform the post-run check, preserve its observed value, and complete the field sheet. Compare LAeq,T, LAFmax and an available percentile only as defined by the meter. Write one supported conclusion and two limits.
Pass criterion: the learner matches position and duration to a named question, states the exact level descriptor, documents wind and background, retains pre/post checks and source conditions, and does not infer exposure or compliance from insufficient sampling. No audio examples.

# Critical evidence audit
## Points of agreement
ISO, FHWA and NPS agree that a sound-level result needs a defined question, representative location and interval, and documented conditions. FHWA and OSHA emphasize calibration checks; NPS and FHWA identify weather and wind as threats to data quality. Confidence is high in those planning and documentation principles. [1–7]
## Different methods and apparent contradictions
FHWA’s defined highway positions and study hours differ from NPS’s broad long-term park monitoring; each samples a different target. OSHA may require personal sampling rather than fixed-area readings to assess a mobile worker. NPS excludes strong-wind intervals under its protocol, while a separate creative field recording might retain actual wind sound. These are task-specific rules, not a shared universal duration or height. [3, 5–7]
## Important gaps
The overview sources do not establish a universal height, wind threshold, background adjustment, exposure limit or compliance period. ISO’s public abstract is not the full method. Local rules and project specifications may impose requirements that this lesson does not reproduce. A single position, short interval or generic dB reading cannot resolve those gaps. [1–7]
